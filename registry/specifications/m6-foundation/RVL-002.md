# RVL-002 — behaviour specification

Wave: `waves/m6-foundation.json`. Method: source-informed reimplementation.
Rights: the upstream project's repository root carries an MIT licence file, read
at the source; see `provenance/m6-foundation/RVL-002.json` for the pinned
revision, the licence identity and the digest.

Confidence markers: **read**, **inferred**, **unknown**.

This entry is a leaf of the wave's content cluster. It depends on RVL-042 and on
RVL-321, adds no facility of its own that anything else in the pack depends on,
and is owned by `content-data`. Almost everything it does is either declared data
or a small numeric rule applied to declared data, so the load-bearing part of this
document is the arithmetic of §9 to §12 and the registration and absence rules of
§5 to §7. Where it consumes something RVL-321 or RVL-042 already specifies, this
document cites the section and does not restate it.

---

## 1. What this entry is

An extension of RVL-321. It adds exactly one tier above that entry's numbered
progression — a **sixth tier** — plus the content that tier needs to be reachable
and useful: a ground block for it, a material for it, six crops that sit at it,
two ore deposits in each of two non-overworld dimensions, and four families of
consumables and tools that span all six tiers rather than only the new one
(**read**).

It is small. Thirty-four source files, of which two are entirely commented out and
two more are third-party integration shims, leave about **1,300 lines of live
code**; against that it ships **413 declarative documents** and 62 images
(**read**). The ratio is the point: this entry is mostly a data pack with a thin
behavioural rim, and a counterpart that gets §9 to §12's numbers right and carries
the data across has reproduced nearly all of it.

It declares itself present and mandatory on **both sides**, and declares hard
dependencies on RVL-042 and on RVL-321, the second with an explicit *load after*
ordering (**read**). It declares no soft dependency on any of the four companion
mods whose content §6 optionally consumes; it discovers those at runtime instead.

### 1.1 Ravel disposition

Content declaration, registration, tag identity, configuration, loot and recipe
data, and side isolation are public Ravel facilities. A counterpart declares this
entry's catalogue through them and does not rebuild any of it.

What survives as counterpart work is small and almost entirely numeric: the fuel
derivation (§9), the food effect rule (§10), the tool grade table (§11), the boss
drop rule (§12), the ground behaviour it shares with RVL-321 §9, and the absence
rules of §6. Everything else in this document is either declared data or an
observation about what the host does with it.

Three areas are **host-blocked** at the reviewed public revision rather than
declined, and are named where they occur: the fluid family of §7.4, the world
generation of §15, and the extension callback registration of §4.

## 2. Side, lifecycle and what happens when

The entry runs on both sides. In order (**read**):

1. **At construction**, six registries are claimed — blocks, items, the creative
   listing, biome-modifier codecs, fluid types and the fluids of §7.4 — and one
   configuration document is declared, so all six are populated before the adjust
   pass of §5. §7 lists what each registry holds. How the original arranges that
   claim is its own structure; the observable consequence is the ordering just
   stated, and a counterpart does not port the shape.
2. **The configuration document is startup-scoped** and common (**read**). This
   matters twice over: it is read before registration completes, which is what
   lets §5 bake one of its values into a registered object; and it is not
   server-scoped, so **nothing in §3 is synchronised to a joining client**. A
   client and a server that disagree about §3 disagree silently.
3. **On a client only**, a colour handler is subscribed (§7.5). The test is the
   process's own distribution, taken once at construction, not a runtime side
   check.
4. **The extension callbacks of §4** run inside RVL-321's registration windows,
   in that entry's documented order (RVL-321 §4).
5. **At common setup**, one game-event subscriber is registered for §12.

There is no client-scoped configuration, no network payload of its own, no
command, no saved data of its own, and no block entity (**read**). §16 says what
that leaves for the reload half of the cluster scenario.

The two third-party integration shims — a recipe-viewer plugin that attaches
descriptive text to two items, and a block-inspection plugin that adds the ground
block's tier to an inspection tooltip — are client-side and add no behaviour
(**read**). A third integration, for a tool-forging mod, is present as **entirely
commented-out source**; its registration site is guarded by a mod-presence test
whose body is empty. That is dead code at this revision, and a counterpart carries
none of it. The ninety-five declarative documents that would feed it, however, **are
still shipped**: §14.4 counts them and says what the host does with them.

## 3. Configuration and the flags it publishes

One common, startup-scoped document with two sections and eight values, all
primitives with bounds (**read**):

| setting | default | bounds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| boss-soul drop chance | 0.35 | 0 to 1 |
| boss-material drop count | 8 | 0 to 64 |
| sixth-tier crops fertilizable | false | — |
| food effect duration, seconds | 180 | 1 to 2147483647 |
| four deposit switches (§15) | true each | — |

Five of these are published as **feature flags** through RVL-042 §4 under this
entry's own namespace: the fertilizable switch and the four deposit switches
(**read**). Two further flags are **consumed** from RVL-321's namespace rather
than declared: the tool-enchantability flag (§11) and the ground-conversion flag
(§8). A counterpart must keep the direction straight — the first five are this
entry's to publish and gate its own data on, the last two are read-only
observations of another entry's setting.

The fertilizable switch is read **once**, during the adjust pass of §5, and its
value is copied into the registered tier. A counterpart that reads it live gets
different behaviour after a settings change without a restart (**inferred** — the
read is a one-time copy at registration and nothing re-reads it).

The food duration is read live on every use and on every tooltip build, with the
tooltip caveat in §10.

## 4. The extension: what it declares and what it opts out of

The entry declares itself an extension of RVL-321, discovered by that entry's own
scan (RVL-321 §4). Three things follow from that declaration (**read**):

- **declares its namespace**, which is what binds its crops to its own identifier
  space;
- **opts out of all three dynamic recipe derivations** — seed crafting, seed
  infusion and seed reprocessing — that RVL-321 §11 would otherwise contribute for
  every crop in this namespace;
- **implements both registration callbacks**, the register pass and the adjust
  pass, forwarding each to §5 and §6.

The opt-out is the single most consequential line in the entry. RVL-321 §11
derives three recipes per crop from the crop's own declared parts; this entry
refuses all three and **ships eighteen equivalent documents instead** (§14.3), one
per crop per kind. The observable difference is not the recipe set — the shipped
documents reproduce the derived shapes — but *where the recipes come from*, which
changes what a data pack can override and changes which of the two entries a
`recipe-parity` failure should be attributed to.

**Host-blocked**: Ravel publishes no equivalent of another package's registration
callback. A counterpart records the callback registration as blocked and
implements §5 and §6 as ordinary declaration against Ravel's own registration
window, accepting that the ordering guarantee then comes from Ravel rather than
from RVL-321 §2.

## 5. The sixth tier

One tier object, declared with four values and adjusted with four more
(**read**). Against RVL-321 §5.1's tier model:

- **identifier**: this entry's namespace, path `6`;
- **numeric value**: 6, one above the highest RVL-321 declares;
- **display colour**: `0x40005E`, a dark violet, used by §7.5 for the ground
  block and its item;
- **text style**: the host's dark-purple formatting.

In the adjust pass it points the tier at its ground block (§8), at its material
item, copies the fertilizable setting of §3, and sets **secondary seed drop to
false**.

That last value is the tier's whole interaction with RVL-321 §8.1. RVL-321's
harvest rule offers a second drop whose availability is a per-tier flag; this
tier turns it off, so **a sixth-tier crop never yields a second seed**, whatever
the general rule computes. A counterpart that carries RVL-321 §8.1 faithfully and
then forgets this one boolean makes the entry's crops self-replicating, which is
the difference between a progression gate and none.

Registration order is fixed by RVL-321 §4: the tier is registered in the register
pass and can only be pointed at its ground and its material in the adjust pass,
because those two registries are not populated when the register pass runs
(**inferred** — the split exists for exactly this reason and the entry uses it
exactly this way).

## 6. The six crops and the three states of absence

Six crops, **all at the sixth tier**, all of the resource kind of RVL-321 §5.2,
each declaring one crafting material and each pointed at its own anchor block in
the adjust pass (**read**). Their materials:

| crop | material named as | companion mod required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| beacon-material crop | a single host item | none |
| dragon-egg crop | a single item of this entry's own (§7.2) | none |
| companion crop A | a single item of another mod's | one |
| companion crop B | a conventional cross-mod tag | one |
| companion crop C | **a tag of this entry's own** | one |
| companion crop D | a single item of another mod's | one |

Every one of the six is **registered unconditionally**. The four companion crops
are then marked disabled unless their one declared companion mod is loaded; the
test is `any of the declared mods is loaded`, applied to a one-element list in
every case (**read**). In a development environment the test is skipped and all
four are enabled — the same escape hatch RVL-321 §15 describes, and it means a
development run and a production run of the same build have different content.

RVL-321 §15's three states apply here unchanged and this entry exercises all
three:

1. **Enabled and satisfiable** — the two unconditional crops, always.
2. **Disabled** — a companion crop whose mod is absent. Its block, material and
   seed identifiers still exist and a world containing them still loads; it
   contributes no recipe, and it is hidden from the creative listing (§7.3).
3. **Enabled but with an empty material** — reachable here through companion crop
   C. Its material is a tag this entry ships itself, and that tag's two members
   are **both declared not-required** (§17), so if the companion mod is loaded but
   provides neither name, the tag resolves empty. The crop is then enabled, its
   identifiers exist, its reprocessing recipe exists, and only its seed recipes
   are missing.

That third state is not hypothetical for this entry: the tag exists precisely
because the companion mod renamed the item in question at some point, and both
spellings are carried. A counterpart that collapses "disabled" and "unsatisfiable"
into one flag reports the wrong content set for `registry-parity` and
`recipe-parity` at once.

Each crop is also pointed at an **anchor block** in the adjust pass — a decorative
block that RVL-321's growth rule consults (RVL-321 §7). The pointing is
unconditional and happens for all six crops including disabled ones (**read**), so
a disabled crop still has a valid anchor.

## 7. Registered content

Six registries, in the order §2 lists them.

### 7.1 Blocks

Twenty-six blocks (**read**): three storage blocks, one ground block (§8), six
fuel storage blocks (§9), four ore blocks, six anchor blocks (§6), and six fluid
blocks (§7.4). The first twenty carry a block item; the six fluid blocks do not.

Hardness and blast resistance, as declared: storage blocks 4.0/6.0 for the
material block and 5.0/6.0 for the two metallic ones; fuel blocks 5.0/6.0; ore
blocks 3.0/3.0; anchor blocks 5.0/10.0 (**read**). The ore blocks additionally
declare an experience range of **2 to 5** on break, which the host applies only
when the drop is not the block itself.

### 7.2 Items

Fifty-three items (**read**). Twenty are the block items of §7.1, and they are
registered **first**, before any other item, and in the block registry's own
insertion order. The mechanism the original reaches that ordering by is its
structure and carries no consequence of its own; what is observable is that
**block items occupy the earliest positions in this namespace's item registry**,
which a counterpart reproduces only if `registry-parity` compares order and not
just membership.

The remaining thirty-three: the tier material (§8's converter), a metal ingot, its
nugget, a gemstone, a shard, the boss-soul item (§12), a dragon-egg fragment, a
dragon scale (§12), a decorative item declared at the host's epic rarity and
permanently enchanted-looking, six fuels (§9), six foods (§10), six tools (§11)
and six buckets (§7.4).

Two items carry a hover line computed at display time rather than declared: the
boss-soul item shows the drop chance of §3 as a percentage, and the ground block's
item shows the tier's display name. The percentage is produced by multiplying the
stored fraction by 100 and concatenating a percent sign, so a default of 0.35
renders as `35.000000000000004%` (**read**). That is a genuine display defect at
this revision and not a rounding choice; a counterpart formats the value instead
and should expect a pixel-level presentation test to disagree.

### 7.3 The creative listing

One listing, titled from a translation key, iconed with the tier material, and
populated in a **fixed hand-written order** through RVL-042 §4's flag-aware
generator, so a flag-disabled item drops out of the listing without disturbing the
rest (**read**).

The six anchor blocks are additionally each guarded by their crop's enabled test
of §6, evaluated when the listing is built. Everything else is unconditional. The
order is: blocks, then the six anchor blocks in crop order, then the plain items,
then the six tools, then the six buckets.

### 7.4 Fluids and buckets

Six fluid families, each comprising a **fluid type**, a **still fluid**, a
**flowing fluid**, a **fluid block** and a **bucket** — thirty registrations in
total across four registries, the fluid-type, fluid, block and item ones, with
the still and flowing members sharing the fluid registry (**read**).

Every family shares one property set: density 2000, viscosity 10000, temperature
1000, and the host's lava bucket-fill and bucket-empty sounds. Each fluid block
copies the host lava block's behaviour wholesale. The families differ **only** in
their tint colour, which is an opaque ARGB constant per family and is used in two
places: the client fluid rendering, and the bucket item's second tint layer
(§7.5). The still and flowing textures are shared by all six.

The buckets declare a crafting remainder of the empty bucket and a stack limit of
one — RVL-042 §10's remainder behaviour applies, not a new rule.

The fluids alone are registered through a general registration event rather than a
deferred registry, because the fluid holders must exist before the property sets
that reference them are built (**read**). That is structure, not behaviour, and a
counterpart is free to order it however Ravel's registration window allows.

**Host-blocked**: Ravel publishes no fluid, fluid-type or fluid-block declaration
at the reviewed public revision. A counterpart records all thirty registrations as
blocked. Nothing else in this entry depends on them: no recipe consumes them, no
crop needs them, and they do not appear in §16's reload path. They are the one
part of this entry that can be omitted entirely without changing any other
section's behaviour.

### 7.5 Colour

Client-only (**read**). Two registrations: the ground block and its item are
tinted with the tier colour of §5 at every tint index; the six buckets are tinted
at **index 1 only** with their fluid's tint, and at every other index with the
host's no-tint sentinel.

## 8. The sixth-tier ground

One block, the sixth member of the family RVL-321 §9 specifies. **Its moisture
rule (§9.1), its fall rule (§9.2) and its conversion target behaviour (§9.4) are
identical to that section and are not restated here.** Three things are specific
to this entry.

**It ships no loot table.** Nineteen of the twenty block items in §7.1 have a loot
document; this block does not, and its drops are computed in code exactly as
RVL-321 §9.3 describes: silk touch yields the block itself, otherwise one dirt
plus, with probability 25% drawn as an integer in `[0,100)` from the level's own
source, one of the tier's material. `loot-parity` must therefore expect nineteen
documents and one code path, not twenty documents.

**Its fall damage is unconditional.** An entity landing on it takes full fall
damage at a multiplier of 1.0 and the block survives, where the host's own tilled
ground would revert to dirt. This is RVL-321 §9.2's rule and it applies at this
tier too — worth stating because it is the behaviour a player notices first and
because a counterpart that inherits the host block without overriding gets the
opposite.

**Conversion into it is gated on RVL-321's flag, not on this entry's.** The tier
material item implements RVL-321 §9.4's converter interface and short-circuits to
a pass-through when RVL-321's ground-conversion flag is off, before consulting
anything of its own (**read**). A counterpart that gates conversion on a local
setting diverges whenever the two disagree.

One shipped recipe of RVL-321's tilling kind produces this ground directly from a
hoe, one tier material and one dirt (§14.1), which is what makes it reachable
without owning a converter item.

## 9. Fuel

Twelve fuel values, six items and six blocks, one pair per tier (**read**). They
are not a table; they are two derivations from one base.

Let `n` be the tier, 1 through 6. Then:

    item burn ticks(n)  = 2400 × 2^(n−1)
    block burn ticks(n) = 9 × item burn ticks(n)

giving 2400, 4800, 9600, 19200, 38400 and 76800 for the items, and 21600, 43200,
86400, 172800, 345600 and 691200 for the blocks. Every one of the twelve declared
constants matches; the derivation is exact, not approximate (**read**, checked
against all twelve).

The factor of nine is the crafting ratio, not a bonus: a fuel block is crafted
from nine fuel items (§14.2) and burns for exactly as long, so **compressing fuel
is storage, not efficiency**. A counterpart that rounds or that uses a bonus
factor changes the economy of every furnace in a pack.

The burn time is returned from a fixed field for any consumer and any recipe kind;
the item does not vary its answer by what is asking (**read**).

## 10. The six foods

Six items, one per tier, sharing one behaviour and differing in three values
(**read**):

| tier | nutrition | saturation modifier | effects, cumulative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 6 | 0.3 | absorption |
| 2 | 8 | 0.4 | + movement speed |
| 3 | 10 | 0.5 | + damage resistance |
| 4 | 12 | 0.6 | + regeneration |
| 5 | 16 | 0.7 | + damage boost |
| 6 | 20 | 0.8 | + dig speed |

The effect list is **strictly cumulative in one fixed order**: tier *n* grants
exactly the first *n* effects of that order. Nutrition rises by 2 per tier through
tier 4, then by 4; saturation rises by 0.1 throughout. All six are declared
**always edible**, so they may be eaten at full hunger, and all six render
permanently enchanted (**read**).

On finishing the item, **on the server side only**, each effect in the tier's list
is applied by this rule (**read**):

    new duration = (duration remaining on the existing instance of that effect, or 0)
                 + (configured duration in seconds × 20)
    amplifier    = 1

Two things are load-bearing. The amplifier is a **fixed 1** — level II — at every
tier and for every effect; the tier changes which effects you get, never how
strong. And durations **add to whatever is already running** rather than replacing
it, and they add unconditionally, with no comparison against the existing
instance's amplifier or duration. Eating six foods in sequence therefore stacks to
six times the configured duration, and a lower-tier food refreshes a higher-tier
food's shared effects at the same strength.

The hover text lists the tier's effects with the level and a minutes-and-seconds
rendering of the configured duration. It is **built once per tier and cached for
the process lifetime**, from the setting's value at first display (**read**). A
settings change mid-session leaves the displayed duration stale while the applied
duration follows the new value. That is a real divergence between what the item
says and what it does; a counterpart that recomputes the line per display is
*more* correct and will disagree with the original under a mid-session change,
which is the right trade and should be recorded rather than matched.

The minutes-and-seconds rendering divides the configured seconds by 60 for
minutes and takes the remainder for seconds, zero-padded to two digits, with no
upper bound on the minutes field (**read**).

## 11. The six tools

Six multi-tools, one per tool grade, all of one kind and all accepting RVL-321
§12.8's slotted modifiers (**read**). The grade table:

| grade | durability | mining speed | attack bonus | enchantability | modifier slots | modifier tier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 2000 | 9.0 | 4.0 | 12 | 1 | 1 |
| 2 | 2800 | 11.0 | 6.0 | 14 | 1 | 2 |
| 3 | 4000 | 14.0 | 9.0 | 16 | 1 | 3 |
| 4 | 6000 | 19.0 | 13.0 | 18 | 1 | 4 |
| 5 | **0** | 25.0 | 20.0 | 20 | 1 | 5 |
| 6 | **0** | 30.0 | 25.0 | 22 | **2** | **5** |

A declared durability of zero means unbreakable, and the entry acts on that
explicitly: **a grade whose durability is zero is given the host's unbreakable
marker at construction** (**read**). So grades 5 and 6 never take damage and never
need repair, and grades 1 to 4 do. A counterpart that treats zero as "breaks
immediately" inverts the entry's top two grades.

Grade 6 is the ritual-upgraded form of grade 5. It shares grade 5's modifier tier
and is distinguished by a second modifier slot and by higher numbers; it is
produced only by the awakening recipe of §14.1 and not by any grid recipe.

Every grade declares its **incorrect-for-drops block tag** and its **repair
material** by reaching into RVL-321's namespace: the tag by name, and the material
by looking one item up in the host item registry (**read**). The **timing** of
that lookup is load-bearing: it happens at first repair, after registration is
complete, and not at construction, because a counterpart that resolves at
construction gets an empty result — the target entry's items are not registered
yet. How the original defers it is that entry's structure; any lazy resolution
satisfies the rule.

All six accept four modifier categories: the generic one plus the three that
correspond to the tool shapes this item combines. All six are enchantable when
**RVL-321's tool-enchantability flag** is on, and otherwise fall back to whatever
the base tool kind allows (§3).

Modifier dispatch is uniform across five interaction points — use on a block, use
in the air, use on an entity, hitting an entity, and breaking a block — plus an
inventory tick. In each case every attached modifier is offered the event **in
attachment order, all of them, with no short-circuit**, and the interaction
reports success if any one of them did (**read**). The inventory tick offers the
event to every modifier and returns nothing. Two consequences: a modifier that
consumes an event does not prevent later modifiers from seeing it, and the
attachment order is therefore observable only through side effects, not through
which modifier "wins".

Breaking a block runs the base tool's own break handling **first**, then the
modifiers, and returns only the modifiers' verdict — so the base handling's result
is discarded (**read**). Whether that discard is reachable as a behavioural
difference is **unknown**: it depends on what the base kind returns, which is
RVL-042's §15 thin-base-type territory and was not read here.

## 12. Boss drops

One subscriber on the host's entity-drops event, registered at common setup and
therefore server-side (**read**). On every such event:

1. If the world's mob-loot game rule is off, **return immediately** — before any
   draw, so the rule gates both drops and nothing is consumed.
2. If the dying entity is the wither-class boss and a draw is below the configured
   chance, add one boss-soul item at the entity's position.
3. If the dying entity is the dragon-class boss and the configured scale count is
   above zero, add that many dragon scales at the entity's position, with **no
   draw** — the count is a certainty, and zero means the drop is off.

Both items are added to the event's drop list as world entities at the boss's
exact position, so they are subject to whatever the host does with that list
afterwards.

The draw in step 2 is taken from a **global, process-wide random source, not from
the level's** (**read**). It is therefore not reproducible from the world seed and
not synchronised with anything else in the world's generation or tick. This is the
same class of defect RVL-321 §9.4 records for its conversion refund. A counterpart
should draw from the level's source and record the change: it makes the drop
seed-reproducible, which is strictly better for testing, and it will disagree with
the original on any replay comparison.

The dragon-class branch fires on every death of that boss, including respawned
ones, because nothing tracks first-kill state (**read**).

## 13. Sides

`side-matrix` observes a clean split (**read**):

- **Both sides**: every registration in §7, the tier and crops of §5 and §6, the
  ground block of §8, the fuel values of §9, all declared data.
- **Server only**: the drop handler of §12, and the effect application of §10,
  which is explicitly guarded on the world not being a client world.
- **Client only**: the colour handler of §7.5, both third-party integration shims
  of §2, and the hover text of §7.2 and §10.

One client type is referenced from a common class: the food's hover text consults
the host's screen state to decide whether to show the effect list or a prompt to
hold a key (**read**). The reference is inside a hover-text method, which the host
calls only on a client, so it is not loaded on a server — but it is a
common-classloaded class naming a client-only type, and a counterpart that mirrors
the structure literally on a stricter loader will fail where the original does
not. Ravel's side isolation makes this a non-issue; it is recorded because
`side-matrix` will otherwise flag the original and not the counterpart.

The entry declares no side-conditional dependency and no client-only or
server-only dependency (**read**).

## 14. Recipes shipped as data

The largest single part of this entry. **157 recipe documents** across three
namespaces (**read**), which is what `recipe-parity` must account for. They fall
into four groups and the fourth is the surprise.

### 14.1 Kinds used

| kind | owner | count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| host shaped | host | 36 |
| host shapeless | host | 22 |
| non-mirrored shaped | RVL-042 §6.1 | 8 |
| tilling | RVL-321 §10 | 1 |
| awakening ritual | RVL-321 §10.1 | 1 |
| seed infusion | RVL-321 §10 | 6 |
| seed reprocessing | RVL-321 §10 | 6 |
| tool-forging mod kinds (§14.4) | third party | 77 |

The eight non-mirrored shaped recipes are the entry's only use of an RVL-042
recipe kind. They all sit in one group — converting a stack of a crop's essence
back into the material that crop grows — and the non-mirroring is what makes the
patterns distinguishable. RVL-042 §6.1 specifies the kind; this entry supplies
data for it.

The single awakening recipe produces the grade-6 tool of §11 from the grade-5 tool
plus four elemental essences at 40 each and four ingredients, and declares
**component transfer**, so the input tool's slotted modifiers and damage carry
onto the output (**read**). That declaration is the mechanism by which an upgraded
tool keeps what a player put into it, and it is the only place in this entry where
a recipe preserves item-level data.

### 14.2 The compression family

Six fuel items, one material, one ingot, one nugget and one gemstone each get a
nine-to-one compression recipe and a matching one-to-nine decompression recipe, in
shaped and shapeless form respectively (**read**). The ingot additionally has a
nugget-to-ingot pair, and the material has a second, alternative compression
recipe. The material's decompression yields **four of a lower-tier material** from
one of this tier's, not nine — the one member of the family that is not a pure
storage conversion but a progression step, and the recipe by which the sixth tier
is entered at all.

`recipe-parity` should treat the nine-to-one identity of §9 and this group as the
same fact seen twice: the fuel arithmetic and the compression recipes agree, and a
counterpart that changes one must change both.

### 14.3 Crop content and the opt-out

Eighteen documents — six crops by three kinds — reproduce what RVL-321 §11 would
have derived, because §4 turned the derivation off. Their shapes match that
section's: a fixed 3×3 pattern of the crop's own parts for crafting, an
eight-ingredient alternating sequence for infusion, and a one-to-two conversion
for reprocessing (**read**).

Every one of the eighteen carries RVL-321 §10.5's crop-enabled condition, and the
crafting and infusion documents additionally carry a crop-has-material condition,
which is what implements §6's third state: an enabled crop whose material tag is
empty loses its crafting and infusion recipes and keeps its reprocessing recipe.

The six crafting documents carry a **third** condition on top of those two: the
same seed-crafting-recipes setting RVL-321 §4 records, which defaults **off**
(**read**). So in a default installation those six load no recipe at all, and a
`recipe-parity` expectation written from the shipped documents alone would look
for six crafting recipes the upstream entry does not produce.

Six more documents build the anchor blocks of §6, each conditioned on its crop
being enabled and, for the four companion crops, on the companion mod being
loaded. A further eight produce host or companion-mod items from crop output.

### 14.4 The seventy-seven documents for an absent kind set

Every recipe under the tool-forging integration's directory **is still shipped,
and none of them carries a mod-loaded condition** (**read**): seventy-seven
recipe documents in six kinds covering casting and melting, plus eighteen
material-definition, statistic and trait documents alongside them.
The Java that would have registered the corresponding materials is entirely
commented out (§2).

So these documents are loaded by the host as unknown recipe kinds whenever that
mod is absent, which in this pack it is, and are discarded. They contribute no
recipe, and they contribute one load-time complaint each on a strict loader
(**inferred** — the entry ships no condition that would suppress them and the
kinds resolve against a namespace that is not present).

This is the finding a `recipe-parity` run will trip over first, and it needs to be
stated in the expectation rather than discovered: **the shipped document count and
the loaded recipe count differ by seventy-seven**. A counterpart ships neither the
documents nor the integration, and its recipe-parity expectation is written
against the loaded set of eighty, not the shipped set of 157.

Six further documents under the same integration's asset tree describe material
appearance and are equally inert. A seventh inert asset document sits nearby but
belongs to a different third-party library and is not part of this count
(**read**).

## 15. World generation

Four deposits, two per non-overworld dimension, each a configured feature, a
placement and a biome modifier gated on one of §3's four switches (**read**).
Against RVL-321 §14's table:

| deposit | feature | size | target | count per chunk | height | biomes | step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| shard ore, lower dimension | host ore feature | 8 | one block match, the dimension's bulk stone | 12 | uniform, absolute 10 to 100 | the lower dimension's biome tag | underground ores |
| essence ore, lower dimension | host ore feature | 8 | same | 16 | uniform, absolute 10 to 100 | same | underground ores |
| shard ore, outer dimension | host ore feature | 8 | one block match, the dimension's bulk stone | 12 | uniform, absolute 10 to 100 | the outer dimension's biome tag | underground ores |
| essence ore, outer dimension | host ore feature | 8 | same | 16 | uniform, absolute 10 to 100 | same | underground ores |

All four use the host's ore feature unchanged — there is no substitution rule here
of the kind RVL-321 §14 specifies for its third deposit — and all four use the
host's count, square-spread, uniform-height and biome-filter placements in that
order, with an air-exposure discard chance of zero (**read**).

Two facts are worth separating from the table. The **shard deposits are rarer than
the essence deposits** at every location, 12 against 16, which is the same relative
scarcity RVL-321 §14 gives the two overworld deposits and is presumably deliberate.
And **all four use identical height bounds** in dimensions whose usable vertical
ranges are quite different, so the outer dimension's deposits sit in a band that is
mostly below its islands (**inferred** — the bounds are absolute and were not
compared against that dimension's terrain).

Each of the four biome modifiers reads its switch **at the moment biomes are
modified**, so turning a deposit off affects chunks generated afterwards and leaves
existing chunks alone (**inferred**, on the same evidence as RVL-321 §14). The
modifier also re-tests the target biome set even though the declared biome tag
already restricts it, so the gate is applied twice.

The four ore blocks drop themselves under silk touch and otherwise drop the
corresponding material of RVL-321's namespace, uniform with the host's ore-drops
fortune formula and explosion decay (§16). The range differs by material: the
two essence ores roll 3 to 5 and the two shard ores roll 2 to 5 (**read**).

**Host-blocked**: Ravel publishes no world-feature or biome-modifier declaration.
A counterpart records the four registrations as blocked, carries the placement
parameters as declared data and the switches as ordinary settings, and notes that
nothing in this entry's world generation needs a portable predicate — unlike
RVL-321 §14, every rule here is expressible in the host's own declarative
vocabulary.

## 16. Loot, tags, translations, and what survives a reload

### 16.1 Loot

Nineteen block loot documents for twenty block items (§8 explains the twentieth).
Three shapes (**read**):

- **fifteen self-drops** — the three storage blocks, the six fuel blocks and the
  six anchor blocks — each with a survives-explosion condition and nothing else;
- **four ore alternatives** — self under silk touch, otherwise a uniform count of
  RVL-321's shard or essence material with the host's ore-drops fortune formula
  and explosion decay applied in that order; the two shard ores roll 2 to 5 and
  the two essence ores roll 3 to 5;
- **no document** for the ground block.

No entity loot table, no chest loot table, no loot modifier: §12's boss drops go
through an event, not through loot (**read**). `loot-parity` therefore observes
nineteen documents, one code path and one event handler, and the event handler is
not loot-table data at all.

### 16.2 Tags

Forty-eight tag documents (**read**), and none of them replaces: every one
appends.
They divide as:

- **conventional storage-block tags**, nine specific ones plus one aggregating
  document, mirrored across the block and item registries — twenty documents for
  ten facts;
- **conventional material tags** for the ingot, nugget and gemstone, each a
  specific document plus an aggregating one;
- **conventional ore tags**: an inferior/superior split by material, an
  in-ground-by-host-stone split, and a singular drop-rate tag, again mirrored
  across both registries;
- **host mineability**: nineteen blocks under pickaxe, the ground block under
  shovel, and a tier split placing the two shard ores and the gemstone block at
  iron and the two essence ores, the ingot block and all six anchor blocks at
  stone;
- **tool tags**: the six tools appear in a conventional tools tag, a conventional
  melee-weapons tag, RVL-042's own multi-tool tag and a host cluster-harvest tag —
  four listings of the same six items;
- **RVL-321 tags**: the six crops' blocks, essences and seeds are appended to that
  entry's three catalogue tags, which is how the crops become visible to RVL-321's
  own tag-driven rules;
- **one tag of this entry's own**, the two-member optional tag of §6.

The block/item mirroring is mechanical and complete: every conventional block tag
here has an item counterpart with identical members. A counterpart that derives one
from the other reproduces it; `tag-parity` will otherwise report each fact twice.

### 16.3 Translations

Twelve language documents (**read**). The reference language carries 104 keys.
The other eleven carry 104, 104, 104, 104, 104, 98, 98, 105, 108, 39 and 39, so
five of them match the reference exactly.

`language-parity` must be written against those numbers, not against an assumption
of parity, because **none of the three deviations is a translation gap of the
ordinary kind**:

- **two languages carry 39 keys**, well under half. They are stale and were last
  updated several content additions ago.
- **two carry 98**, six short.
- **two carry more keys than the reference**, 105 and 108. Extra keys are either
  stale entries for removed content or entries for content this entry no longer
  ships; they are inert either way, and they mean the parity comparison is not
  a subset test in one direction.

The keys themselves cover item and block names, the creative listing title, three
tooltip lines and two recipe-viewer descriptions. Their text is upstream material
and is not transcribed.

### 16.4 What survives a save and reload

This is the cluster scenario's second half, and the answer is short (**read**):

**This entry owns no world-persisted state of its own.** It has no block entity,
no saved data, no attached data, no capability and no network payload. Everything
it contributes to a world is either a block state the host persists, an item stack
the host persists, or a registry identifier.

So the reload contract is entirely about identity:

- **Block states.** The ground block's moisture is a host block-state value in the
  0-to-7 range of RVL-321 §9.1 and round-trips through the host's own chunk
  storage. Nothing else in §7.1 has a state beyond its identity.
- **Item-level data.** Two carriers. The six tools hold RVL-321 §12.8's slotted
  modifier list and the host's damage and unbreakable markers; the six buckets hold
  nothing. A tool's modifier list is RVL-321's format, written by RVL-321's
  facilities, and this entry only ensures it is present from construction (§11).
- **Effects.** §10's effects are on the player, in the host's own effect storage,
  and survive a reload with their accumulated durations intact — including
  durations accumulated past what any single food grants.
- **Registry identifiers.** All 53 items and 26 blocks must resolve on reload, and
  §6 guarantees the four companion crops' identifiers exist even when their mods do
  not — so a world saved with a companion mod present and reloaded without it keeps
  its blocks and items and loses only their recipes.

That last point is the one real reload hazard, and it is inherited from RVL-321
§15 rather than introduced here. A counterpart that registers conditionally instead
of registering-then-disabling turns a recipe loss into a world-corrupting
identifier loss.

## 18. What this specification does not specify

- **The catalogue's names and appearance.** Twenty-six blocks, fifty-three items,
  six fluid families and six crops were read as existing and their identifiers are
  not transcribed; every one of them is an upstream identifier. §5 to §7 and §14
  are the structure a counterpart needs to carry equivalent content of its own
  choosing. What *is* carried across is behavioural: the tier values of §5, the
  arithmetic of §9 to §12, the placement parameters of §15 and the census counts,
  because tests observe those.
- **The four companion mods by name**, and the specific third-party items the
  eight recipes of §14.1 produce. They are other projects' identifiers and naming
  them would put an upstream identifier in this document for no behavioural gain.
  §6's table says what shape each dependency takes, which is the part that matters.
- **The two third-party integration shims** (§2) beyond their gating and the fact
  that they add no behaviour. Each names the mod it integrates with.
- **The commented-out tool-forging integration** as behaviour, because it has
  none. What is specified is the consequence of shipping its data anyway (§14.4).
- **The 413 declarative documents individually.** Block states, models, and the
  62 images are presentation; they were read for the census of §7 and §16 and for
  nothing else. The textures were fetched with the source, were not read as
  behaviour, and nothing is shipped from them.
- **The translation text** (§16.3). The key counts are specified because
  `language-parity` measures them; the strings are upstream material.
- **A guide-book chapter and five entries** shipped under RVL-321's asset
  namespace, read as existing only. They are documentation for a third mod's
  reader and carry no behaviour.
- **The thin base types** this entry builds on — a base block, a base ore block, a
  base item, a base block item and a base multi-tool, all from RVL-042. RVL-042 §15
  already declines to specify them; every override in this entry that carries
  behaviour is named above.
- **Whether the discarded base result in §11's break handling is observable**,
  marked **unknown**. It depends on what RVL-042's base multi-tool returns, which
  RVL-042 §15 does not specify.
- **Whether the outer dimension's two deposits generate usefully** (§15), marked
  **inferred, not read**: the height bounds are absolute and were not compared
  against that dimension's terrain generation. If they are largely below the
  islands, the two deposits are far rarer in practice than their counts suggest,
  and `worldgen-parity` would measure a placement that matches and an outcome that
  does not.
- **Whether the ninety-five documents of §14.4 produce a load error or a silent discard**
  on this pack's loader, marked **inferred**. Both readings agree that no recipe
  results; they differ on whether a launch produces seventy-seven complaints. The
  wave's own launch is where that resolves.
- **Whether either of the two languages at thirty-nine keys is intentional**
  (§16.3), marked **unknown**. They are treated here as stale.

### Coverage

**Read in full**: every one of the thirty-four source files, including the two
that are entirely commented out — about 1,300 lines of live code, so the code half
of this entry was read completely and nothing in §2 to §13 is a sample. Every
configuration value and bound. All twelve fuel constants, checked individually
against the derivation of §9. All six food rows and all six tool rows, checked
individually. All four world-generation feature, placement and biome-modifier
documents. All nineteen loot documents. All forty-eight tag documents. The mod
descriptor including its four dependency declarations.

**Read as a census, not individually**: the 157 recipe documents. Every one was
read for its kind, its result, its result count and its conditions, and the
groups of §14.2 and §14.3 were read individually within each group's first member
and then confirmed to be uniform. The seventy-seven documents of §14.4 were read
only far enough to establish their kinds and the absence of a mod-loaded
condition, because they produce no recipe.

**Counted, not read**: the twelve translation documents, counted by key. The 62
images and the block-state and model documents, counted by file. The guide-book
chapter and its five entries.

**Not read**: nothing in the source tree was skipped entirely.

**Revision**: this specification is written against the upstream revision whose
build script declares the version matching the pack's pinned artifact exactly.
The fixture tree also holds a later branch of the same project, at a different game
version; it was **not** read, and no claim here comes from it. The provenance
record names the revision and its digest.

**The weakness that remains**: one reader wrote both this document and its
provenance record in one pass, and no independent review has read either. The
read/inferred/unknown markers and this coverage statement are what stand in for a
second reader, and they are weaker than one. The specific places to distrust first
are the four **inferred** claims, because each of them is an inference about host
behaviour made from reading this entry's source rather than the host's.
