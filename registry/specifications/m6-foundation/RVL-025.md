Wave: `waves/m6-foundation.json`. Method: source-informed reimplementation.
Rights: the upstream project's repository root carries an MIT licence file, read
at the source; see `provenance/m6-foundation/RVL-025.json` for the pinned
revision, the licence identity and the digest.

Confidence markers: **read**, **inferred**, **unknown**.

This entry is admitted as a foundation seed and names exactly one dependency,
RVL-120 (**read**, from the wave manifest and the plan row). Three admitted
counterparts outside this wave — RVL-018, RVL-051 and RVL-293 — declare it as a
required dependency on both sides, and none of them can start until it exists

Where this entry consumes what RVL-120 provides, the citation is to that
specification by section number and title, because RVL-120 is under revision

## 1. What this entry is

A small mod that is two things at once (**read**). It is a playable content pack
in its own right — seventeen items, five blocks, one machine, one ore that
generates in three environments, twelve crafting recipes — and it is the
published integration surface that a family of larger mods builds against. The
two halves are unequal in size and inverted in importance: the content half is
what a player sees, and the interface half is the reason three other admitted
entries depend on it.

The published interface surface declares no behaviour at all: it is interfaces,
enumerations and small value carriers that a dependent implements or receives
(**read**). The rest is the mod proper, with a client-presentation part — screens,
renderers and the client entry, setup and world-render points — that §18 excludes
because Ravel draws its own. Two of the subsystems the surface publishes are not
implemented anywhere in this entry — a programmable-machine instruction model and
a cabling-network model — and are published here only so that the mods owning
those subsystems and the mods consuming them can agree on a type without
depending on each other (**read**). That matters to scoping: the contract is the
larger half of this entry, not the code. How the tree divides file by file is its
own package layout, and is stated only in §18's coverage paragraph.

The entry declares itself required on both sides, and declares two requirements
of its own: the loader, and the shared library of RVL-120 at a stated minimum
version (**read**, from the mod descriptor).

### 1.1 Ravel disposition

Ravel already owns content declaration and registration, configuration
declaration, saved-data ownership, side isolation and screen drawing, so the
registration mechanics below are expressed through Ravel facilities rather than
rebuilt. What survives as counterpart work is in three groups:

- **The decision rules and arithmetic** that a parity test can check: the match
  verdict (§8), the infusing loop and its energy discount (§7), the ore placement
  numbers (§6), and the carrier's slot cycling (§10).
- **The wire and document contracts** a dependent or a saved world observes: the
  five item data records (§4), the six network payloads (§13), and the recipe,
  tag, loot and world-generation documents (§5, §6).
- **The published interface surface** (§15), which is a Ravel-side API design
  problem rather than a transliteration problem, because the upstream shapes are
  written against the host's own types.

One area is host-blocked rather than declined: the optional in-game guidebook
integration (§17) reaches a third-party mod that is not in scope, and a
counterpart records the gap rather than stubbing it.

## 2. Side, lifecycle and what happens when

The entry runs on both sides (**read**):

- At construction it composes its feature groups, declares its two
  configuration documents, claims eleven registration groups, and asks to be told
  about common setup, group initialisation, data generation, and network payload
  registration. It also registers a block-capability provider for its one machine
  family. Splitting the features across a fixed number of group objects is the
  original's structure; the observable consequence is that each setup point below
  is asked for once, and there is none beyond that, so do not port the shape. The
  eleven registration groups are not structure: §4 says which are claimed and
  which are left empty, and a counterpart is answerable for that.
- At construction, on a client only, it asks for three further points: client
  setup, key-mapping registration, and per-group client initialisation.
- At construction it begins observing the server-started signal, and empties the
  cross-block ordering queue when it fires (§14).
- At common setup, on every side, it enqueues two pieces of work that must run
  after registration and after configuration is read: it registers its two
  server-directed commands (§14), and it tells RVL-120's command transport how to
  encode one list-valued reply.
- Also at common setup it begins observing the post-tick signal for every level,
  and on the server side only it drains the ordering queue for the level that
  ticked.
- At client setup it begins observing the world-render signal and the key-input
  signal.

**Ordering that is load-bearing** (**read** for the ordering, **inferred** for
the consequence). The command registration and the reply-encoding registration
are both deferred to the enqueued phase specifically because they need every
registry populated and configuration resolved; a counterpart that runs either
earlier gets a partially-populated view. The ordering-queue drain is guarded to
the server side by an explicit test on the level, so a counterpart declares that
observer server-only rather than testing at runtime.

In Ravel terms: the eleven registration groups belong in `ContentDeclaration` and
are claimed through `Registrar`; the two configuration documents are one
`ConfigurationDeclaration` resolved before `configure`; the observed signals are
`Mod::observe`; and every client-only registration and observer is declared
client-only rather than guarded by a runtime side test.

## 3. Configuration

Two documents are declared, one server-scoped and one common-scoped, and **only
one of them is ever populated** (**read**). Four settings exist, all four in the
server document, all four in a single grouping that describes the infusing
machine:

| setting | default | range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| upgrade steps to full infusion | 256 | 1 to signed 32-bit max |
| energy consumed per tick while infusing | 600 | 0 to signed 32-bit max |
| energy buffer capacity | 60000 | 0 to signed 32-bit max |
| energy accepted per tick | 600 | 0 to signed 32-bit max |

The common document is built and registered but no feature group adds a setting
to it, so it resolves to an empty document (**read**). A counterpart should
declare one document, not two: an empty second document is a file a pack author
can find, edit and be ignored by.

Neither document is synchronised to a joining client, and all four settings are
read on the server. **They are not read at the same time** (**read**). The two
energy settings — buffer capacity and accept rate — are read once, at the
moment the machine's block entity is constructed. The upgrade ceiling is read on
every acceptance test and the per-tick price on every start, so both take effect
immediately. The consequence is that editing the buffer capacity or the accept
rate leaves an already-loaded machine unchanged until it is reloaded, while
editing the other two changes behaviour at once. A counterpart that resolves all
four uniformly through Ravel configuration differs from the original in one
direction only — it is more responsive — and should record that rather than
reproduce the split.

## 4. What it registers

The full census, counted from the tree twice (**read**):

| kind | count |
| --- | --- |
| items | 17 |
| blocks | 5 |
| block entity kinds | 2 |
| menu kinds | 4 |
| item data component kinds | 5 |
| per-owner data attachments | 1 |
| world-placement modifier kinds | 2 |
| creative grouping | 1 |
| sound kinds | 0 |
| entity kinds | 0 |

Two registration groups are claimed and left empty: sounds and entities
(**read**). A counterpart claims neither.

Sixteen of the seventeen items are placed in the entry's own creative grouping.
The exception is the second appearance of the handheld carrier (§10), which is a
display variant reached only by filling the carrier and is deliberately absent
from the grouping so a player cannot obtain it directly (**read**).

**The five item data records** are the entry's persistent vocabulary, and their
serialised identifiers are compatibility-required: a saved world written by the
original carries them, and a counterpart that wants to read that world needs the
exact spellings, which are obtainable from the pinned artifact and are not
written here (see the names surface in the provenance record, which is
unresolved). What each record holds is behaviour and is written here:

1. **Pattern contents** — an ordered list of item stacks, empty entries
   preserved, persisted and network-synchronised (§9).
2. **Match-rule contents** — a list of item stacks, a list of tag identifiers, and
   four boolean mode flags (§8).
3. **Infusion progress** — one integer (§7). This one identifier is registered
   *twice*, once as an item data record and once as a per-owner attachment, which
   is how progress survives the block being broken and replaced.
4. **Carrier contents** — a list of item stacks plus one integer naming the
   selected slot (§10).
5. **Selected position** — one dimension-qualified block position (§11).

Records 1, 4 and 5 tolerate absent entries in their persisted form; record 2 does
not, and its list of stacks is decoded strictly (**read**). A counterpart that
makes record 2 tolerant changes what a corrupt save does, and should record the
choice.

## 5. Content, recipes and the documents that ship with them

The generated document set is 39 files, and the hand-written asset set adds 13
world-generation and guidebook documents (**read**). By kind:

| document kind | count |
| --- | --- |
| crafting recipes | 12 |
| recipe-unlock advancements | 12 |
| block loot tables | 5 |
| tag documents | 9 |
| block-state documents | 7 |
| model documents | 32 |
| language entries | 55 |

The table covers one origin and a half: the 39 generated documents are the first
four rows plus the loot-modifier document named in the defects below, and the
last three rows are assets that ship beside them. The 13 hand-written data
documents are in neither figure and appear nowhere in this table: four attach the
ore to its biomes, four describe its shape and four place it (§6), and one
declares the guidebook (§17).

Every one of the twelve recipes is a shaped crafting recipe and every one has a
matching unlock advancement, one to one (**read**). Two of the seventeen items
have no recipe at all: the alternate mode of the configuration tool (§11), which
is reached by using the tool rather than by crafting, and the filled variant of
the handheld carrier (§10). Three of the five blocks have no recipe either: they
are the ore blocks, which are obtained by mining (§6).

**Five defects in the shipped documents, all read directly** (all five are
things a parity test will find, so a counterpart should decide about each rather
than copy it):

- **Six item labels and one block label name content that is not registered at
  this revision.** The language file carries eighteen item labels for twelve
  items that need one, and six block labels for five that do. The surplus names a
  monitoring device, a locating device, two cards, a wand, a joke item and a
  security block, none of which exists here any more.
- **Two block-state documents name blocks that are not registered.** They belong
  to two crafting intermediates that are plain items at this revision.
- **Two key-binding labels are dead**, matching two bindings that are present in
  the source only as commented-out lines (§14).
- **The loot-modifier document is empty.** It declares no entry and does not
  replace, so it is a file with no effect.
- **One of the four ore shape documents is unreferenced.** The fourth placement
  document names the overworld variant's shape document rather than the
  identically-named one that ships beside it, so that fourth shape document is
  read by nothing at this revision and its vein size never reaches generation.
  §6 states the effective numbers and what a counterpart should do about it.

The one loot behaviour worth stating exactly is the machine's (§7): its loot
table copies the block's name and four named data components from the block
entity into the dropped item, so a broken machine keeps its energy, its
inventory, its infusion progress and its infusion level. Two of those four
component identifiers belong to RVL-120 and one belongs here (**read**). This is
the concrete case of RVL-120 §16 *What dependents need* — the dependent's loot
document names the shared library's component identifiers directly, so the
identifier vocabulary is a cross-entry contract and not an implementation detail
of either side.

## 6. The ore and how it generates

One material generates as ore in three environments, through three ore blocks
that are distinct registrations but identical in behaviour (**read**). Each is a
plain block with metal sound and colour, hardness 3 and blast resistance 5, and
each emits light level 7. Breaking one on a client spawns ten firework particles
with Gaussian-distributed velocity scaled by one third; the particle burst is
client-only and does not affect drops (**read**).

**The drop rule**, identical for all three (**read**): a silk-touch tool drops the
ore block itself; otherwise the block drops the raw material with a count drawn
uniformly from 4 to 5 inclusive, then a uniform fortune bonus with multiplier 1,
then explosion decay. All three are mineable with a pickaxe and require at least
an iron-tier tool.

**The placement numbers**, counted twice from the four placement documents and
the shape documents they actually name (**read**). The fourth row's vein size is
the one figure where a declared number and the effective number differ: the
fourth shape document declares 10, but no placement document names it, so
generation reads the overworld shape document and the effective vein size is 5.
The table states the effective figure throughout.

| variant | vein size | veins per chunk | height range | biome selector |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| overworld | 5 | 2 | −15 to 40 | overworld biome tag |
| nether | 8 | 8 | 2 to 40 | nether biome tag |
| end | 10 | 8 | 2 to 80 | end biome tag |
| other dimensions | 5 (see below) | 6 | −15 to 40 | overworld biome tag |

All four are placed in the underground-ore generation step, all four discard
nothing on air exposure, all four spread horizontally within the chunk square, and
all four draw height uniformly between the stated absolute bounds. The nether and
end variants target the ordinary nether-stone and end-stone replaceable tags. The
overworld and fourth placements read the same shape document, which targets the
stone replaceable tag and places the *overworld* ore block, so the fourth variant
is the overworld ore under different placement rules rather than a fourth ore
(**read**). The unreferenced fourth shape document, listed as the fifth defect in
§5, would place the same block at vein size 10 if anything named it.

**The fourth variant is the reason this entry registers placement modifiers at
all.** Two custom placement modifiers are registered, and each wraps the standard
biome check with a dimension test: one places only when the level *is* the
overworld, the other only when it is not. The overworld variant uses the first;
the fourth variant uses the second and is attached to overworld biomes. The
effect is that the fourth variant generates in any dimension that uses overworld
biomes *except the overworld itself* — that is, in dimensions created by other
mods in this family — at a richer rate than the overworld gets (**inferred**;
the source states the rule but never states this intent).

**A defect a counterpart must not copy** (**read**): both custom placement
modifiers report the *standard biome check* as their kind rather than their own.
Anything that serialises a placed feature back out therefore writes the standard
check's identifier and loses the dimension test. It does not affect generation
from the shipped documents, because those documents name the custom kinds
directly and are only ever read.

## 7. The upgrading machine

One machine, one block entity kind, one menu. It consumes the raw material from
§6 to raise an "infusion level" on any machine item that declares itself
upgradable, which is a facility RVL-120 owns (RVL-120 §6, *Common data every
dependent's block carries*).

**Structure** (**read**): two slots. Slot 0 accepts only items carrying the
material's own tag, and holds up to 64. Slot 1 accepts only an upgradable machine
item that is not already at the ceiling, and holds exactly 1. Both the insertion
test and the slot limit are enforced on the automation-facing handler as well as
in the menu, so a hopper cannot bypass them. The machine exposes four
capabilities: the item handler, an energy buffer, the menu provider, and its own
infusion level.

**The cycle** (**read**, and this is the parity-testable core):

1. If progress is zero and slot 0 holds the material and slot 1 holds an
   acceptable machine item, the machine *starts*: it computes the energy price,
   refuses and does nothing if the buffer holds less, otherwise deducts the price,
   consumes exactly one item from slot 0, and sets progress to 5.
2. If progress is non-zero, it decrements by one each tick. On reaching zero it
   raises the output item's infusion level by exactly one.

So one unit of material costs five ticks and one energy payment, and the payment
happens **at the start** of the five ticks, not spread across them (**read**).

**The energy price is discounted by the machine's own infusion level**
(**read**). The price is the configured per-tick figure multiplied by
`(2 − f) / 2`, where `f` is the machine's own infusion factor as RVL-120 defines
it (RVL-120 §6). At `f = 0` the price is the full configured figure; at `f = 1`
it is half. The multiplication is done in single-precision floating point and
truncated toward zero to an integer, so a counterpart that computes it in
double precision or rounds to nearest will disagree at some inputs. A parity test
should check the boundary values and at least one mid-range factor.

**Two behaviours worth stating because they are easy to get wrong** (**read**):

- The acceptance test for slot 1 re-reads the configured ceiling on every call,
  so lowering the ceiling immediately makes already-infused items unacceptable.
- On the tick that completes an infusion, the output item is re-read from the slot
  rather than remembered from the start. If it was extracted mid-cycle, the
  completion silently applies to nothing and the material is already spent.

## 8. The stack-match decision

A single-stack carrier item holds a set of item stacks, a set of tag identifiers,
and four mode flags, and answers one question: does this item stack pass?

**The verdict**, in order (**read**):

1. An empty stack never passes. This is decided before the inversion, so an empty
   stack fails even in exclusion mode.
2. If the tag set is non-empty, the stack passes the tag test when any tag the
   item carries is in the set. The tag test is skipped entirely when the set is
   empty.
3. If the tag test did not match, the stack set is scanned in order. A stored
   entry is *rejected* when the damage flag is set and the two damage values
   differ, or when the component flag is set and the two are not the same item
   with the same components. An entry that survives both rejections matches when
   the namespace flag is set and the two namespaces are equal, or, with the flag
   clear, when the two items are equal. The first matching entry wins.
4. The raw verdict is inverted by the exclusion flag: the answer is the raw
   verdict *unequal to* the flag.

Three consequences a counterpart should preserve deliberately rather than by
accident (**inferred** from the rule, stated nowhere in the source):

- The two rejection flags apply to the stack test only. A tag match ignores
  damage and components entirely.
- With the namespace flag set, the component rejection still runs first, so a
  rule in namespace mode with the component flag also set matches only entries
  that are already component-identical, which makes the namespace comparison
  almost always redundant.
- The stack test compares against stored entries whose count is always exactly 1
  (§8.1), so a match rule can never depend on a stack size.

### 8.1 How the match rule is filled

Three ways (**read**): through the item's own menu; by using it on a
block while crouching, which stores either every distinct item in that block's
inventory or, when the block has no inventory, the block's own item form; and
programmatically by a dependent.

Insertion is deduplicating and count-normalising: an added stack is copied, its
count forced to 1, and the addition abandoned if an existing entry is the same
item with the same components (**read**).

**A defect in removal** (**read**): removal by item scans the list by index and
removes in place without adjusting the index, so removing when two adjacent
entries both match skips the second. Because insertion deduplicates on the same
predicate, two adjacent entries cannot both match, and the defect is unreachable
through the shipped paths (**inferred**). A counterpart writes the correct loop.

**The carrier's own tooltip** enumerates its contents by expanding every stored
tag to its members, and does so on the client for every hover (**read**). A
counterpart should bound that expansion; the source does not.

## 9. The pattern card

An item, stackable to 8, that stores a crafting pattern and the result it
produces, so that machines elsewhere in the family can be told what to make.

**The grid is 5 by 4, not 3 by 3** (**read**). Twenty input positions are stored
plus one output position at index 20. A 3-by-3 recipe occupies the first three
positions of each of the first three rows, and the remaining eleven input
positions are empty. The output position is filled by evaluating the pattern
(below), never by the player.

**Evaluating a pattern** (**read**): on the "test" command (§14) the first three
positions of each of the first three rows are copied into a 3-by-3 crafting input
in row-major order, every registered crafting recipe is scanned in registry
iteration order until one matches, and the first match's assembled result is
written to the output position. No match writes an empty output. The scan is a
linear pass over the whole recipe set on every test, with no index and no cache
(**read**); a counterpart should say so as a known ceiling if it reproduces it.

**Reading a pattern back**, three ways, and they are not equivalent (**read**):
one returns all twenty inputs as ingredients; one returns only the positions
inside the 3-by-3 sub-grid as ingredients; one returns those same positions as
raw stacks. A dependent that wants a vanilla-shaped recipe uses the second or
third; one that wants the full pattern uses the first.

**A defect that disables a published test** (**read**): the predicate that asks
whether a card fits a 3-by-3 grid iterates every stored position and demands that
each be inside the sub-grid. Since the stored list is always twenty-one entries
long, the fourth position — which is outside the sub-grid by construction — fails
the test, and the predicate returns false for every card that has ever been
written. A counterpart implements the intended test, which is that every
*non-empty* position lies inside the sub-grid, and should expect a parity test
against the original to disagree here.

**One menu quirk** (**read**): while the card's menu is open, the hotbar slot the
card itself came from is made non-removable, so a player cannot take the card out
from under its own screen.

## 10. The handheld carrier

A single-stack item that holds up to six sub-items, each of which must declare
itself carrier-compatible, and presents whichever one is selected.

**Behaviour** (**read**):

- The carrier has six slots, each limited to one item, and rejects any item that
  does not declare carrier support. Extraction with simulation returns the slot's
  contents without clearing it; extraction without simulation clears the slot
  unconditionally and ignores the requested amount.
- Cycling forward or backward walks the slots modulo six, at most seven attempts,
  stopping at the first non-empty slot and selecting it. A carrier with one
  occupied slot therefore selects that slot; a carrier with none leaves the
  selection unchanged.
- Using the carrier while crouching always opens the carrier's own screen. Using
  it otherwise opens the selected sub-item's screen, or the carrier's own screen
  when the selection is empty.

**The item swap is the surprising part** (**read**). The carrier is *replaced*
whenever the selection changes or the selected slot's contents change: if the
newly-selected slot is occupied, the carrier becomes the item form that the
sub-item nominates, otherwise it becomes the plain carrier, and the data record
is carried across. The replacement is written directly into the player's
inventory at the *currently selected hotbar index* rather than into the hand the
interaction came from. A counterpart should write to the interacting hand; the
original's choice misplaces the carrier whenever the two differ (**inferred** —
the source contains a commented-out hand-based write, so the divergence was
noticed and not resolved).

**A latent failure** (**read**): the replacement path casts the slot's contents to
the carrier-support interface without testing it, and the insertion path is what
guarantees the test. Any route that writes the data record directly, such as the
hand-synchronisation payload of §13, can produce a carrier whose selected slot
holds an unsupported item and whose next selection change fails.

## 11. The configuration tool

Two item registrations that are one tool with two modes (**read**). Using the
tool switches modes by *replacing the item with the other registration*, carrying
the stored position across, and telling the player. The mode is therefore a
property of which item you hold, not a flag inside it, which is why the second
registration has no recipe and why both carry the shared tool tag.

**In the first mode** the tool does nothing itself: it exists so that a
crouch-use on a block from this family activates that block's own interaction
without placing anything (**read**).

**In the second mode** it stores and applies a selection (**read**):

- A crouch-use on a block from this family activates that block, as in the first
  mode, and takes precedence over selection.
- Otherwise, if a position is stored and it is in a different dimension from the
  player, the tool refuses and says so.
- Otherwise, if the block at the stored position accepts selections, the tool
  hands it the clicked position.

The stored position is dimension-qualified and lives in one item data record
(§4). Clearing it removes the record rather than storing a sentinel (**read**).

**A gap worth recording** (**read**): nothing in this entry ever *sets* the stored
position. The setter is published for dependents, and every write comes from
outside this entry. A counterpart's tool is therefore untestable within its own
package, and its parity test needs a dependent or a fixture.

## 12. The readout block

A block that displays information taken from whatever block sits behind it.

**Behaviour** (**read**):

- It looks at the position opposite its own facing, and asks that position for an
  information provider capability. Every tenth server tick, if a provider is
  there, it calls the provider's tick so that rate-derived figures can be
  computed.
- When no provider is published but the block behind holds energy, the readout
  synthesises a default provider that reports stored and maximum energy as two
  named long values.
- It stores one mode as a byte, saved to the world and sent to the client
  separately from the world save. Toggling advances to the next mode in the
  provider's supported list, wrapping; a mode not in that list leaves the mode
  unchanged.
- The provider declares two reserved modes, plain energy and graphical energy,
  and may declare its own beyond them.

**Two defects** (**read**): the facing lookup returns nothing when the block state
is not the expected kind, and both the tick path and the provider lookup use the
result without testing it. And the default energy provider re-queries the energy
capability on every information request rather than holding it.

**The client never computes the readout.** It asks (§13), the server answers with
a typed map, and the client renders from that map. A counterpart keeps that split:
the readout's content is server-derived, and the client holds only the last answer
and the timestamp of it.

## 13. The network surface

Six payloads on one channel, declared optional and versioned, so a client without
the mod can still connect (**read**). Five travel to the server and one to the
client:

| direction | payload | carries |
| --- | --- | --- |
| to server | components of the held item | one item stack |
| to server | typed arguments for the held card | a typed map |
| to server | a pattern from the recipe browser | a list of item stacks |
| to server | a readout request | one block position |
| to server | the held item, wholesale | one item stack |
| to client | a readout answer | a block position and a typed map |

**The validation rules, which are the security-relevant part** (**read**):

- The components payload applies the sent stack's *components only* to the held
  item, and only when the held item is the pattern card.
- The wholesale payload replaces the held item outright, and guards it with a
  type test on **both** the held item and the sent item: both must be either the
  match item or the handheld carrier. The comment in the source says this is
  to stop clients from sending arbitrary items.
- The typed-arguments payload validates the held item and then does nothing: its
  entire body is commented out at this revision, so the payload is registered,
  accepted and discarded.
- The recipe-browser payload writes into the open menu only when the held item is
  the pattern card *and* the open menu is that card's menu.
- The readout request checks that the chunk is loaded before touching the block
  entity, and answers only to the requesting player.

**Three findings a counterpart must act on rather than copy** (**inferred**;
the source states the checks but not their sufficiency):

- The wholesale payload's type test is a *class* test, not a contents test. A
  client may send a match item whose data record is arbitrary — any stack
  list, any tag list — and the server accepts it. A counterpart validates the
  record, not just the item kind. This is also the route that produces the latent
  failure of §10.
- The readout request checks chunk load but not distance or line of sight, so any
  player may read any loaded position.
- The components payload copies the whole component map of an attacker-chosen
  stack onto a held item. A counterpart copies only the one record it means to.

The typed map used by two of these payloads is RVL-120's, and its encoding is that
specification's (RVL-120 §9, *The network surface*). This entry adds one
registration to it at common setup: it teaches the transport how to encode a
list-of-strings reply, with each element bounded at 32767 characters (**read**).
That is the one place this entry extends the shared transport rather than merely
using it (RVL-120 §7, *The command transport dependents build on*).

## 14. Commands, key bindings and tick ordering

**Two server-directed commands** are registered through RVL-120's command
transport (RVL-120 §7), both operating on the held item (**read**). One evaluates
the pattern card (§9). One advances or retreats the selection of any held item
that declares itself cyclable, and takes a single boolean argument for the
direction. The second reads the player's active hand in a way that can be
unset, and the source discards the result of that read on the line before using
it, which is a leftover rather than a behaviour (**read**).

**Two key bindings** are registered, both client-only, both in an in-game-only
conflict context, defaulting to the two bracket keys, and both simply send the
selection command above with the direction set (**read**). Two further bindings
exist only as commented-out lines, which is where the two dead labels of §5 come
from.

**One cross-block ordering service** is published (**read**), and this is the
piece dependents rely on most quietly. It exists so that block entities in
different mods that must act in a fixed order relative to one another can do so:

- A block entity enqueues itself, naming its dimension and one of five ranks.
- After every server-side level tick, everything queued for that dimension is
  run in rank order, rank 0 first, and the queues are emptied.
- A monotonic counter is published so a caller can tell whether it has already
  enqueued itself this tick.
- The whole structure is cleared when a server starts.

Two properties are load-bearing and a counterpart must keep them (**read** for
the mechanism, **inferred** for the requirement): the queue is per-dimension, so
ordering is not global; and each rank's list is cleared as it is run, so anything
enqueued *during* the drain of an earlier rank is picked up by a later rank in the
same tick, while anything enqueued during the last rank waits for the next tick.
The rank meanings are documented upstream only as a comment naming five device
kinds, none of which is in this entry (**read**), so the ranks are a contract
this entry publishes and does not itself use.

## 15. The published interface surface

The entry publishes an interface surface for dependents (**read**). What each
part of it publishes is an obligation in both directions: what a dependent
supplies, what this entry promises to do with it, what is refused, in which
lifecycle window, and on which side. Every one is stated that way below, from the
declarations and their documentation comments (**read**); where a claim rests on
a comment's wording rather than on a signature it is marked. How many files the
original spent on each is its own package layout and is stated nowhere but the
coverage paragraph in §18.

**Display modules.** A dependent supplies a module item that declares
its own codecs and data component type, a server-side value producer, a
client-side renderer, a name, and a settings screen built through a supplied
builder. In return the screen of the owning mod carries that module: it calls the
producer on the server whenever the screen updates, ships the produced value to
the client, calls the renderer with it, charges the per-tick energy cost the
dependent states, and forwards clicks to modules that want them. Refused: a
module whose data is not one of the five built-in kinds, until the dependent
registers a factory for it under its own namespace. That registry is reached
through an inter-mod message rather than a capability, a discovery mechanism
older than the rest of the surface (**inferred** from the comment's wording).
Window: registration at startup, production per screen update, rendering per
frame.

**Cabling network.** A dependent supplies a channel kind: an
identifier, codecs and stream codecs for both channel and connector settings, a
test for whether a given block supports the kind on a given side, and factories
for a fresh channel and a fresh connector. It may also supply a connectable or a
consumer provider instead of implementing the connection test on its block. In
return a controller ticks the channel server-side, resolves its consumers, and
persists both settings objects; a dependent can also read the network shape for a
level. Refused: anything not registered before the registration window closes,
and the surface itself is reached only through an inter-mod message to the mod
that owns it. Side: the tick and the persistence are server-side, the settings
editor is client-side.

**Programmable machines.** A dependent supplies an instruction or a
function, each declaring typed parameters, an output kind, categories and a
runnable body. In return a processor evaluates every parameter to the declared
type before the body runs, and runs it against a program that holds a last value,
an optional craft ticket and a delay the body can set to suspend itself. Refused:
an unregistered instruction, and a parameter that resolves to nothing when the
body asked for the non-null form. Window: registration at startup, evaluation and
execution during a program's run, server-side.

**Dimension services.** To read, a dependent supplies a dimension
identifier and gets back that dimension's owner, its current and maximum energy
and its probe count. To shape generation, it supplies a match description and a
settings description built through supplied builders, and gets a rule inserted
ahead of every rule added later but behind its own earlier ones. Refused: an
identifier the owning mod did not create yields nothing, and the read is declared
server-side only.

**Bulk item access.** A dependent supplies an inventory that reports a
version number it increases on every change, and in return the readers of that
inventory can skip work when the number has not moved. Separately it supplies a
stack or a predicate and an amount, and gets back an extraction capped at that
amount and the item's own stack limit, or a count across the whole system that
may exceed a stack. Both take a routability flag. Refused: a request the
providing block cannot power returns an empty stack rather than an error. Two of
these declarations carry no member at all and exist only to mark a type. Side:
server.

**Readouts.** A dependent supplies a block entity that exposes one of
two sided capabilities. For machine values it supplies a tag count, a name and a
description per index, and a value per index. For the screen it supplies a list
of supported modes, a tick entry point, a server-side collection of the values a
mode needs, and a client-side render of them. In return this entry's readout
block (§12) and a dependent's display module read the block without either side
naming the other. Refused: a block that does not expose the capability on the
queried side is absent rather than an error. Window: values are collected about
once a second on the server, rendered per frame on the client.

**Hand-held declarations.** A dependent supplies an item that names the
container item it installs into and opens its own screen when opened from there,
or an item that accepts a cycle step in either direction. In return this entry's
carrier accepts the first (§10) and its two key bindings drive the second (§14).
Refused, and it is a gap rather than a rule: this entry ships no item of either
kind, so neither obligation is testable within this package alone.

**The overlay feed.** A dependent supplies a block entity's orientation, an
above-air test, a log and an update stamp. In return the heads-up overlay is
drawn client-side from the feed that is current. Refused: a stamp at or below the
one already held, because the stamp is what says which of two feeds is current.
Side: the feed is taken on the server, the drawing is client-side.

**The recipe-browser hand-off.** A dependent supplies a grid of stacks pushed
from a recipe browser, the first being the recipe it is showing and the rest its
ingredients. In return a shown recipe can be written into a card (§17). Refused:
a grid that is not exactly that shape, and a stack a card position will not
accept. Side: server.

**Player relocation.** A dependent supplies a level and a position, and gets back
a receiver's name or nothing; it may also create a receiver at a stated power,
read a receiver's name from its item form, move a player to a dimension and
position, or ask for every destination in a dimension. Refused: a destination
that holds no receiver, because a move to a position nothing receives puts a
player where no dependent can reach them. Side: server.

These three were each declared in a file of their own upstream and no other
property joins them; that is the original's structure and there is no observable
consequence, so do not port the grouping.

Two observations about this surface (**read**):

- **Two of the groups describe subsystems this entry does not implement.**
  The cabling and programmable-machine groups have no implementation
  anywhere in this tree. They are here so that a consumer can compile against them
  without depending on the mod that owns them.
- **The interfaces are written against the host's own types** — stacks, levels,
  positions, render buffers, codecs. A Ravel counterpart cannot mirror them
  shape-for-shape; it publishes the same *capabilities* against Ravel's types.
  This is the largest design decision in the entry and it is not a translation.

Two small render helpers ship alongside the interfaces rather than inside them: a
text layout helper that handles alignment and truncation for module text, and a
scaled-text renderer that halves the transform when a true-type font is in use.
Both are client-only, both cache font instances in an unbounded map keyed by font
identifier, and both are presentation Ravel draws itself (**read**). Neither is
part of the surface above.

## 16. What dependents need from this entry

Three admitted counterparts declare this entry as a required dependency on both
sides: RVL-018, RVL-051 and RVL-293 (**read**). All three name a minimum version;
two of them name this entry's exact pinned version as the minimum and the third
names an earlier one (**read**, from the pack dependency graph). What they consume,
in the order a counterpart should build it:

1. **The published interface surface (§15)** — this is the dependency. All three
   are large mods in the same family, and the reason the graph edge exists is that
   they implement or consume the interfaces here rather than that they use the
   items. A counterpart that ships the content of §5 to §12 and not §15 satisfies
   no dependent.
2. **The cross-block ordering service (§14)** — the five ranks name device kinds
   that live in the dependents, so at least one dependent enqueues into it, and
   the ordering guarantee is a cross-mod contract (**inferred**; no dependent's
   source was read for this specification).
3. **The upgrade material and its tag (§6)** — the machine of §7 upgrades any
   block from the family, so the dependents' machines are its inputs and the
   material is a shared crafting ingredient. The tag identifier is the contract;
   its exact spelling is obtainable from the pinned artifact.
4. **The infusion mechanism (§7)** — dependents' blocks declare themselves
   upgradable through RVL-120 (RVL-120 §6, *Common data every dependent's block
   carries*) and this entry is what raises the level. Neither side works alone.
5. **The two crafting intermediates (§5)** — they are ingredients in the
   dependents' recipes and have no other purpose here.
6. **The carrier and cycling declarations (§10, §14)** — dependents' handheld
   devices declare themselves carrier-compatible and cyclable, which is what makes
   the carrier of §10 non-empty. This entry ships no carrier-compatible item of
   its own (**read**), so the carrier is untestable within this package alone.
7. **The pattern card readers (§9)** — a dependent's crafting machine reads a card
   to learn what to make.

**One thing they do not need**: the readout block (§12) is consumed through the
published provider interface, not through the block, and a dependent that
publishes a provider never names this block.

**The build order this implies.** RVL-120 first, because §7, §13 and §14 all rest
on it. Then §15, because it is what the dependents compile against and it is a
design problem rather than a port. Then §6, §7 and the two intermediates, because
they are the dependents' ingredients. The content of §8 to §12 is playable
surface that no dependent blocks on, and can come last.

## 17. Optional integrations

Two, both client-visible, neither required (**read**):

- **A recipe browser.** When one is present, this entry registers a transfer
  handler for the pattern card's menu, so a recipe shown in the browser can be
  written into a card. The transfer collects the first ingredient of each of ten
  slot views, sends them to the server, and the server writes the first into the
  output position and the rest into the first three columns of the first three
  rows. The integration is compiled against the browser's own types and is
  inactive without it.
- **An in-game guidebook.** One item's only behaviour is to open a guidebook page
  on the server side, and every block and tool in the entry names a guidebook
  entry it belongs to. Seventeen guidebook documents ship, eleven of them
  entries, four category headings, one book declaration and one placeholder.
  The book declaration is
  also one of §5's thirteen hand-written data documents, so the two figures
  overlap by exactly one and must not be added together. **This is the one
  host-blocked area**: the guidebook is a third-party mod outside the inventory,
  and a counterpart records the entry names without opening a book.

The entry declares a hook for further compatibility setup and leaves it empty
(**read**).

## 18. What this specification does not specify

- **Screen layout, widget placement and rendering.** Four screens and three
  renderers were read only far enough to establish which values reach them and
  which side computes those values. Ravel draws its own screens.
- **The host's types.** Item stacks, tags, data components, capabilities, the
  loot and recipe document schemas, biome tags, and the placement pipeline are
  host concerns. Where a rule depends on one — component equality in §8, the loot
  functions in §5, the placement steps in §6 — the rule is stated and the host
  type is named for what it does.
- **RVL-120's own behaviour.** The energy buffer, the item handler, the menu
  scaffolding, the command transport, the typed map encoding, the infusion level
  and the block-builder facilities are that specification's subject and are cited
  here, not restated.
- **The exact serialised spellings.** Data component identifiers, the material
  tag, the payload identifiers, the guidebook entry names and the world-generation
  document names are all compatibility-required, and every one of them is
  obtainable from the pinned artifact. None is written here, because the names
  surface is unresolved in the provenance record and a save-compatible counterpart
  is exactly what that record cannot clear.
- **The dependents' side of §16.** No dependent's source was read. Every claim
  about what RVL-018, RVL-051 or RVL-293 consumes is inferred from this entry's
  published surface and the dependency graph, and is marked as such.
- **Behaviour under a dedicated server.** Nothing here was run. The side claims
  come from the declared sides, the explicit side tests in the source, and the
  client-only registration points.

**Coverage.** The tree holds 143 source files: 79 published interface
declarations and 64 that are the mod proper, ten of the latter being client
presentation — four screens, three renderers and three client entry, setup and
world-render files. The two render helpers of §15 sit with the shared tools and
are not among those ten. Read in full: the mod entry point, the four setup
files, all seven feature groups, all five data records, both block entities,
the match decision and its store, the pattern card, the carrier and its
handler, the configuration tool, the ore blocks, both placement modifiers, the
ordering service, all six network payloads, the command registrations, the key
bindings, both optional integrations, the mod descriptor, the build declaration
and the licence file. Read as a census rather than line by line: the 79
published interface files — 21 display-module, 20 cabling, 20
programmable-machine, 5 dimension, 4 bulk-item-access, 4 readout, 2
hand-held-declaration and 3 single-declaration files — from which §15's
grouping is taken and whose documentation comments were read where they state a
contract. Read as documents: all 39 generated documents, all 13 hand-written
data documents and the language file, from which §5 and §6's numbers come. Not
read: the four screen files, the three renderer files and the two render
helpers beyond what §12 and §15 state; the 29 image files and the 32 model
documents, which were listed and never opened; the 17 guidebook documents,
which were listed by name and not read.
