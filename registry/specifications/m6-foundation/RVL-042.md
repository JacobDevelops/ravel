# RVL-042 — behaviour specification

Wave: `waves/m6-foundation.json`. Method: source-informed reimplementation.
Rights: the upstream project's repository root carries an MIT licence file, read
at the source; see `provenance/m6-foundation/RVL-042.json` for the pinned
revision, the licence identity and the digest.

Confidence markers: **read**, **inferred**, **unknown**.

This entry is the wave's shared library. Three other admitted entries — RVL-321,
RVL-002 and RVL-086 — load only if it does, and the wave admits it under both
foundation closure and cluster closure for that reason (**read**, from the wave
manifest). Its dependents observe it as a set of registered content kinds, a
recipe-time selection rule, a persistence envelope, and four published events;
those four areas are §5 to §9 and are the load-bearing part of this document.

---

## 1. What this entry is

A shared library with no gameplay of its own. It ships no block, and the only
content it contributes to a world is one item-level data value, one sound, five
recipe kinds, one ingredient kind and one data-pack condition (**read**). Its
whole purpose is to be depended on: it publishes base behaviour, arithmetic and
events that the mods around it build on, and its own registrations exist to
support those dependents rather than to be found by a player.

It is small — about a hundred and fifteen source files, roughly half of them
client presentation or thin base types over host types — and it declares itself
mandatory and present on both sides (**read**).

### 1.1 Ravel disposition

Ravel already owns most of what this entry's structure exists to provide.
Content declaration and registration, configuration declaration and resolution,
tag identity, saved-data ownership and side isolation are public Ravel
facilities, and a counterpart expresses the behaviour below through them rather
than rebuilding them. What survives as counterpart work is the decision rules and
the portable arithmetic: §4, §5, §7, §10 and §11.

Several areas are host-blocked at the reviewed public revision rather than
declined: they need a surface Ravel does not publish. Each is named where it
occurs, and a counterpart records them rather than stubbing them.

## 2. Side, lifecycle and what happens when

The entry runs on both sides and its behaviour splits cleanly (**read**):

- Everything in §6 is registered on every side, during the registration window,
  before anything else it does (**read**).
- Two configuration documents are declared at the same point, one client-scoped
  and one common-scoped. Neither is server-scoped and neither is synchronised to
  a joining client (**read**); §3 says why that matters.
- A one-time scan that makes every dependent's flag declarations exist runs at
  construction, before registration completes (**read**; §4).
- After the registration barrier, on every side, it observes the
  command-registration and tag-set-updated signals; on a client only, it also
  observes item tooltips and the field-of-view computation (**read**).

In Ravel terms: registration belongs in `ContentDeclaration` and is claimed
through `Registrar`; the two configuration documents are one
`ConfigurationDeclaration` resolved before `configure`; the observed signals are
`Mod::observe`; and the client-only observers are declared client-only rather
than guarded by a runtime side test.

## 3. Configuration

Four settings, in two documents, both under one grouping (**read**):

**Client document.** Two booleans turning the two tooltip additions of §12 on and
off; one defaults on, the other off (**read**). Both are pure presentation.

**Common document.** Two settings that govern §5:

- An ordered list of namespaces, presented to a pack author as the preference
  order for picking a representative item for a tag. The shipped default names
  five namespaces from mods commonly present in large packs; it is **read** but
  deliberately not transcribed, because every entry in it is an upstream
  identifier. **This setting has no observable effect at this revision**: §5.3
  shows the selection never consults it. It is declared, documented, editable,
  and inert. A counterpart that implements §5.3's proposed rule makes the setting
  live for the first time, which is a behaviour change and not parity.
- A boolean, defaulting on, that makes §5.2's file self-heal when an entry names
  an item that no longer exists.

The common document is **not** synchronised to clients (**read**). Since §5
decides a recipe's result, two sides that resolved a tag differently would
disagree about what a recipe produces. What makes that safe is that the result
travels over the network already resolved (§6), so the serialising side is
authoritative and the other side takes the answer rather than recomputing
(**inferred** — the encoding sends a resolved item rather than the tag; the
source does not state the reasoning). A counterpart must preserve that, and it
matters more for a counterpart than for the original: once §5.3's rule is live,
the settings really can differ between two sides, where today they cannot
produce a difference because neither side reads them.

## 4. Feature flags

A flag is a named boolean whose value is a live predicate over resolved
configuration. Dependents declare flags; this entry supplies the mechanism and
declares two of its own for the tooltips of §12 (**read**).

Observable contract (**read**):

- A flag is created with an identifier and a predicate, and creating it publishes
  it into a single process-wide table keyed by identifier. A later publication
  under an identifier that already exists replaces the earlier one; nothing
  refuses a duplicate.
- A flag may instead be *referenced* by identifier alone, with no predicate. Such
  a reference carries no value until it is first asked.
- **The first ask binds the reference permanently.** On first ask, the reference
  looks its identifier up in the table and adopts whatever predicate it finds; if
  the identifier is absent, it adopts a constant-false predicate. It never looks
  again. A reference asked before the owning flag was published is therefore
  permanently disabled even after the flag appears.
- Once bound, the predicate is evaluated live on every ask, so a runtime
  configuration change is visible.

The bind-once rule is required rather than incidental: it is what makes the
data-pack condition below cheap enough to evaluate per recipe, and it is directly
observable as the difference between a flag that works and one stuck off. A
counterpart reproduces it, and should make the permanent-disable case
diagnosable rather than silent (deliberate divergence: the original neither logs
nor refuses).

**Making declarations exist.** The one observable guarantee is that a dependent's
flags are published before anything asks for them, and that a dependent whose
flags fail to publish does not stop the others (**read**). How the entry arranges
that is the original's structure and is not specified. **Host-blocked**: nothing
public lets a mod force another mod's declarations to exist, so a counterpart's
flags are declared by their owner rather than discovered, and the guarantee is
met by declaration order rather than by discovery.

**The data-pack condition.** A condition kind taking one flag identifier, holding
when that flag is enabled, used to include or exclude data-driven content at load
(**read**). A counterpart exposes the same shape through Ravel's data
declaration. **Host-blocked**: no conditioned data-pack loading is public.

## 5. Choosing one item to stand for a tag

This is the entry's most consequential behaviour and the one dependents feel most
directly. A recipe may declare its result as a *tag* rather than an item; this
mechanism decides which concrete item that tag means in this installation.

### 5.1 Where the answer comes from, in order

Asked for a tag's representative, the entry consults, in this order (**read**):

1. **An item-unification mod, if present.** If a well-known unification mod is
   loaded, its preferred item for that tag wins outright and nothing else is
   consulted. Its absence is not an error.
2. **An in-memory table**, populated from the file below.
3. **A file in the configuration directory**, read and written by this entry.
4. **A fresh selection** by the rule in §5.3, which is then written into the file
   and the table.

If none of those yields an item, the answer is the empty item, and a recipe whose
result is empty declares itself special so the crafting book does not show it
(**read**).

The order is required: an installation that has unified its tags must not have
this entry contradict the unification, and a pack author's explicit file entry
must beat an automatic selection.

### 5.2 The file

A flat map from tag identifier to item identifier, pretty-printed, written into
the configuration directory (**read**). Observable rules:

- One key is reserved as a comment carrying a documentation pointer, and is
  skipped when reading. A fresh file contains only that key.
- A value that is empty, or spells the word null in any mixture of cases, means
  "no representative"; the entry re-selects rather than treating it as an answer.
- When the self-heal setting is on, every value naming an item that is absent
  from the item registry is re-selected on load, and the file is rewritten once
  afterwards.
- A malformed or unreadable file is logged and otherwise ignored, and the table
  is left empty. It does **not** fall through to a fresh selection: a per-tag ask
  against an unreadable file yields the empty item, so every tag-resulted recipe
  in the installation silently has no result until the file is fixed. A
  counterpart should select rather than yield nothing, and record the divergence.

This is pack-visible state a server operator edits by hand, so its shape is part
of the contract. A counterpart may choose its own file name but must keep the
flat map, the skipped comment key, the two "no representative" spellings and the
self-heal rewrite. **Host-blocked**: no public surface gives a mod arbitrary
files in the configuration directory, so a counterpart implements the selection
and leaves the file to the host.

### 5.3 The selection rule: the first member wins

**The shipped rule is that the tag's first member is chosen and the configured
namespace list is never consulted** (**read**).

A comparator over namespace positions exists and is written as though it ranked
members. It is not reached. The reduction that would apply it is folded over a
sequence holding the tag itself as a single element rather than over the tag's
members, so the sequence never has two elements to compare, the comparator is
never called, and the single element's first member is taken as the answer. The
configured list is loaded and passed in, and nothing reads it.

That is the whole rule, and it has three consequences worth stating plainly:

- The namespace setting of §3 is inert. A pack author who reorders it observes no
  change.
- The answer is whatever the tag's member order presents first. That order is
  the host's, not this entry's, and it is not a reproducible property.
- Because the answer is then written into §5.2's file and the file is consulted
  before selection, the arbitrary answer is frozen on first ask and persists.

**What a counterpart implements — and why this is a behaviour change, not a
fix.** Ascending position in the configured list, unlisted namespaces after every
listed one, ties broken by full identifier in lexicographic order. This is the
rule the setting was plainly written for, so it is a genuine improvement rather
than a divergence for its own sake; but it must be declared as a behaviour change
because it makes an inert setting live, and no parity test should be written
against the original here.

The change is narrower than it first looks, and the scope is what makes it safe
to ship (**inferred**, from the ordering in §5.1 and the persistence in §5.2):

- A tag already claimed by the unification path is unaffected; that path runs
  first and this rule is never reached for it.
- A tag already recorded in an existing installation's file is unaffected; the
  file is consulted before selection, so a recorded answer is kept whatever rule
  produced it.
- Only tags that are asked for the first time, in an installation without a
  unification mod, resolve differently — and for those the original's answer was
  arbitrary, so nothing was depending on it.

A counterpart must not make the result depend on member iteration order even
though the original does, because that order is not reproducible across runs.

## 6. Registered content

Everything the entry registers, with what a dependent can rely on (**read**):

- **One item-level data value**: a boolean, persisted with the item and sent to
  clients, recording whether a refillable liquid-carrying tool is full. Defaults
  false and is set on the tool at construction.
- **One sound event**, variable-range, played while that tool is in use.
- **One ingredient kind**, "this item, at least this many": it matches when the
  candidate's count is at least the declared count and its item is one of the
  declared alternatives. It declares itself non-simple, so the host must not
  optimise it into a plain item test. Serialised as one value plus a count.
- **One data-pack condition kind** (§4) and **five crafting recipe kinds**
  (§6.1).

### 6.1 The recipe kinds

Each is a shaped or shapeless crafting recipe with one behavioural change
(**read**):

1. **Shaped, not mirrored.** Identical to a shaped recipe except that the pattern
   is matched only in its declared orientation; the horizontal flip a shaped
   recipe normally also accepts is not accepted. This is the base for kind 4.
2. **Shaped, transferring components.** Names one input slot by index; the result
   is the declared result with that slot's item-level data applied over it.
3. **Shaped, transferring damage.** Scans the input for the first damageable
   item; with none, the recipe behaves normally. Otherwise the result takes
   either that item's whole data patch or just its damage value, per a boolean
   defaulting to "damage only". The scan follows the input's own slot order, so
   two damageable inputs make it order-sensitive (**read**); a counterpart keeps
   the scan and defines the order explicitly.
4. **Shaped, result from a tag.** A not-mirrored shaped recipe whose result is a
   tag plus a count, resolved through §5 on first ask and cached for the recipe's
   lifetime, so a mid-session mapping change does not move an already-asked
   recipe.
5. **Shapeless, result from a tag.** The same resolution, shapeless.

All five use the host's own pattern codec, with its maximum width and height
checks intact (**read**). A relaxed variant of that codec, dropping the two size
checks and keeping only "non-empty" and "every row the same width", is defined in
the source and referenced by nothing at this revision (**read**). It is recorded
here only so a reader does not mistake its presence for a capability: no
dependent can declare an oversized pattern through these kinds, and a counterpart
must not offer one.

### 6.2 Two wire-format defects

Both are **read** directly from the encode and decode paths and both must be
fixed rather than reproduced.

- **The shapeless tag recipe's encoding and decoding do not agree.** The decoder
  reads a group, an ingredient count, a category, that many ingredients, then the
  result. The encoder writes the group, the category, the ingredients with no
  preceding count, the result, and a trailing boolean nothing reads. Two peers
  exchanging this recipe over a network would desynchronise. Why that evidently
  does not bite in practice is **unknown**; the likely explanation is that the
  path is unreachable because such recipes reach both sides from the data pack,
  but that was not confirmed.
- **The two tag recipes disagree about emptiness**: one encodes an optional item
  and the other a required one, while both decode as optional. A shapeless tag
  recipe whose tag resolved to nothing therefore fails to encode.

A counterpart defines one symmetric encoding per kind — length-prefixed
ingredients, an optionally-empty result, no field written that is not read — and
records the divergence.

## 7. Persistence

The entry owns the saved shape of every inventory its dependents hold, so this is
the section a save-compatibility failure would surface in.

An inventory is a fixed number of slots holding items. Its saved form is a
compound holding (**read**):

- a list of entries, one per non-empty slot, each carrying its slot index
  alongside the item's own encoding;
- the slot count.

On load the declared count is read if present, the inventory grows to at least
that many slots and never shrinks below its current size, and each entry is
placed at its recorded index when that index is in range. An entry that fails to
decode is logged and skipped and the rest still loads (**read**). That
partial-load tolerance is required: it is the difference between a removed mod
costing one slot and costing the whole machine.

**The one deliberate divergence from the host's own encoding is the count.** The
entry substitutes its own item encoding purely to remove the host's upper bound
on a stack count, so a slot may hold far more than a normal maximum stack
(**read**). Dependents storing bulk material rely on this, and a counterpart's
saved form must round-trip counts above the normal maximum.

Related, and observable through the same inventory (**read**):

- A per-slot limit may be set, falling back to an inventory-wide default. A limit
  above the normal stack maximum must be a whole multiple of it, and one that is
  not is refused at construction with an error. That refusal is behaviour; keep it.
- Slots may be marked as output slots. Insertion into one and extraction from a
  non-output one are both refused, but only for access from outside; the owner
  bypasses both. With no slot marked, neither restriction applies.
- Separate insert and extract predicates may be supplied, consulted on outside
  access only.
- A side-aware view may wrap the inventory, one per direction, each consulting a
  direction-aware predicate before delegating. The wrapped view answers "may this
  item go here" as false when no insert predicate is set, where the unwrapped
  inventory answers true. That asymmetry is **read** and looks unintended; a
  counterpart makes both default to permitting and records the divergence.
- A deep copy is available, copying limits, predicates, output slots and items.

**Host-blocked**: Ravel hands a mod a payload for its saved data rather than a
slot-addressed compound, so a counterpart implements the envelope and the
above rules as portable logic and leaves the write to Ravel's saved-data
lifecycle. No save-compatible identifier mapping is specified by this document,
so a counterpart's alias declaration is empty.

## 8. Recipe access and dynamic recipes

Two things dependents rely on here, both delivered by intercepting the host
rather than by any public host surface (**read**).

**A globally reachable recipe view.** The entry captures the active recipe
collection as each side builds one — the server's with its reloadable resources,
the client's with its connection listener — and publishes lookup by recipe kind,
plus a read of the whole collection. There is no published lookup by recipe
identifier. The capture is held weakly, so a stale view is collected
rather than pinned, and asking before any capture is a hard error rather than an
empty answer (**read**). Keep the error: an empty answer is indistinguishable
from a pack with no such recipes.

**Recipes contributed at load.** While the host assembles its recipe collection,
and before it is frozen, the entry publishes an event carrying a list a listener
may add recipes to; everything added is inserted under its own identifier and
kind (**read**).

The ordering is the contract and RVL-321 depends on it: contributors are asked
during assembly, so their recipes are present before anything reads the
collection, and no listener sees a half-built one. A second event fires after the
collection is final and tags have been updated, for listeners that read rather
than contribute. An exception from any listener of either event is caught and
logged and assembly continues (**read**); a counterpart instead refuses the load,
because a silently dropped contribution is a world that quietly lacks content.

**Host-blocked**: Ravel publishes no recipe-assembly interception point and no
recipe view, so neither event nor the lookup can be made live by a counterpart at
the reviewed public revision.

## 9. Events published to dependents

Four, all **read**:

- **An item broke**, when damage is about to destroy it, carrying a *copy* of the
  item as it was, the damage amount, the world and the holder if any. The copy is
  the point: the real item is about to be emptied. Not cancellable, server-side
  only. **Host-blocked**: nothing public observes item destruction.
- **Recipes are being assembled** and **recipes have been assembled** (§8).
- **A crop was harvested by a sweeping harvest tool** (§11), carrying the world,
  position, block state, tool and player. **Cancellable**, and a cancel stops that
  one block without stopping the rest of the sweep.

A counterpart declares the equivalents as Ravel services or events.

## 10. Reusable items and the crafting remainder

An item may be declared *reusable* with a number of uses. Observable behaviour
(**read**):

- Fewer than one declared use means unlimited. Otherwise durability is one less
  than the declared uses and the item is not repairable.
- It always declares a crafting remainder, so crafting returns it rather than
  consuming it.
- The remainder is a single copy. Unlimited, it returns unchanged. Otherwise a
  random draw decides whether the use is charged at all: it is skipped with
  probability one minus the reciprocal of one plus the item's
  durability-preserving enchantment level. If charged, damage rises by one, and
  exceeding the maximum makes the remainder nothing — the item is consumed.
- Remaining uses shown to a player are maximum damage minus current damage plus
  one, with a distinct message at exactly one use left and a third when
  unlimited.

The plus one is load-bearing arithmetic, not an off-by-one: a fresh item has
durability one below its use count, so the displayed figure and the number of
crafts actually available agree. A counterpart reproduces the formula exactly and
takes the random draw from a caller-supplied source rather than a global one, so
it can be tested.

## 11. Derived arithmetic and base behaviours

Portable, host-free, and the natural counterpart surface (**read** unless marked):

- **Sweeping harvest.** A harvest tool with a radius sweeps the horizontal square
  of that radius around the clicked block at the same height. §9's cancellable
  event is asked first; then a mature crop is reset to age zero, its drops are
  produced with exactly one of its own seed item withheld, and the tool takes one
  damage. A mature nether-wart block is treated the same way with its own
  produce. A sound plays once if anything was harvested. The single withheld seed
  is what makes the sweep replanting rather than farming, and it is required.
- **Sweeping harvest, as a weapon.** On a full-strength attack, other living
  entities within a derived radius take two-thirds of the tool's attack damage
  plus knockback, excluding the attacker, the target and the target's allies. The
  derived radius is one at radius one and grows by a quarter per step beyond.
- **Radial mining.** Breaks the cube of the tool's radius around a mined block,
  skipping blocks it is not the correct tool for and any block whose hardness
  exceeds the mined block's by more than five. Instantly-breakable blocks charge
  one damage with probability one third rather than always. The hardness margin
  stops a tool tearing through a stronger material it happens to be valid for.
- **Combination tool.** Answers yes to three tool families' abilities, tries each
  family's interaction with the targeted block in a fixed order and stops at the
  first that applies, and carries one and a half times its material's normal
  durability.
- **Refillable liquid tool.** Filled by using it on a source of the liquid, then
  used by holding, which repeatedly wets farmland in a cube around the aimed
  block to maximum wetness and, with a declared probability, advances one random
  growth step on every growable block in that cube. A cooldown throttles repeats.
  The cube's half-extent is the declared side length minus one, halved and
  truncated, so a declared side of one is a single block and an even side behaves
  as the odd number below it. That truncation is **read** and is stated because
  computing the half-extent the obvious way gives a different area.
- **Stack arithmetic.** Growing, shrinking and combining stacks, with shrinking to
  zero optionally yielding the crafting remainder instead of nothing, and an
  insert returning both the merged stack and what would not fit. Two stacks
  combine only on same item, same data, and a sum within the maximum.
- **Colour arithmetic.** Channel packing, interpolation through a
  hue-saturation-brightness space, saturation scaling and opacity substitution.
  One helper computing an opacity from a ratio truncates to an integer before
  multiplying, so it can only return zero or the maximum (**read**); that is a
  defect and a counterpart computes it in floating point.
- **Multiblock offsets** resolved against an origin, and a numeric view over
  getter and setter pairs presented as an indexed array.

## 12. Client presentation

Two tooltip additions, each gated on its own setting and on the player having
advanced tooltips on (**read**): every tag the item, its block form and its
liquid form belong to, under three headings behind one modifier key, with the
liquid tags sorted by identifier and the other two left in host order; and every
item-level data value, truncated to fifty characters, behind another modifier
key. Client-only besides: a field-of-view reduction while a custom drawn weapon
is drawn, scaled by that weapon's draw speed.

None of it is specified further. Drawing, key choices, layout and translation
text are presentation. The one point worth carrying is that both additions are
diagnostic aids gated behind an existing diagnostic mode, not decoration.

---

## 13. The command

One command, rooted at the mod's own name, with one subcommand in two forms, both
requiring the highest operator permission: fill to capacity the energy store of
the block the invoker is looking at, or of the item in their main hand
(**read**). It reports a translated success or failure line and returns zero in
every case alike. A counterpart returns a meaningful result and records the
divergence. Command declaration and dispatch are Ravel facilities; the energy
store is **host-blocked**.

## 14. What dependents need from this entry

RVL-321 and RVL-002 both list this entry as a dependency, and RVL-086 shares its
cluster (**read**, from the plan rows). What they actually consume:

- **RVL-321** needs §8's recipe contribution and its ordering guarantee, §7's
  inventory envelope including the above-maximum counts, §5's tag resolution for
  its data-declared recipes, §4's flags for its optional content, and §11's
  sweeping-harvest and stack arithmetic. Its registry, tag and networking tests
  all sit downstream of §6 and §8.
- **RVL-002** reaches this entry both directly and through RVL-321. Directly it
  needs §6's recipe kinds, §5's tag selection for the results those recipes name,
  §4's flags for its optional content, and the derived arithmetic its own counts
  are written against. It does **not** need §7's persistence envelope: it owns no
  block entity and persists nothing of its own, as RVL-002 §16.4 and §17 record
  (**read**). RVL-321 is the dependent that needs the envelope.
- Both need §2's ordering: these registrations committed before a dependent's
  registration window, and these flags published before a dependent's data is
  conditioned on them.

A counterpart that delivers §4, §5, §7, §10 and §11 as portable logic, and
records §6, §8, §9 and §13 as host-blocked, satisfies what these dependents can
observe today.

## 15. What this specification does not specify

- **Thin base types over host types**: base blocks, items, tools, armour, fluid
  holders, block entities, menus and slot kinds. Read; each adds at most a
  constructor convenience or one small override, and every override carrying
  behaviour is named above. Nothing else in them is specified.
- **The client widget and screen set**: three meters, four button kinds, a
  ghost-item renderer, a render-type holder and the sound instance behind §11's
  refillable tool. Listed, not specified.
- **The translation strings and the eight shipped translations**, read as
  existing; their text is upstream material and is not transcribed. The sound file
  and the two interface textures were fetched with the source, were not read as
  behaviour, and nothing is shipped from them.
- **The interception points behind §8 and §9** as mechanism. Only the resulting
  ordering is described, because the ordering is the contract and the injection is
  the original's structure.
- **A crop's seed lookup**, which reaches a host method reflectively. §11 says
  "the crop's own seed item"; obtaining it is a host concern.
- **Whether the unreachable network path of §6.2 is truly unreachable**, marked
  unknown. It matters: if it is reachable, a counterpart that fixes the encoding
  is incompatible with an unfixed peer, and the wave's networking probe is where
  that surfaces.
- ~~Whether the shipped namespace list was tuned around the §5.3 rule.~~
  **Resolved, not unknown**: it cannot have been, because nothing reads the list
  (§5.3). No pack can have tuned against a setting with no effect, which is why
  §5.3's proposed rule is safe to ship as the behaviour change it is.
- **Coverage.** Read in full: configuration; the flag mechanism and its
  condition; the tag-representative selection, its file and its fallbacks; all
  five recipe kinds and both serialisation directions of each; the ingredient
  kind; the persistence envelope and every inventory rule in §7; the recipe view
  and both load events; all four published events; the reusable-item rule; the
  sweeping, radial, combination and refillable tool arithmetic; the stack and
  colour arithmetic; the command. Read in part: the client tooltip handlers, for
  their gating and content but not their layout. Read as existing only: the
  widget and screen set, the base types, the translations and the binary assets.
  Not read: the build configuration and the tag data files beyond their names.
