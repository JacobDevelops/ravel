# RVL-060 — behaviour specification

Wave: `waves/m6-foundation.json`. Method: source-informed reimplementation.
Rights: the upstream project's repository root carries an MIT licence file, read
at the source; see `provenance/m6-foundation/RVL-060.json` for the pinned
revision, the licence identity and the digest.

Confidence markers: **read**, **inferred**, **unknown**.

This entry is a foundation seed: the wave admits it with no dependency of its
own, and two pack entries load only if it does (**read**, from the wave manifest
and the plan row). It is a toolbox rather than a mod. It contributes no block, no
item, no recipe, no tag and no loot to any world, and a player never encounters
it except through one client command. What its dependents observe is a set of
facilities: a content declaration surface, a networking channel builder, two saved-data
envelopes, a family of data-driven predicates and tag validators, a
codec-described recipe and ingredient shape, a menu payload shape, a fluid
registration path, and a client presentation toolkit. Sections 4 to 12 are those
facilities and are the load-bearing part of this document.

---

## 1. What this entry is

A shared library with no gameplay of its own. Its whole purpose is to be
depended on. The pinned tree holds three modules: a portable one, a
loader-specific one that the pinned artifact is built from, and a second
loader-specific one that the pinned artifact does not contain (**read**). Only
the portable module and the first loader module are in scope; the second is
named here only so a later reader does not look for its behaviour here. How many
files each holds is the original's layout and says nothing about what this entry
does; §15 records the counts as coverage of what was read.

The tree carries four resource documents and no binary asset the game loads: no
texture, no sound, no model, no image source (**read**, counted over the whole
tree twice). One binary build-tool archive sits in the build wrapper directory
and is not shipped.

It declares each of its two dependencies, the loader and the game, required on
both sides, and declares no dependency on any other pack entry (**read**, from
the mod descriptor and confirmed against the plan row). The entry itself carries
no side declaration.

### 1.1 Ravel disposition

Ravel already owns most of what this entry exists to provide. Content
declaration and registration, side isolation, saved-data ownership, networking
channels and configuration resolution are public Ravel facilities, and a
counterpart expresses the behaviour below through them rather than rebuilding
them. What survives as counterpart work is the portable decision logic: the
predicate and validator semantics of §7, the payload shapes of §8, the split
integer transport of §6.3, and the colour arithmetic of §11.

Several areas are host-blocked at the reviewed public revision rather than
declined: they need a surface Ravel does not publish. Each is named where it
occurs. Two areas are **declined** rather than blocked, meaning a counterpart
should not implement them at any Ravel revision; §12 says which and why.

## 2. Side, lifecycle and what happens when

The entry runs on both sides and its behaviour splits sharply (**read**):

- At construction, before anything else, it prepares two directories outside the
  game directory and writes a fixed explanatory text file into each if absent
  (§6.1). This happens on every side, including a dedicated server.
- At construction it also installs a side-appropriate accessor for the game's
  registry set: on a client build, one that reads the client's connection to a
  server; otherwise one that reads the running server. Asking either before the
  respective thing exists is a hard failure rather than an empty answer
  (**read**).
- On a client build only, and only there, it loads the resource-pack
  declarations of §10.1, observes block highlight rendering, registers one client
  command, and registers one client reload listener.
- Networking channels declared by dependents are collected at construction time
  into a process-wide list and registered together when the loader asks for
  payload handlers (§5).

**The side split has a defect that a counterpart must not copy.** The
resource-pack declaration path of §10.1 is capable of contributing both
client-facing and server-facing packs, and its pack-finder listener asks the
event which kind is wanted. But both the scan that reads the declarations and
the registration of that listener happen only on the client branch (**read**).
On a dedicated server no declared pack is contributed at all, so a dependent that
ships data through this mechanism silently has no data there. A counterpart
registers the contribution on both sides and records the divergence.

In Ravel terms: the directory preparation and the registry accessor are
`Mod` construction; the client-only observers are declared client-only rather
than guarded by a runtime side test; and the channel collection is a
`ContentDeclaration` claimed through `Registrar` rather than a mutable static
list.

## 3. What dependents need

The pack graph records two mods depending on this entry, both requiring it on
both sides with the same minimum version, and both classified as resolved edges
(**read**, counted from the graph twice). Both are decoration-and-building mods
whose own plan rows put them in client presentation with a black-box method
(RVL-130 and RVL-288 in `inventory/atm10-tts-2.0.2/plans.json`). Neither is
admitted to this wave.

That shapes what parity means here. The edges are both required-on-both-sides,
so a counterpart must satisfy the *load* contract on both sides even where the
capability behind it is client-only. Concretely, the capabilities those two
edges imply, in the order a dependent reaches for them:

- **Registration.** Both dependents register large numbers of blocks and items.
  §4 is the surface they hold, and its observable contract is the deferred entry
  handle, not the underlying mechanism.
- **Client presentation.** Screen scaffolding, cursor shapes, a scissor stack, a
  memory that restores a screen and the block-highlight system of §10 are what a
  decoration mod uses. §10 is therefore the section a visual regression would
  surface in.
- **Colour.** §11's colour value and its two encodings are used pervasively by
  presentation code and appear in saved and networked data.
- **A resource-pack contribution point.** §10.1 is how a decoration mod ships an
  optional alternative texture set. The side defect of §2 bites here.

Neither dependent's own behaviour was read; these are the capabilities the edges
imply, not an audit of what each one calls (**inferred**). A counterpart that
implements §4 to §11 satisfies the edge; a counterpart that implements only the
portable half does not, because both edges are declared on both sides.

## 4. Declaring content

A dependent declares content by asking a namespace-scoped collection for an
entry: it supplies a path string and a factory, and receives back a handle
(**read**). The observable contract of a handle is that it yields the registered
object and its full identifier, and that asking for the object before
registration has run is an error rather than a null (**read**).

The original spells more than one handle class apart by what each one yields.
That is the original's class hierarchy; the observable consequence is none,
because the contract above is the same for every one of them and one of the
distinctions is drawn by a host class a counterpart does not have. A counterpart
declares one handle and does not port the shape (**read**).

Rules a dependent can rely on (**read**):

- A collection can be *nested* under a parent. A nested collection forwards every
  declaration to its parent and additionally remembers it, so a dependent can ask
  "everything I declared in this group" without a second bookkeeping structure. A
  nested collection's own initialisation is deliberately inert; only the root
  initialises.
- A further nesting keys those groups by an enumeration value, so a dependent can
  ask for the declarations belonging to one variant.
- Initialisation is explicit and per-collection. Nothing is registered until the
  owning collection is initialised against the loader's event bus.
- One extension point exists for registries the loader models specially rather
  than as a plain registry. Exactly one such kind is defined at this revision,
  for fluids (§9), and asking for any other kind is refused with an error rather
  than silently returning a plain collection (**read**). Keep the refusal: it is
  the observable behaviour here, and a refusal needs something to accept. How
  many kinds the original happens to define is the original's structure, and a
  counterpart adds a second only when Ravel gives it one to model, never on the
  original's authority.

**Host-blocked in one narrow place.** The grouped-by-enumeration accessor
returns the group for a value that has had at least one declaration and null
otherwise, with no empty-collection fallback (**read**). A counterpart returns an
empty group instead, which is a behaviour change small enough to make without
argument, and records it.

A counterpart expresses all of §4 as Ravel content declaration. The nesting and
grouping are the only parts with behaviour of their own; the handles are Ravel's.

## 5. Networking

A dependent creates a channel from a base identifier and an integer protocol
version, optionally marking the channel optional (**read**).

**Two derived identifiers are the wire contract and are required rather than
incidental** (**read**):

- The channel's effective identifier is the base identifier with a
  version-derived suffix appended. That same suffixed string is what the channel
  is registered under with the loader.
- Each packet kind's payload identifier is the effective channel identifier with
  a further suffix appended, built from the packet's own identifier.

Both spellings are compatibility-required: two peers agree on a packet only if
they derive the same string. Neither derivation is transcribed here; both are
obtainable from the pinned artifact recorded in
`provenance/m6-foundation/RVL-060.json`.

Behaviour a dependent relies on (**read**):

- A packet kind is declared as clientbound or serverbound, never both. A
  clientbound kind's handler yields a deferred action; a serverbound kind's
  handler yields an action taking the sending player. Declaring the direction is
  what decides which registration the loader gets.
- Three packet-kind shapes are offered: one carrying no data at all, whose decode
  simply constructs a fresh instance; one described by a codec; and one described
  by the game's own stream codec. The dataless shape writes nothing and reads
  nothing, which is symmetric and safe.
- An optional channel refuses to send to a player whose connection does not carry
  it, silently. A non-optional channel does not check. The check is
  channel-level, not packet-level (**read**, and the source says so).
- Convenience sends exist for one player, a collection, every player on the
  server, every player in a world, every player tracking a position's chunk, and
  every player within a distance of a position.

**One send helper is wrong and must be fixed rather than reproduced.** The
distance-limited send compares a *squared* distance against the caller's
unsquared distance argument (**read**). A caller asking for sixteen blocks
reaches players within four. Every existing caller has silently been compensating
or getting a smaller radius than it asked for; which is **unknown**, because no
caller was read. A counterpart defines the argument as a plain distance, compares
against its square, and records the divergence.

**Host-blocked**: the process-wide list that collects channels so they can all be
registered at one loader event has no Ravel equivalent, and needs none. A
counterpart declares each channel through Ravel's own networking declaration and
drops the list.

## 6. Persistence and storage

Three distinct things live here and a save-compatibility failure would surface in
the first two.

### 6.1 Two directories outside the game directory

At construction the entry computes a cache directory and a data directory keyed
to the host operating system, creates both, and writes a fixed multi-line
explanatory text file named for a readme into each if one is not already there
(**read**). It then hands any dependent a subdirectory of either, named for that
dependent, and does no file work beyond that: creating and reading the
subdirectory is the dependent's job (**read**, and the source says so).

The placement rule is three-branched (**read**): on Windows, a dotted directory
under the machine-local application-data location, with cache and data as two
children; on macOS, the user's caches folder and the user's application-support
folder; otherwise the freedesktop cache and data locations if the environment
declares them, falling back to the conventional dot-cache and share directories
under the user's home. A failure to create either is logged as a warning and
otherwise ignored, so the paths are handed out whether or not they exist.

**This is state outside the world and outside the game directory.** It survives
world deletion, is shared between every instance the user runs, and on a shared
machine is per-user rather than per-installation. That is the behaviour, not an
accident: the whole point is a cache that outlives an instance.

**Declined for Ravel, not host-blocked.** Ravel gives a mod its own storage
through the host, and a Ravel package writing into the user's home directory on
its own initiative is outside what Ravel's sandbox should permit. A counterpart
exposes "a durable per-package directory" and lets Ravel decide where it is. Any
dependent that had recorded an absolute path would break, and none is known to
(**unknown** — no dependent was read).

### 6.2 A codec-described world save

A dependent describes its saved state with a codec and a storage name and
receives a per-world handle (**read**). Observable rules:

- The handle carries a default value used when nothing is stored and when parsing
  fails. A parse failure is logged and falls back to the default; it does not
  refuse the load (**read**). A counterpart keeps the fallback, because refusing
  would cost a world its state, but must make the failure loud.
- Writing merges the encoded compound into whatever the host handed it. An
  opt-in mode clears every existing key first, so a shrinking record does not
  leave stale keys behind. Without that mode, a removed field persists forever
  (**read**). A counterpart makes clearing the default and records the
  divergence: the non-clearing behaviour is a data-rot source with no upside.
- An opt-in mode marks the record permanently dirty so it is written on every
  save regardless of change tracking.
- An opt-in mode stores the record against the primary dimension rather than the
  current one, giving a single world-wide record.
- If the codec produces something that is not a compound, the write is logged and
  silently dropped (**read**). A counterpart refuses at declaration time instead,
  since a codec's output shape is knowable before any world exists.

A second, older envelope also exists: a dependent implements read and write over
the host's own tag type directly, and optionally supplies a client-side stand-in
returned when the request comes from a world that is not a server world
(**read**). The stand-in is a single shared instance, not a copy, so two client
worlds share it. That envelope carries a source comment saying its data-fixer
argument exists only because one loader rejects the absent one; it is inert
(**read**).

**Host-blocked**: Ravel hands a package a payload for its saved data rather than
a keyed compound, so a counterpart implements both envelopes as portable logic
over a Ravel payload and leaves the write to Ravel's saved-data lifecycle. No
save-compatible identifier mapping is specified by this document, so a
counterpart's alias declaration is empty.

### 6.3 Wide values through a narrow synchronisation channel

The host's screen-data synchronisation truncates each value to sixteen bits. The
entry works around this by storing each logical value as two halves and
recomposing them on read: a write shifts the value right by sixteen and stores
that as the first half, then stores the low sixteen bits as the second; a read
shifts the first half left by sixteen and combines it with the masked second
(**read**). The underlying array is allocated at twice the declared size, and the
declared size is what the dependent indexes against.

This is genuinely the behaviour rather than an implementation detail: it
round-trips the full signed thirty-two-bit range through a channel that otherwise
loses everything outside sixteen bits, and a dependent showing a large stored
quantity in a screen depends on it. A counterpart reproduces the arithmetic
exactly, including the sign handling, and keeps the doubled backing size hidden
from the index arithmetic.

The narrow accessors that the host itself calls remain available and are marked
as compatibility-only in the source (**read**). A counterpart keeps both, because
the host still calls the narrow pair.

## 7. Data-driven predicates and tag validators

This is the largest portable surface and the one most exposed to pack authors.

### 7.1 Block state property matching

A block state predicate is a map from property name to a matcher (**read**). It
holds when every named property that the state definition actually declares
satisfies its matcher; a named property the definition does not declare fails the
whole predicate rather than being ignored (**read**).

Two matcher kinds exist (**read**):

- **Exact.** A single value spelled as text, parsed by the property's own parser.
  It holds when the state's current value compares equal to the parsed one. An
  unparseable spelling never holds.
- **Range.** An optional lower bound and an optional upper bound, each spelled as
  text and parsed by the property's own parser. Present-and-unparseable fails.
  Present-and-parseable fails when the current value orders below the lower bound
  or above the upper. Both bounds are therefore inclusive, and an absent bound is
  unbounded on that side.

The two are distinguished on decode by shape rather than by a kind field: a bare
text value is exact, an object is a range (**read**). That is a wire contract and
a counterpart preserves it.

A third operation runs the predicate backwards, constructing a representative
state from the matchers, for a caller that needs an example rather than a test.
Exact contributes its value; **range picks one of its two bounds at random when
both are present**, and its single bound when only one is (**read**). A
counterpart keeps the randomness and keeps the caller-supplied random source, so
a construction is reproducible.

### 7.2 Three composite predicates

Three predicates combine a registry identity with optional extra tests
(**read**). Each also exposes its stored tag for a caller that wants to inspect
rather than test.

- **Block.** A required block identity, an optional stored-data test, an optional
  location test, and a state predicate defaulting to "anything". It holds when
  the state at a position is that block, the state predicate holds, the location
  test holds, and, when a stored-data test is present, a block entity exists there
  and its fully-saved form satisfies it.
- **Entity.** A required entity kind and five optional tests: location, status
  effects, stored data, flags, and a test applied to the entity's current target.
  The target test is applied with a null target for a non-targeting entity, which
  the host's own predicate treats as a normal absent value (**inferred** — the
  source passes null and does not say what that means).
- **Item.** A required item identity, an optional stored-data test, a durability
  bound and a count bound. **This one is broken.** The identity check returns
  false when the stack *is* the declared item (**read**), so the predicate can
  only ever hold for a stack that is not what it names, and then only if every
  other test passes. A counterpart inverts it to the obvious meaning and records
  the divergence; no parity test should be written against the original here.

A second item-level defect sits behind the first and would be invisible until the
first is fixed: a durability bound that is anything other than "any" causes the
predicate to fail outright for a non-damageable item, and the durability value
compared is the *remaining* durability rather than the damage taken (**read**).
Both are defensible as intended; they are recorded so a counterpart chooses them
deliberately.

### 7.3 Stored-data comparison

A separate stored-data predicate compares an expected tag against a candidate,
with a strict mode delegating to the host's own comparison and a lenient mode of
the entry's own (**read**). The lenient rules are the behaviour:

- A null expectation matches anything, including a null candidate.
- A null candidate against a non-null expectation fails.
- Two numeric tags compare by decimal value, so a byte and a long holding the
  same number match. This is deliberate and is the point of the lenient mode.
- Two tags of different concrete kinds, both non-numeric, fail.
- A compound holds when every key it names is present in the candidate and
  matches. Extra keys in the candidate are ignored.
- A list holds when every one of its elements matches *some* element of the
  candidate, in any order and with repeats allowed. An empty expected list
  requires an empty candidate.
- Anything else compares by equality.
- Empty means null, an empty compound, or an empty list, and an empty expectation
  matches only an empty candidate.

These rules are a wire-visible contract because pack authors write against them,
and a counterpart reproduces them exactly, including the order-insensitive list
rule and the numeric widening.

### 7.4 The tag validator vocabulary

Eleven validator kinds in four families, counted from the tree twice (**read**):
five over text, two over lists, two over numbers, and two over compounds. Each
family has a default kind that decodes from an object carrying one named field
and no kind field, and every kind additionally decodes from an object naming its
kind (**read**).

| family | kinds | what the default kind does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| text | 5 | equality |
| list | 2 | positional, element for element |
| number | 2 | equality |
| compound | 2 | a subset of named keys |

The kinds, named for what they do:

- **Text**: exact equality, substring containment, prefix, suffix, and a regular
  expression that must match the whole value.
- **List**: an ordered exact match, where the candidate must have the same length
  and each element must satisfy the validator at the same position; and a
  universal match, where every element must satisfy one validator. Both reject an
  empty candidate outright (**read**), which is surprising and is behaviour a
  counterpart keeps, because a pack author testing "the list is empty" would
  otherwise have written the ordered kind with no validators and expected it to
  hold.
- **Number**: exact equality compared at the candidate's own width, and a bounded
  range with optional ends.
- **Compound**: a partial match, where every named key must be present and
  satisfy its validator and extra keys are allowed; and an exact match, which
  additionally requires that the candidate name no key the validator does not.

Every kind first checks that the candidate tag belongs to its family and fails if
not, so a text validator against a number is false rather than an error
(**read**). The list family deliberately admits the three primitive array kinds
as well as the general list kind (**read**).

**Two defects here must be fixed rather than reproduced.**

- The bounded number range, applied to a floating-point candidate with no lower
  bound given, substitutes the smallest *positive* representable value as the
  lower bound rather than negative infinity (**read**). Every negative value and
  zero therefore fail a range that declared only an upper bound. The integral
  widths do not have this problem because their smallest values are genuinely
  their minima. A counterpart uses negative infinity.
- Decoding a validator with no family known from context tries the four families
  in a fixed order and takes the first that parses (**read**). Each family's
  default kind is gated by its own distinct field name, a much stronger
  separation than a first-parse order suggests. Which orderings actually collide
  is therefore **unknown**; the collision may be unreachable, and it was not
  tested. A counterpart requires the family to be named wherever context does not
  supply it, and records the divergence.

**Host-blocked**: Ravel publishes no data-pack document loading a counterpart
could hang §7.1 to §7.4 on, so a counterpart implements them as portable logic
over Ravel's own declared data types and cannot make them live at the reviewed
public revision.

## 8. Recipes, ingredients and menu payloads

Three related shapes, all thin over the host and all serialisation contracts
(**read**).

**A codec-described recipe.** A dependent supplies a recipe kind, a document
codec and a network codec. The base shape declares itself special, so the
crafting book does not attempt to show it; assembling yields the empty item and
the result item is empty; and it accepts any grid size. That is deliberate: this
shape is for recipes that are consumed by machinery rather than by a crafting
grid, and a dependent that wants a crafting-grid recipe overrides all four
(**read**).

**A codec-described ingredient.** A dependent supplies an identifier, a document
codec and a network codec, and implements the test, the list of example stacks it
matches, and whether it is *complex*, meaning its answer depends on more than the
candidate's item. Complex defaults to true, and the loader is told the inverse:
a complex ingredient must not be optimised into a plain item test (**read**).
Keep the default and keep the inversion, because getting it wrong is a
correctness bug that only shows under a pack that uses the optimisation.

Registration of ingredient kinds is namespace-scoped: the first ingredient
registered for a namespace creates that namespace's collection lazily, and every
later one joins it (**read**). The lazy map is not synchronised and is written
during load; whether load is single-threaded here is **unknown**.

**A menu payload.** A dependent's screen may carry an opening payload described
by a codec. The server writes it when opening; the client decodes it into an
optional. Decoding may yield nothing, and the screen must handle that: an absent
payload is the declared state when no serialiser was supplied at all (**read**).
The provider also decides whether opening resets the pointer position, defaulting
to yes.

**Host-blocked**: Ravel publishes no recipe kind registration, no custom
ingredient kind and no screen-opening payload, so none of §8 can be made live by
a counterpart at the reviewed public revision. A counterpart records the shapes
and implements the portable halves: the complexity flag's meaning, the empty
defaults, and the optional payload.

## 9. Fluids

The entry offers a fluid registration path behind the one extension point of §4
(**read**). A dependent names a fluid and supplies a property record; the entry
creates the loader's fluid kind from it and returns a handle.

The property record is a flat carrier of the host's own fluid attributes:
conversion, drowning, extinguishing, hydration, entity pushing, swimming,
density, fall-distance scaling, light level, motion scale, path types, rarity,
temperature and viscosity, plus a map from a named sound action to a sound
(**read**). Its display key is derived from the fluid's identifier by the host's
own rule.

A companion client-side property record supplies textures, an overlay, a tint
colour, an optional custom render, a fog colour modification and a fog range
modification. It is looked up lazily by identifier the first time the client
needs it and then cached per fluid kind (**read**). The lookup happening lazily
matters: a client property record registered after the first render is never
seen.

The loader's fluid-kind accessor is available on the entry's own fluid base
class, cached on first ask and raising a hard error if the data is absent
(**read**). The original supplies it by injecting into that class; that is the
original's structure, and the observable consequence is only the caching and the
hard error. Do not port the shape.

The whole area is marked experimental in the source (**read**).

**Host-blocked**: Ravel publishes no fluid registration and no client fluid
rendering surface. A counterpart records the property vocabulary as a declared
shape and implements nothing live.

## 10. The client presentation surface

A decoration dependent (§3) is the main consumer. The observable pieces:

- **A scissor stack.** Clip rectangles nest, each new one intersected with the
  one below, and popping restores the previous. A closeable wrapper ties a clip
  to a lexical scope. A counterpart reproduces the intersection rule; the scoping
  is language-specific.
- **A pose scope.** The same shape for the transform stack.
- **A cursor system.** A screen declares which pointer shape applies over a given
  region, and the shape is resolved per frame from the region under the pointer.
- **A screen history.** A screen may record where it came from and offer a way
  back, and a priority rule decides which of two competing screens is shown.
- **A memory that restores a screen.** A process-wide map from a screen
  identifier to a state object, so reopening a screen restores what it looked
  like. The accessor takes
  the expected state kind and replaces a state of the wrong kind rather than
  failing (**read**). The map is never cleared, including on disconnect, so state
  persists across joining a different world (**read**). A counterpart clears it
  on disconnect and records the divergence, because carrying one world's screen
  state into another is a bug rather than a feature.
- **Widgets.** An image-backed button, a selectable variant, a parent that
  forwards events to children, and a scrolling selection list.
- **System information.** A builder collecting categorised facts about the
  running machine, with four built-in categories covering the operating system,
  the Java runtime, the game, and the launcher (**read**, counted twice), and a
  registration point for a dependent to add its own. §12 says what a counterpart
  does with it.
- **Block highlights** (§10.2).

### 10.1 Declared resource packs

A dependent may declare, in its own mod descriptor, one or more resource packs
that ship inside its own file. A declaration is either a bare name, or a record
carrying a name, an optional description and a required flag (**read**). At
registration each declared pack is looked for at a fixed path inside the
declaring file, built from a fixed directory name and the declared name; a
declaration whose directory does not contain the requested pack kind is skipped
silently.

The pack's own identity is the declaring mod's namespace and the declared name.
Its description falls back to a translation key derived from the declared name,
with the name itself as the fallback text. A required pack is contributed as
hidden and always-on, with synthesised metadata rather than the pack's own; a
non-required one reads its own metadata and falls back to synthesised metadata if
absent (**read**).

The descriptor key that carries these declarations is compatibility-required,
because a dependent writes it into its own descriptor. Its exact spelling is
obtainable from the pinned artifact and is not transcribed here; a counterpart
that wants to accept existing dependents' descriptors must read it there.

Two failure paths are wrong and a counterpart fixes both: the outer scan logs the
failure with its cause but continues to the next dependent, which is right; the
inner registration catches its failure, discards the cause, and logs a message
that does not say what went wrong (**read**).

And the whole mechanism is client-only, which §2 says is a defect.

### 10.2 Block highlights

A client-side data-driven system that replaces the outline drawn around a
targeted block (**read**). Documents are loaded from a fixed directory beneath
the resource root, named for the entry itself; the directory name is
compatibility-required and obtainable from the pinned artifact.

Two document shapes share the directory and are told apart by whether the
document names a line list (**read**). One shape defines a named reusable outline
as a list of line segments, each with a start, an end and a normal. The other
maps a block's states to outlines, either inline or by naming one of the first
shape.

On load, both shapes are read, every block-state-to-outline mapping is flattened
into a flat array of nine floats per line segment, and identical outlines share
one array (**read**). The named-outline table is then discarded, so a name is
resolvable only during the load that defined it (**read**).

At render time, a block whose own class declares an outline wins outright and
nothing else is consulted; otherwise a flattened entry for the exact state is
used if one exists; otherwise the host draws its usual outline. Colour and
opacity are fixed process-wide values, and the opacity's shipped default is the
only one of the four that is not zero (**read**). A flat array whose length is
not a multiple of nine is skipped silently rather than drawn partially, which is
right and a counterpart keeps it.

The lookup is by exact state identity, not by block, and uses an identity-keyed
map (**read**). Two structurally equal states from different definitions do not
share an entry. That is fine for the host's interned states and would be a bug
against any state a counterpart constructed itself; a counterpart keys by the
state's own identity and says so.

**Host-blocked**: Ravel publishes no block-outline interception point and no
client reload listener, so §10.2 cannot be made live. Ravel-owned screens are
drawn in Java, so the widget and cursor material in §10 is declared rather than
implemented in a counterpart package.

## 11. Colour

A colour is a red, green, blue and alpha quartet with a packed integer form, and
the packing is alpha in the top eight bits, then red, then green, then blue
(**read**). That layout is a wire contract: it appears in saved data and in
network payloads through the two encodings below.

Observable rules (**read**):

- A colour may be *named*. Named colours live in one process-wide table keyed by
  the lowercased name. Registering a plain named colour does not replace an
  existing entry under that name; registering an animated one returns the
  existing entry under that name rather than replacing it. Both are
  first-registration-wins, by two different mechanisms.
- One colour is the *default*, a fully transparent white flagged as such; and
  one is animated, cycling the hue by a fixed step.
- **The two encodings disagree in shape and both must be preserved.** The
  document encoding is a text spelling parsed permissively. The network encoding
  is a leading discriminator byte selecting one of three bodies: nothing at all
  for the default colour, a text spelling for a named colour, and the packed
  integer otherwise. A counterpart reproduces the discriminator and its three
  arms exactly, because a peer decodes by it.
- An animated colour mutates itself on a background timer (§12). Its packed value
  therefore differs between two reads, and encoding one over the network sends
  its name rather than its current value, so the receiver animates independently
  (**inferred**, from the encoding's named arm; the source does not state it).

A counterpart implements the packing, the parsing and the three-arm encoding as
portable arithmetic. The animation is §12.

## 12. What a counterpart declines outright

Three things in the pinned tree are not host-blocked. They are available in
principle and a counterpart should not implement them.

**Direct memory and reflection access.** The tree carries a helper wrapping the
Java runtime's unsupported low-level memory interface, obtained by making a
private field accessible, and offering arbitrary instance and static field writes
(**read**). A second helper builds on it to add values to an enumeration at
runtime by writing the class's hidden constant array and clearing two cached
fields (**read**); the source marks it obsolete. Ravel forbids unsafe code in
every maintained crate with no exception, and this is exactly the category the
rule exists for. A counterpart implements neither, and a dependent needing an
extensible enumeration is a Ravel feature request rather than a package's
problem.

**Outbound network requests.** The tree carries a synchronous fetch helper with a
ten-second connect timeout, a fixed user-agent string, redirect following, an
optional success-status filter and a convenience that parses the body as a
document (**read**). It swallows every failure into an absent result. A library
that any dependent can use to reach an arbitrary address from the game process is
a capability decision, not a utility, and it belongs to Ravel's own permission
surface rather than to a package. A counterpart does not ship it.

**A background timer pool.** The tree creates a ten-thread scheduler at class
load, names its threads by an incrementing counter, and offers one-shot and
fixed-rate scheduling (**read**). §11's animated colours run on it, mutating
shared state that the render thread reads without synchronisation. The pool is
never shut down. A counterpart drives animation from the frame it is drawn in,
which removes both the pool and the data race, and records the divergence.

The client command of §2 is a fourth candidate and is kept rather than declined:
it gathers the four categories of §10 into a formatted block and puts it on the
clipboard for a user to paste into a support channel. It reads only what the game
already knows about its own machine and sends nothing. A counterpart keeps it and
must keep it explicitly user-invoked.

## 15. What this does not specify, and how much was read

**Coverage.** Every source file in the two in-scope modules was listed and
counted, and the counts were taken twice. Two hundred and seventeen source files
sit across the three modules: a hundred and sixty-seven portable, twenty-one in
the loader module the pinned artifact is built from, and twenty-nine in the one
it does not contain. Forty of the portable files are client presentation. Of a
hundred and eighty-eight in-scope files, roughly ninety were read in full: the whole of the registration,
networking, persistence, predicate, validator, recipe, ingredient, menu and
registry material, the whole of the loader-specific module, and the entry points.
The client presentation material was read selectively: the highlight loader, the
memory that restores a screen and the system-information builder in full, and the
widget, cursor, scissor and previous-screen material by shape only. The colour
arithmetic
was read for its packing, its naming table and its two encodings, and not for its
conversion helpers. The twenty-nine files of the module the pinned artifact does
not contain were not read at all.

**Not specified here:**

- Any dependent's behaviour. §3 states what the two graph edges imply, not what
  either dependent calls. Neither dependent's source was read.
- The widget layout arithmetic, the selection list's scrolling, and the cursor
  shape catalogue. A counterpart drawing Ravel-owned screens in Java does not
  need them, and a parity test over them would be testing pixels.
- The codec and network-serialisation helper library, which is a separate
  dependency this entry consumes rather than defines. Its behaviour is that
  dependency's, and this document treats it as given.
- The document-format layer built over an external compact binary notation. It is
  consumed the same way and was read only far enough to confirm it is not this
  entry's own definition.
- Any save-compatible identifier mapping. The names surface is unresolved in the
  provenance record, so a counterpart's alias declaration is empty and no
  existing world is loadable by it.
- Whether the validator family collision of §7.4 is actually reachable, and
  whether the ingredient registration map of §8 is ever written concurrently.
  Both are marked **unknown** above and both need a test rather than a reading.

**One reader.** This document was written by a single reader in one pass. Nothing
contradicted them. The read/inferred/unknown markers and this section stand in
for the second reader and are weaker than one.
