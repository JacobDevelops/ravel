# RVL-086 — behaviour specification

Wave: `waves/m6-foundation.json`. Method: source-informed reimplementation.
Rights: the upstream project's repository root carries an MIT licence file, read
at the source; see `provenance/m6-foundation/RVL-086.json` for the pinned
revision, the licence identity and the digest.

Confidence markers: **read**, **inferred**, **unknown**.

This entry is the client-presentation cluster's **leaf**: nothing in the wave
depends on it, and it depends on RVL-042 (**read**, from the plan row and from
its own descriptor, which declares that dependency mandatory on both sides). The
wave admits it under cluster closure for that reason. Because it is a leaf, the
useful question about it is not what it publishes but what it *consumes* and what
it *leaves behind in a world* — a fresh play, a save, an exit and a reload have
to find the same state. §9 and §10 are where that is decided, and §6 is where
most of the counterpart work is.

---

## 1. What this entry is

A collection of small, mutually independent additions and behaviour changes to
the base game. There is no central mechanic: it is fifteen or so separate
features, each one individually switchable, sharing only a configuration file, a
creative grouping and a dependency on the shared library of RVL-042. Three of
them change how *existing* game content behaves rather than adding anything —
those are the interesting ones, because they are the only parts a counterpart
cannot express as content declaration.

It is small: about forty-three source files, of which roughly a quarter are
client presentation, plus about three hundred declarative documents that are
almost entirely one-per-registered-thing model, recipe, loot and tag files
(**read**). It declares itself mandatory and present on both sides (**read**).

The portable core, in decreasing order of how much of it is genuinely this
entry's own behaviour:

- the crafting-grid tool repair rule and its arithmetic (§6, §7);
- the item-matching expression shared by three settings (§8);
- the refusal rule that stops a nearly-destroyed tool being used (§11);
- the ineffective-tool list (§12);
- the right-click crop harvest (§13);
- the attractor's sweep (§10.2).

### 1.1 Ravel disposition

Ravel already owns nearly all of the structure here. Content declaration and
registration, configuration declaration and resolution, tag identity, item-level
persistent data, network payload declaration and side isolation are public Ravel
facilities, and a counterpart expresses the behaviour below through them rather
than rebuilding them (**inferred** from the dependency rules, which is where the
public/private split is recorded, not from the upstream source).

What survives as counterpart work is §6 through §8 and §11 through §13: decision
rules and arithmetic over items, with no host type in the signature that Ravel
does not already publish.

Several areas are **host-blocked** at the reviewed public revision rather than
declined: they need a surface Ravel does not publish — armour rendering layers,
entity models, tooltip line rewriting, effect application, and the accessory-slot
capability of §15. Each is named where it occurs. A counterpart records them
rather than stubbing them.

## 2. Side, lifecycle and what happens when

The entry runs on both sides and splits cleanly (**read**):

- Six registries are claimed during the registration window, on every side,
  before anything else happens: blocks, armour materials, item-level data value
  types, items, the creative grouping, and one recipe kind (§5). Item
  registration deliberately runs after block registration, because the block
  items are contributed into the item registry by the block pass (**read**; the
  source says so in a one-line comment and the ordering is load-bearing).
- Two configuration documents are declared at the same point: one client-scoped,
  one declared at the earliest scope the loader offers rather than the ordinary
  common scope (**read**). §3.4 says why that distinction matters and where it
  goes wrong.
- The network payload of §10.1 and the accessory-slot capability of §15 are
  registered in the same window.
- Three client-only registrations — block and item tinting, the entity model
  layer, and two client item extensions — are guarded by a *build-time
  environment test* rather than declared client-only (**read**). A counterpart
  declares them client-only instead; the observable behaviour is the same and the
  Ravel form is the safer one.
- After the registration barrier, on every side, four observers are attached:
  the night-vision upkeep of §14.3, the crop harvest of §13, and the two tool
  behaviour changes of §11 and §12. The accessory-slot observer of §15 joins them
  only if that optional dependency is present.
- On a client only, three further observers are attached: the inventory toggle of
  §10.1 and the two informational tooltips of §14.1.
- Also after the barrier, and explicitly deferred onto the synchronised phase of
  that stage, the override table of §7 is parsed once from configuration
  (**read**). It is parsed exactly once, at that point, and never again.

In Ravel terms: the six registries are one `ContentDeclaration` claimed through
`Registrar`; the two documents are one `ConfigurationDeclaration` resolved before
`configure`; the observers are `Mod::observe`; and §7's parse is startup work
that has to happen after the item registry is complete, because it resolves item
identifiers against it.

**One ordering hazard is worth stating on its own.** §7's parse resolves
identifiers to items eagerly and caches the result for the process lifetime. Any
later change to that setting has no effect until restart, and any tag named there
is captured as a tag *key* rather than a resolved set, so tags stay live across a
data reload while items do not (**read**). A counterpart that resolves the whole
table lazily at repair time would be more correct and would not be parity; §7
records the divergence rather than taking it silently.

## 3. Configuration

Twenty-eight settings in two documents (**read**). They fall into four groups.

### 3.1 The client document

Two settings, both presentation:

- A boolean, default on, turning the informational tooltips of §14.1 on and off.
- A list of item-matching expressions (§8) naming tools that should not receive
  the mining-speed tooltip. It ships non-empty, naming one whole third-party
  namespace, which is **read** but deliberately not transcribed because that
  entry is an upstream identifier.

### 3.2 The feature switches

Fourteen booleans, all default on, each enabling one feature: the tinted stone
blocks, the light-emitting block, the fuel fragments, the two restorative foods,
the watering tools, the sieve meshes, the attractor, the two headpieces, the
combined mining tools, the first added material tier, the second added material
tier, the right-click harvest of §13, and the two sweeping harvest tool families
(**read**). §4 says what a switch actually does, which is less than it looks.

### 3.3 The grid repair group

Ten settings governing §6 and §7 (**read**):

- **enabled** — boolean, default on. Checked first inside the recipe (§6.1), not
  used to skip registration.
- **cost** — integer, default 3, constrained to 1..8 inclusive. Units of material
  for a full repair.
- **enchantment cost** — integer, default 1, constrained to 0..8 inclusive.
  Additional units when the tool carries any enchantment.
- **strip enchantments** — boolean, default off. §6.4.
- **disable defaults** — boolean, default off. When on, the host's own
  notion of a valid repair material is ignored entirely and only §7's table
  applies.
- **override mode** — boolean, default off. §6.2's third branch.
- **cheaper for the digging-tool family** and **cheaper for the shearing tool** —
  two booleans, both default **on**, halving both costs for those two tool
  shapes. Note that these default on, so the halving is the *normal* case for
  those shapes rather than an opt-in.
- **blacklist** — a list of item-matching expressions (§8), default empty.
- **overrides** — a list of override expressions (§7), shipping two entries by
  default, both mapping a shearing tool to its metal or mineral repair material.
  Their exact content is **read** and not transcribed, being upstream
  identifiers.

### 3.4 The tweak group

Two settings (**read**): a boolean, default on, enabling §11; and a list of
item-matching expressions, default empty, naming the ineffective tools of §12.

### 3.5 The scope defect a counterpart must not copy

The second document holds everything in §3.2, §3.3 and §3.4 — that is, every
setting that decides a *recipe result* and every setting that decides whether a
*server-side interaction is refused* — and it is declared at the loader's
earliest scope, which is a per-installation file that is **not synchronised to a
joining client** (**read**).

The consequence is observable. A client and a server that disagree about §3.3's
cost produce different answers from §6, and a client that disagrees about §3.4's
list will draw a tool as ineffective that the server will happily let the player
use, or the reverse. The recipe result itself is safe, because a crafting result
is resolved by the server and sent to the client rather than recomputed there
(**inferred** — the source does not state this, but the recipe's network
encoding carries nothing, so nothing else can be happening). The *preview* the
client draws before crafting is not safe, and neither is §11's refusal message,
which is a client-side display driven by a value the client owns.

A counterpart declares this group as server-authoritative and synchronised, and
records the divergence. This is the one place in the entry where matching the
original exactly would be the wrong call.

## 4. Feature flags and what a switch actually does

Seventeen named flags are declared, each binding a name to one of §3's booleans
(**read**). They are RVL-042 §4's mechanism, consumed exactly as that section
describes: this entry declares flags and that entry supplies the predicate
machinery and the one-time scan that makes them exist.

What a flag actually gates is narrow, and it is worth being precise because the
setting names promise more than they deliver:

- **The creative grouping** (§5.4) consults them, so a disabled feature's items
  vanish from that list. Two entries are gated on *two* flags at once, both of
  which must hold (**read**).
- **The declarative recipe documents** are conditioned on them, so a disabled
  feature's recipes do not load (**inferred** — the flags exist as data-pack
  conditions in RVL-042 §4 and the recipe documents are the only consumer that
  would explain seventeen declared flags against fourteen switches; the recipe
  documents themselves were not read line by line).
- **Nothing else.** The items and blocks are registered unconditionally. A
  disabled feature's content still exists in the registries, still loads from a
  save, and is still obtainable by command (**read**).

Three flags do not correspond to a switch of their own and three switches have no
flag; the two sets are not in bijection. One flag in particular is bound to the
*wrong* switch: the flag named for the light-emitting block reads the watering
tool setting (**read**). This is an upstream defect, not a subtlety — turning off
the watering tools removes the light-emitting block from the creative grouping,
and turning off the light-emitting block does nothing. A counterpart binds each
flag to its own switch and records the divergence; reproducing the mis-binding
would be parity with a bug.

## 5. Registered content

### 5.1 Blocks

Seventeen (**read**): sixteen tinted variants of the common stone drop, and one
smooth light-emitting block.

The sixteen tinted variants are one behaviour parameterised by a colour value.
They share strength and explosion resistance figures with the block they imitate,
use its sound family, require the correct tool for drops, and each carries one
integer colour consumed by the client tinting of §14.2 (**read**). They exist as
sixteen registered blocks rather than one block with a colour property, and a
counterpart that collapsed them into one block with a state property would break
every identifier a save holds. Keep the sixteen.

The light-emitting block is a full-brightness block with a low strength figure
and a glass sound family (**read**).

Each block contributes exactly one block item, generated by the block pass and
handed to the item registry (§2).

### 5.2 Items

Fifty-nine registered items beyond the block items (**read**), in seven groups:

- **Two fuel fragments**, each declaring a fixed burn duration of two hundred
  ticks — that is, one eighth of the item they are a fragment of (**read**).
- **Two restorative foods** (§10.3).
- **Two watering tools**, differing only in a radius and a per-block chance:
  three and one quarter, seven and roughly one third (**read**). All of their
  behaviour is RVL-042 §11's base watering arithmetic; this entry overrides
  exactly one decision, whether a non-player actor may use them, and answers it
  from §3.2's setting (**read**).
- **A plant fibre and three sieve meshes**, the meshes being RVL-042 §10's
  reusable items with twenty, sixty-four and five hundred and twelve uses
  respectively (**read**). §10 of that entry supplies the whole of their
  behaviour including the crafting remainder and the durability-preserving
  enchantment draw; this entry supplies only the three numbers.
- **The attractor** (§10.2).
- **Two head-worn night-vision pieces**, differing in armour material and in a
  durability multiplier of fifteen against thirty (**read**).
- **The gear**: two added material tiers each yielding a sword, a pickaxe, a
  shovel, an axe and a hoe, one of them additionally a shearing tool with a flat
  hundred-point durability; four armour pieces per tier; eight combined mining
  tools, eight of one sweeping harvest shape and eight of the other, spanning the
  host's own tiers plus the two added ones (**read**). Every one of these is a
  thin construction over an RVL-042 base type, and the sweeping shapes carry a
  radius of one to three that RVL-042 §11 consumes.

### 5.3 The two added material tiers and four armour materials

Both tiers are **read** in full: durability, mining speed, attack bonus,
enchantability, repair ingredient and the tag of blocks each tier *cannot* drop.
The lower tier sits between the host's stone and iron on durability and above
both on speed; the upper sits above diamond on durability and speed and below it
on attack. The four armour materials each give per-slot protection, an
enchantability figure, an equip sound, a repair ingredient and a toughness
figure; two of them declare non-zero toughness, one also a knockback resistance
of zero (**read**).

These figures are pure data. They are not transcribed here as a table, because a
counterpart declares them as data and the numbers themselves are the entry's
balance rather than its behaviour; they are available at the pinned revision.

**One defect is worth naming**: the second added tier's *armour* declares its
repair ingredient as the metal of a different tier entirely, not the mineral its
tools are made of (**read**). §6.2's default-material branch therefore behaves
differently for that tier's armour than a player would expect. A counterpart
either matches it and records it, or fixes it and records that; both are
defensible, and silently doing either is not.

### 5.4 The creative grouping

One grouping, titled from a translation key and iconed with one of the watering
tools, listing every item above in a fixed hand-written order, each entry gated
on one or two of §4's flags (**read**). The order is presentation. The gating is
not, and is the main observable use of §4.

### 5.5 Item-level data value types

Exactly one: a boolean saying whether the attractor is switched on (**read**).
It is declared both persistent and network-synchronised. §9 is about that one
value and nothing else.

### 5.6 Recipe kind

One (§6), registered as a serialiser whose network and file encodings both carry
no data at all, because every input the rule needs comes from configuration
rather than from the recipe document (**read**). One declarative document exists
solely to instantiate it.

### 5.7 Tags and declarative data

The entry contributes tag membership documents in five namespaces: its own, the
host's, the conventional cross-mod namespace, RVL-042's, and the optional
accessory-slot dependency's (**read**). Its own namespace also declares the two
"cannot drop" block tags of §5.3, and item tags for two of the sweeping harvest
shapes at one tier. Seventeen block loot documents, one per block, and about
seventy recipe documents complete the data. None of it was read as behaviour
beyond the census here; it is declarative and a counterpart declares it.

The membership in RVL-042's tag namespace is a genuine consumption of that entry:
the three tool-shape tags it defines are how that entry's own logic recognises
these tools (**read**).

## 6. The crafting-grid tool repair

The one genuinely novel mechanic. A damaged tool placed in a crafting grid
together with valid repair materials is mended. The rule is a special recipe:
it declares no ingredients, no result, and no shape, and it answers "does this
grid match?" by running the whole assembly and asking whether the answer is
non-empty (**read**). A counterpart preserves that identity — matching and
assembling are the same computation — because any divergence between them shows
up as a grid that previews a result and refuses to craft it.

### 6.1 Reading the grid

Over the grid in slot order (**read**):

- Skip empty slots.
- The **first** non-empty damageable item encountered becomes the tool. Every
  other non-empty slot, including any later damageable item, becomes a material.
- Refuse if no tool was found, if the tool is undamaged, or if there were no
  materials.
- Refuse if the tool matches §3.3's blacklist under §8.
- Refuse if §3.3's enabled setting is off. This is checked before all of the
  above.

"First in slot order" is load-bearing: a grid holding two damaged tools repairs
the earlier one using the later one as a material, if the later one qualifies as
a material at all. That is **read** behaviour, not an inference.

### 6.2 What one material is worth

For a (tool, material) pair, the value is a real number (**read**):

- If the pair has an entry in §7's table, the value is that entry's multiplier —
  **unless** the third branch below fires.
- Otherwise, if §3.3's disable-defaults setting is off and the host considers the
  material a valid repair item for that tool, the value is exactly one.
- **The override-mode branch.** If §3.3's override-mode setting is on, the pair
  has *no* entry of its own, and the table holds *some* entry for this tool
  against a different material, the value is zero. The tool-level test asks only
  whether the tool appears anywhere in the table; it does not consider the
  material. This is what "override mode" means: listing a tool once replaces its
  default materials entirely.
- Otherwise zero.

A value of zero is the refusal signal (§6.3), so the third branch is not a
discount, it is a rejection.

### 6.3 The craft loop

Walking the materials in the order they were collected, holding a running total
and a saturation flag (**read**):

- If the saturation flag is already set when a material is examined, **refuse the
  whole craft**. The saturating material must be the last one in the grid; a
  surplus material after the tool is already full does not overflow harmlessly,
  it voids the craft.
- If the material declares a crafting remainder — that is, if crafting it would
  give it back — refuse the whole craft. This is exactly RVL-042 §10's remainder
  contract seen from the consuming side: that entry's reusable items always
  declare a remainder, so none of them can ever be a repair material.
- If the material's value is exactly zero, refuse the whole craft.
- Otherwise add the value to the running total, and if the tool's damage minus
  the per-unit repair times the running total would reach zero or below, set the
  saturation flag.

The comparison against zero is an exact floating-point equality (**read**). A
negative multiplier in §7's table therefore passes it and accumulates as a
*negative* contribution, reducing the total and, with a large enough magnitude,
producing a result *more* damaged than the input. A counterpart either preserves
that or rejects non-positive multipliers at parse time; the honest choice is to
reject at parse time and record the divergence, because the alternative is a
configuration-driven way to destroy a player's tool.

### 6.4 The result

The per-unit repair is the tool's maximum damage divided by the sum of §3.3's
cost and, only if the tool carries any enchantment, its enchantment cost — with
truncating integer division (**read**). Before that division, if §3.3's two
cheaper-shape settings apply to the tool's shape, both costs are halved by
truncating division with the repaired cost floored at one and the enchantment
component floored at zero (**read**).

The result is a single copy of the tool whose damage is reduced by the truncation
toward zero of the per-unit repair times the running total (**read**). Both
truncations matter and both round the player's way only by accident; a
counterpart reproduces the integer semantics exactly, because the arithmetic *is*
the observable behaviour here.

If §3.3's strip-enchantments setting is on, every enchantment that is not a
curse is then removed. A curse is identified structurally, as an enchantment
carrying either the effect that prevents the item being dropped on death or the
effect that prevents it being unequipped (**read**) — not by a name or a list. A
counterpart uses the same structural test, because a name list goes stale.

The recipe declares that it consumes every input: it returns an empty remainder
for every grid slot including the tool's (**read**). The tool leaves the grid
only as the result.

## 7. The repair-material override table

Parsed once at startup (§2) from §3.3's list. Each entry is a string of the form
*tool selector* `=` *material selector*, with an optional `@` and a real number
appended to the material selector giving the multiplier, default one (**read**).

A selector is either an item identifier or the token `tag:` followed by a tag
identifier. Both are distinguished by counting colon-separated fields: three
fields means a tag and requires the `tag:` prefix, two fields means an item,
anything else is an error (**read**). An unparseable entry, an unknown item, or a
malformed multiplier is logged and that entry is skipped; the rest of the table
still loads and startup continues (**read**). Only the multiplier failure is
partial: a bad multiplier falls back to one rather than dropping the entry
(**read**).

Lookup is a linear scan returning the **first** entry whose tool selector and
material selector both match (**read**). Order in the configuration file is
therefore significant and a counterpart preserves it. The separate tool-level
test used by §6.2's third branch asks whether *any* entry's tool selector matches.

Two divergences a counterpart should record rather than reproduce: the eager
item resolution of §2, which makes the table deaf to registry changes after
startup; and the fact that an item identifier containing no colon at all is
treated as a syntax error rather than defaulting to the host namespace.

## 8. The item-matching expression

Three settings — §3.1's tooltip exclusions, §3.3's repair blacklist and §3.4's
ineffective list — share one small matching language over a single item
(**read**):

- `tag:` followed by a namespaced tag identifier, matching membership in that
  tag. Recognised by the field count being three *and* the string starting with
  the prefix.
- A namespace, a colon and `*`, matching every item in that namespace.
- A full item identifier, matching that item exactly by string comparison.
- Anything else matches nothing.

A list matches if any entry matches. This is a pure predicate over an item and
its registry identity and it is the most reusable thing in the entry; a
counterpart implements it once and the three settings share it, as the original
does. The tag branch resolves the tag key at each call rather than caching, so it
follows a data reload correctly — unlike §7.

## 9. Persistence: what survives a save, an exit and a reload

The entry owns **no** world-level saved data, no block entities, no player
attachments and no server state (**read**). Everything it stores is item-level.

The complete inventory of persisted state is:

- **One boolean per attractor item**, §5.5's data value, declared with a
  persistent codec and a network codec, and given the default `false` on the item
  itself so a freshly registered attractor carries it explicitly (**read**).
- **Ordinary item damage** on every damageable thing it registers, which is the
  host's own storage and not this entry's (**read**).

Nothing else. In particular the night-vision upkeep of §14.3 keeps a
**process-lifetime, in-memory list** of who is currently wearing a headpiece,
which is not saved, is rebuilt from the wearer's equipment within one tick of a
reload, and is the correct thing to be transient (**read**).

For the M6 scenario — fresh play, save, exit, reload — the round trip a
counterpart must demonstrate is therefore small and completely stated: an
attractor switched on before the save is switched on after the reload, its
foil-like highlight is drawn, its tooltip reads as enabled, and its sweep resumes;
and a partially damaged tool of every registered shape returns with the same
damage. There is nothing else to compare, and a counterpart that stores anything
world-level has added state the original does not have.

One caution about the transient list: its identity key is the wearer's profile
name concatenated with whether they are the local player (**read**). That is not
a stable identity — it collides across a name change and is not a persistent
identifier — but because the list never outlives the process and is rebuilt every
tick, no incorrect state can survive into a save. A counterpart keys on the
persistent player identifier instead and records the divergence.

## 10. Behaviours over items

### 10.1 The one network exchange

Exactly one payload, client to server, on a channel the entry versions as `"1"`
(**read**). Its body is a single variable-length integer: a slot index within the
sending player's currently open container.

The server handler, executed on the main thread rather than the network thread,
reads the item in that slot of the sender's open container, checks it really is
an attractor, and flips §5.5's boolean on it (**read**).

**This is an unvalidated index into a container the sender names.** The handler
does not bound-check the index before indexing the slot list, and it does not
verify that the slot is one the player may interact with — only that the item in
it is an attractor. A malformed or hostile client can therefore send an index
outside the list. A counterpart validates the index against the container's slot
count before indexing, and rejects a slot the sender does not own; that is a
divergence to record, and it is not optional. Ravel's payload declaration is the
place to enforce it.

The client half is a screen observer: a right mouse press, on a slot under the
cursor, with nothing held on the cursor, in either the player's own inventory or
the optional accessory screen of §15, whose item is an attractor, sends the
payload, plays a sound locally, and consumes the event (**read**). The accessory
screen is recognised by **comparing the screen class name as a string**
(**read**) — a fragile test that a counterpart replaces with a real capability
query and records.

The client does not update its own copy of the boolean; it waits for the server's
item synchronisation. The sound it plays is chosen from the *pre-flip* value,
so the pitch it plays is the one for the state being left rather than entered
(**read**). The direct use-in-hand path of §10.2 has the same ordering. Whether
that is intentional is **unknown**.

### 10.2 The attractor

A single-stack item carrying §5.5's boolean, default off (**read**).

- **Used in hand**, it flips the boolean, plays a sound whose pitch depends on
  the pre-flip state, and reports success.
- **It draws with the enchanted-item highlight exactly when it is switched on**,
  which is the entry's one use of that hook.
- **Its tooltip** reads as enabled or disabled from the same boolean.
- **Every inventory tick, on both sides, when switched on and held by a player**,
  it collects loose item entities and experience orbs within a configured radius
  of the holder's bounding box, defaulting to seven and constrained to 1..16
  (**read**), and moves them to the holder's position.

The sweep's exclusion rules are **read** and precise: a dead item entity is
skipped; an item entity carrying the host's marker forbidding remote movement is
skipped; and an item entity whose recorded thrower is this same holder *and*
which still has a pickup delay is skipped, so a player's own just-dropped items
are not immediately re-collected. Experience orbs get none of these tests. The
actual repositioning, and the clearing of the pickup delay that precedes it, are
performed on the server side only, but the enclosing scan runs on both — a
counterpart runs the scan server-side only and records the divergence, since the
client-side scan produces no effect.

The radius is applied by inflating the holder's bounding box, so the region is a
box and not a sphere (**read**). That is the behaviour; a counterpart that used a
distance test would collect a visibly different set.

### 10.3 The two restorative foods

Two always-edible foods of six and ten nutrition and one and a half and two
saturation, both at the highest rarity (**read**).

On being eaten, each applies four or five timed effects. The extension rule is
identical for every one and is worth stating exactly, because it is not the
obvious one (**read**): a shared duration accumulator starts at zero; for each
effect in a fixed order, if the eater already has that effect at or above a
per-effect amplifier threshold, the accumulator is **replaced** by that effect's
remaining duration; then the effect is applied at its fixed amplifier for the
accumulator plus a fixed increment.

The accumulator is shared across the whole sequence and is never reset between
effects. An earlier effect's remaining duration therefore extends a *later*
effect that the eater does not have at all. That is **read**, it is almost
certainly not intended, and a counterpart that gives each effect its own
accumulator produces different durations. Record the choice either way.

Effect application is **host-blocked**: nothing public applies a timed effect to
an entity. The arithmetic above is portable and testable on its own.

## 11. The nearly-destroyed tool refusal

When §3.4's switch is on, a tool that is *nearly* destroyed stops working before
it breaks, rather than breaking (**read**).

- **The test**: an item whose maximum damage exceeds two and whose current damage
  is at or above its maximum minus two. So the last two points of durability are
  unusable, and items with a maximum of two or less are exempt.
- **Where it applies**: block break speed, block breaking, attacking an entity,
  using an item in the air, using an item on a block, interacting with an entity,
  and RVL-042 §9's cancellable crop-harvest event. All seven observers cancel.
- **Which tools**: the digging, sword and shearing shapes for most of the
  observers; the in-air use path applies instead to the shearing shape and
  ranged weapons; the entity-interaction path applies only to the shearing shape.
  The lists differ per observer and the differences are **read**, not tidied.
- **Exemption**: a player in the host's build-without-consumption mode is never
  refused.
- **Feedback**: an action-bar message naming the item, and a tooltip line on a
  broken tool. The tooltip observer additionally covers ranged weapons.

All seven observers are attached at a raised priority so the refusal is decided
before other observers act (**read**). A counterpart preserves the relative
ordering; RVL-042 §9 states that a cancel of the crop event stops one block
without stopping the surrounding sweep, which is exactly the granularity wanted
here.

The refusal is a pure predicate over an item plus the event surface. The
predicate is portable; the seven attachment points are Ravel observation.

## 12. The ineffective-tool list

An unconditional behaviour — it has **no switch of its own** and is governed only
by §3.4's list being non-empty (**read**).

An item is ineffective if it matches that list under §8 **and** is of a tiered,
ranged-weapon or shearing shape. The source's own comment insists the list is a
whitelist of things to ruin rather than a blacklist of things to protect, and the
behaviour agrees (**read**).

An ineffective tool, in the hands of a player not in build-without-consumption
mode (**read**):

- mines at speed zero;
- deals no damage: incoming damage whose source is a player attack is cancelled
  outright, which cancels the *whole* damage event and not merely this tool's
  contribution;
- cannot be used on a block, in the air, or on an entity — all three are
  cancelled;
- cancels RVL-042 §9's crop-harvest event;
- has its attack-damage tooltip line rewritten to zero, and gains one of four
  explanatory lines chosen by shape plus a shared second line.

Two things a counterpart should notice. The damage cancellation keys on the
damage source's message identifier being the string for a player attack
(**read**), which is a stringly-typed test over a host constant; use the typed
source instead. And the tooltip rewriting walks the assembled tooltip looking for
a *localised substring* and replaces the whole line (**read**) — that is
host-blocked, presentation, and not something a counterpart reproduces
mechanically.

## 13. Right-click crop harvest

When §3.2's switch is on, on the server side only, a main-hand right click on a
block (**read**):

- **A mature crop**: its seed item is identified through RVL-042's crop helper,
  its drops are produced, exactly one copy of that seed is withheld from the
  drops, the remainder is dropped in the world, the player's arm swings, the
  block's own break sound plays, and the crop is set back to its first growth
  stage.
- **A mature variant of the nether-growing crop**: the same, with a fixed seed
  item and its own age property reset to zero.

The withholding is the mechanic: the player gets the harvest minus one seed,
which is the seed that stays in the ground. It is withheld by decrementing the
first matching drop stack by one rather than by removing a stack, so a drop stack
of several is reduced rather than deleted (**read**).

Nothing is consumed, nothing is damaged, and no tool is required. The hand check
means an off-hand click does nothing. The seed identification is a direct
consumption of RVL-042 §11's helper surface, and the drop production is a host
call a counterpart will find host-blocked in part; the withholding rule itself is
portable.

## 14. Client presentation

### 14.1 Informational tooltips

Two, both gated on §3.1's boolean (**read**). On a digging tool not excluded by
§3.1's list, a mining-speed line reading zero if §12 has made the tool
ineffective and the tier's speed otherwise. On a ranged weapon, either a count of
usable ammunition across the off-hand slot and the main inventory, or — if the
weapon carries the infinite-ammunition enchantment — a recolouring of that
enchantment's existing tooltip line, green when ammunition is present and red
when it is not.

The ammunition count is portable arithmetic and the only part worth specifying:
it sums the stack sizes of every inventory stack the weapon accepts as a held
projectile, off-hand first. Everything else here is line insertion and
recolouring into an assembled tooltip, which is **host-blocked**.

### 14.2 Tinting

The sixteen tinted blocks and their block items are registered with RVL-042's
colour handlers, which read §5.1's per-block integer (**read**). One texture and
one model serve all sixteen. A counterpart declares the colour as content data;
the tinting itself is presentation.

### 14.3 The head-worn night-vision pieces

A per-tick upkeep, attached on both sides (**read**): a wearer — head slot, or
the accessory slot of §15 — is added to §9's transient list; while listed and
still wearing, they are given a short, hidden, ambient night-vision effect
refreshed every tick; when they stop wearing, the wearer's night-vision effect
is removed without a client update and the entry is dropped from the list.

The removal is **unconditional** (**read**): it clears whatever night-vision
effect the wearer holds, without checking that this upkeep is what granted it.
So taking the piece off also cancels a night vision the wearer drank for, and a
potion swallowed while the piece is worn is overwritten by the next tick's
refresh. A counterpart should remove only the effect it applied, and record the
divergence; keeping the original here would make an unrelated potion silently
disappear, which is not behaviour a parity test should be written against.

The refresh duration is a little over twelve seconds against a one-tick refresh
interval, which is what makes removal look instant rather than fading (**read**).
Effect application is **host-blocked**.

The rendering of the piece on a wearer's model — a dedicated model layer, two
textures chosen by which piece is worn, and a client item extension supplying the
humanoid model — is **host-blocked** in full and is not specified further. It is
registered only when §15's optional dependency is present (**read**).

## 15. The optional accessory-slot integration

One optional dependency, detected by asking the loader whether that mod is
present and never by catching a failure (**read**). Present, it adds:

- an item capability on the attractor making it tick in an accessory slot,
  delegating to §10.2's inventory tick with a sentinel slot index;
- an item capability on the two head-worn pieces allowing them to be equipped
  directly from use;
- an observer that damages a worn night-vision piece by one point whenever its
  wearer takes damage, before that damage is applied;
- the model layer and screen recognition already noted in §14.3 and §10.1.

All of it is **host-blocked**: Ravel publishes no accessory-slot surface, and the
integration is with a mod outside this wave. A counterpart records it and
implements nothing. The important consequence for the M6 scenario is that
**none of it is reachable** in a pack without that mod, so §9's round trip is
unaffected by it.

## 17. What this specification does not specify

- **Anything drawn.** The armour model layer and its textures, the tooltip line
  insertion and rewriting of §12 and §14.1, and the screen interaction of §10.1
  are **host-blocked**: Ravel publishes no rendering, model, or tooltip-assembly
  surface. A counterpart records them and implements nothing.
- **Timed effect application**, in §10.3 and §14.3, for the same reason. The
  duration arithmetic of §10.3 is specified and is testable without it.
- **The accessory-slot integration** of §15, in full.
- **The balance numbers** of §5.3 and the burn, nutrition, radius and durability
  figures of §5.2. They are data, they are available at the pinned revision, and
  transcribing them here would put an upstream balance table in a document whose
  whole point is to carry no upstream material.
- **Any identifier.** No registry name, mod id, tag id, translation key, item
  identifier or configuration key from the original appears here, and no
  save-compatible identifier mapping is specified, because the names surface is
  unresolved in `provenance/m6-foundation/RVL-086.json`. §5.1's warning to keep
  sixteen blocks rather than one is about *cardinality*, not about names.
- **The declarative documents** beyond §5.7's census. About three hundred model,
  recipe, loot, tag and blockstate files were fetched and were read only far
  enough to count them and to name the namespaces they touch.
- **The textures.** Sixty-two image files were fetched with the source and were
  not opened.

Every Java source file at the pinned revision was read in full — forty-three

  §10.3's shared duration accumulator are known upstream. All three are read as
