# RVL-097 — behaviour specification

Wave: `waves/m6-foundation.json`. Method: source-informed reimplementation.
Rights: the upstream project's repository root carries an MIT licence file, and
its own readme repeats that all code and images are under it; see
`provenance/m6-foundation.json` for the pinned revision and digest.

Confidence markers: **read**, **inferred**, **unknown**.

This is the largest of the five entries by a wide margin and the one whose
specification is most incomplete. §10 says exactly which parts were read.

---

## 1. What this entry is

A declarative mod-authoring contract. Several other mods in the pinned pack depend
on its observable behaviour, including RVL-217, and that dependency is why RVL-217
is admitted to the foundation wave at all (**read** — RVL-217 relies on settings
being settled before registration, ordered registration, and presence-gated
integration activation).

Two facts shape the Ravel contract (**read**):

- The pinned implementation supports several host platforms. That confirms that
  host-specific bootstrapping is not dependent-visible behaviour. Ravel must
  reproduce the platform-independent outcomes below through its own public
  lifecycle; it must not recreate the source's platform bindings or internal
  arrangement.
- **Its central idea is declarative registration.** A dependent declares each
  thing, its settings, and whether it is enabled; enabled declarations become
  registered content and disabled declarations do not. In Ravel terms, settings
  belong in the public configuration declaration and resolved `configure`
  lifecycle, while content belongs in `ContentDeclaration` and is claimed through
  `Registrar` during the registration window. No additional handler abstraction is
  part of this contract.

The pinned artifact also ships a debug-oriented item family, a particle, and two
developer-environment options (**read** as existence only). Their behavior is not
specified by the accepted evidence (§10), so a counterpart must not invent content
or option effects from their names.

### 1.1 Ravel disposition

Ravel has absorbed this entry's central mechanism. Dependents satisfy the
configuration, enablement, registration, ordering, side, command-ownership,
packet-contract, and saved-data declaration obligations below by using Ravel's
public lifecycle and SDK directly. The counterpart must not reimplement, wrap,
proxy, or mediate those public facilities. It must not instantiate the 34 coverage
families in §2.2 or expose a second declaration surface for them.

A behavior remains counterpart work only when this specification establishes a
separate dependent-observable result and Ravel does not already provide it. A
cross-mod residual, when genuinely required, is exposed only as a declared Ravel
service; the existence of an upstream helper is not by itself a reason to invent a
service. At the public revision reviewed for this reconciliation:

- configuration declaration and resolution, content declaration and registration,
  lifecycle ordering, side isolation, command ownership, packet contracts, and
  versioned saved data are platform-absorbed;
- optional presence-gated activation remains blocked by open `RVL-GAP-07` (§4);
- packet transport is not a public Ravel capability, so the message actions in §6
  cannot be made live by a counterpart without a future public capability;
- field-level automatic persistence in §7 is a residual behavior, but its unknown
  key scheme and Ravel's deliberately mod-owned saved-data envelope do not justify
  a reflective compatibility layer.

Under the behavior currently both specified and publicly implementable, no own
content behavior remains for this entry. Its settled disposition is therefore
**wholly absorbed; no package**: this entry produces no counterpart package, and
`counterparts/` holds none for it.

The evidence is the four findings above, each of which this document establishes
and none of which a package could act on. The obligations of §2, §3 and §5 are
met by dependents using Ravel's public lifecycle and SDK directly, so a package
could only re-expose them. §4 is blocked on the missing public presence-gating
capability recorded as `RVL-GAP-07`. §6 is blocked on the absence of a public
packet transport. §7 is residual behavior whose key scheme is unknown and whose
absorbed saved-data contract already covers what dependents observe. What is left
after those is §8, which this document does not specify at all. A package would
therefore carry nothing but a name and a lifecycle entry point, and shipping one
would assert compatibility work that does not exist.

That is an absorbed mechanism, not an incomplete implementation. Future evidence
or a formally resolved public capability may reopen this disposition; it must not
be anticipated here.

## 2. Declarative registration

### 2.1 The model

A dependent declares one entry per thing it wants to exist. Each declaration
states (**read**):

- which behavioral kind of thing it is, from the coverage set in §2.2;
- a name, unique within the mod;
- the content to make available when enabled;
- whether a player or pack may disable it, and its resolved enabled value.

The dependent expresses this directly through two coordinated public Ravel
declarations, not through a counterpart-owned object, adapter, or facade (**read**
for the outcomes; Ravel-native expression):

- `ConfigurationDeclaration` carries every player- or pack-controlled enabled
  setting, including its validation and default. `configure` reads the resolved
  value before registration.
- `ContentDeclaration` carries the content the dependent owns. During `register`,
  the dependent claims enabled entries through `Registrar`; disabled entries make
  no claim and produce no registered content.
- The declared setting and content entry must identify the same dependent-owned
  thing so a setting cannot silently govern some other registration. How the
  counterpart represents that association internally is not observable and is not
  specified.

For kinds whose observable rule is one entry per mod, a second declaration for the
same kind must be refused before either can become registered (**read**). The
source enforces that through an instance convention; that convention is not part
of the Ravel contract.

Two failure modes are named explicitly in the source and must be preserved: a
malformed author declaration is a load-time refusal, and an attempt to disable an
entry declared non-disableable is a distinct load-time refusal (**read**). Ravel
may report them through its native declaration/configuration errors; callers must
still be able to distinguish the two causes.

### 2.2 The published kinds

Thirty-four kinds are published (**read**). They cover: items; blocks; block
entities; entities; villager professions; status effects; capabilities; recipes,
recipe types, recipe displays and recipe book categories; world features,
structures, structure pieces, foliage placers and trunk placers; command argument
types; creative tabs; advancement criteria; five loot-table extension kinds;
sound events; data component types; armour materials; enchantment entity effects;
screens; particles; item tint sources; game test instance types; points of
interest; and a settings-only placeholder that registers no content.

This inventory is behavioral coverage data, not an executable taxonomy a Ravel
counterpart must reproduce. Dependents declare only the content kinds Ravel's
public SDK publishes and use those declarations directly. The counterpart must
not instantiate these families, mirror them as types, translate them through a
proxy, or add a parallel 34-kind dispatcher. Where the SDK has no public kind or
capability, that absence is an explicit SDK gap rather than counterpart work. The
wave's registry-parity test measures outcomes available through the public SDK; it
does not require 34 counterpart-owned representatives.

The source's per-kind config-file category names are observable and were read, but
several are also upstream identifiers and are intentionally not transcribed here.
A Ravel counterpart must publish stable Ravel-native setting keys and preserve the
setting's availability, default and effect on registration; exact source category
spellings are a deliberate names-surface divergence while that surface remains
unresolved.

### 2.3 Configurable properties

Separately from content entries, a dependent may declare a configurable setting.
The setting is data in Ravel's public `ConfigurationDeclaration`; no field
annotation, reflection rule, or configuration-object layout is part of this
contract. Each declaration states (**read**):

- the stable grouping and key under which it appears, including a disambiguating
  id when a mod has several instances of the same setting family;
- human-readable explanatory text written into the configuration surface;
- which of four **config locations** it belongs to: common (both sides, global
  directory, not synchronised), client (client only, global directory, not
  synchronised), server (server only, per-save directory, **synchronised to
  clients on connect**), or startup (loaded as early as possible, global
  directory, not synchronised). The default is common;
- whether it may be changed at runtime by command;
- whether changing it requires a world regeneration or a full restart;
- whether it appears in a settings screen;
- for numeric properties, an inclusive minimum and maximum.

All nine are observable through the generated configuration surface and the
command in §5, and all must be preserved. The dependent receives only validated,
resolved values through `configure`; it does not read or write configuration files.
Any facet the public declaration schema cannot express remains an SDK capability
gap rather than private metadata added by this counterpart.

The four locations in particular carry the whole of the entry's side-and-sync
behaviour: a server-scoped value changes what a connecting client sees, and a
startup value is settled before registration can be affected by it. The source
notes the hazard for startup values itself — using one to enable or disable
registration can make a client unable to join a server whose value differs
(**read**).

## 3. Mod lifecycle

Dependants observe a fixed relative order (**read**, from RVL-217 and the pinned
source entry point). The contract is that order, not a base type:

- the mod is identified and its author declarations are validated;
- `ConfigurationDeclaration` is resolved and `configure` applies the resulting
  values before any enabled/disabled registration decision;
- `ContentDeclaration` entries are claimed through `Registrar` during Ravel's
  registration window;
- after the registration barrier closes, the dependent's common setup may consume
  the committed registrations;
- enabled, present integrations activate after that common setup (§4);
- client-only state is constructed only on a client side, while common state is
  constructed on every applicable side;
- a mod that declares no creative-tab entry claims none and no tab is created
  (**read**).

Registration ordering is part of the contract: RVL-217 arranges for contributors
to fill its registries before RVL-217 consumes them (**read**). In Ravel this must
be expressed through resolved mod order and the public registration barrier. A
registry-of-registries, listener-priority API, or lifecycle superclass is neither
required nor permitted as a substitute. If the required ordering cannot be
expressed publicly, composition must be refused rather than allowing the dependent
to observe an empty registry.

## 4. Third-party integrations

A mod declares integrations, each identifying an optional foreign provider. Each
declaration states whether it is enabled by default and carries human-readable
explanatory text; its enabled state is a setting in the mod's public
`ConfigurationDeclaration` so a player or pack can turn it off (**read**).

The dependent-observable outcomes are (**read**):

- an absent target does not invalidate the mod set and the callback does not run;
- a present but disabled target does not run the callback;
- a present and enabled target runs its registration callback once, at the point
  fixed by §3;
- failures from a present target are not misreported as absence.

This is the same observable purpose as RVL-129 §6 and belongs in one public Ravel
mechanism. `RVL-GAP-07` remains the historical open record for the missing
optional-presence and per-integration activation capability until a shipped public
repair is formally recorded as its resolution. Until then this part of RVL-097 is
blocked: a counterpart must not invent an integration loader, inspect host
internals, or turn the optional relationship into a required dependency merely to
proceed.

If a later resolution leaves a distinct cross-mod callback contract for dependents,
that residual is declared as a Ravel service. No service is prescribed now: the
accepted evidence identifies activation outcomes, not a separate callable behavior
that survives once public presence-gating exists.

## 5. Commands

Ravel already absorbs command declaration, ownership and dispatch through
`ContentDeclaration`, `Registrar` and `run_command`; a counterpart must not wrap
that path. The actions below are specified own behavior, but the reviewed public
surface does not provide the complete operations they require: live packet send,
entity selection and ignition, resource-reload triggering, or a command-time edit
of the player's resolved configuration document. A partial command facade would
claim behavior it cannot deliver, and nothing requires one of an entry whose
disposition is wholly absorbed with no package.

A command tree rooted at the framework's own name, with four subcommands, all
requiring game-master permission (**read**):

- **config** — read and write, at runtime, any configurable property that was
  declared runtime-changeable. Reading prints the current value to the invoking
  player. The available property names are offered as completions, sourced from
  the declarations of §2.3, so the command surface changes with the loaded mods.
- **debug** — send one of a set of round-trip test messages from client to
  server, printing what was sent. The available message names are likewise
  offered as completions.
- **ignite** — set entities matched by an entity selector on fire, for a given
  number of ticks, or two ticks when no duration is given. Each affected entity
  produces a translated chat line naming it and the duration.
- **reloadresources** — trigger a resource reload.

Every one of these returns zero regardless of outcome (**read**). As with
RVL-162's command, a counterpart should return meaningful values and record the
divergence.

## 6. Message framework

Ravel absorbs declaration, deterministic encoding/decoding, direction and version
checking through its public packet contracts. It does not yet transport packets.
The remaining live-delivery behavior is therefore blocked on a public transport
capability; a counterpart must not add its own channel or wrap packet contracts as
a substitute.

A framework for defining messages between the two sides (**read**):

- Every message declares whether it may be handled off the main game thread. If
  it may, the handler is **not** guaranteed a player, and the source says so
  explicitly. If it may not, the player is available. This is the thread-model
  statement RVL-129's specification could not make, and a counterpart should
  prefer this one: the asynchrony is a declared property of the message, not an
  ambient property of the transport.
- Every message defines a client-side action and a server-side action separately,
  each given the level and the player.
- Encoding and decoding failures are wrapped and re-raised with the message named
  in the failure, rather than propagating raw (**read**). The message identity in
  the failure is observable in logs and is worth reproducing.

A convenience layer lets a message declare its payload as a set of marked fields
instead of writing an encoder. Its observable contract (**read**):

- marked fields must never be absent;
- **fields are encoded in a defined total order, and that order is by field name,
  not declaration order.** The source states the reason: the platform does not
  guarantee a stable order for reflected fields, and an unstable order corrupts
  messages between a client and a server running the same code. The *ordering
  guarantee* is required; a counterpart with a stable declaration order may use
  that instead, but must have some total order fixed at build time and must not
  rely on a runtime-derived one;
- fields inherited from a supertype are included, and supertype fields are
  ordered after the subtype's under the same rule (**inferred** — the source
  walks up the hierarchy appending groups, and this document states the
  consequence rather than the walk);
- a field whose type has no registered encoding is an error.

The framework's own message set includes a button-press notification, a player
position report, a resource-reload trigger, a value-change notification, and four
round-trip test messages used by the debug command — synchronous and
asynchronous, simple and structured (**read**).

## 7. Field-level persistence

Ravel absorbs ownership, versioning, migration and atomic opening of mod-owned
saved data through its public saved-data lifecycle. It deliberately hands the mod
a payload rather than reflecting over its fields. The field-level convenience
below is residual behavior, but it does not justify a second persistence facade:
the on-disk key scheme remains unknown and no accepted dependent requires this
mechanism independently of the absorbed saved-data contract.

Block entities may declare individual fields as persisted, and the framework
writes and reads them without the author writing any save code (**read**).

Observable contract:

- A declared field is written into the block entity's saved data and restored
  from it.
- Each declaration states what happens when the key is **absent** on load: either
  the field is reset to a framework-supplied default (the default behaviour), or
  it is left untouched. This is a real behavioural fork and must be reproduced —
  it is the difference between a world upgrade zeroing a machine and leaving it
  alone.
- Fields whose type is itself a serialisable structure must never be absent; they
  must hold a placeholder that the load populates.
- The set of field types that can be persisted this way is fixed and known; a
  field of an unsupported type is an error.

**unknown**: whether the on-disk key for a field is its name, and whether renaming
a field is therefore a breaking change for existing worlds. The reader did not
confirm this, and it matters enough that the implementer should confirm it before
choosing a scheme. The wave's saved-data round-trip test is where a wrong answer
surfaces.

## 8. Everything else

The following areas exist and were **not** read in behavioural detail. They are
listed so the gap is visible and sized, not so a counterpart can be planned from
the list (**read**, as an inventory of the source's top level):

- helpers for blocks, entities, items, inventories, locations, rendering, text
  formatting, minecraft-version-specific behaviour and more (the single largest
  area after configuration);
- an inventory and container/menu framework, including sided wrappers, slot
  kinds, and a value-synchronisation mechanism between a screen handler and its
  screen;
- a client framework: screens, widgets, item and block rendering hooks, a
  developer-world main-menu button, and a startup music suppressor, both of the
  latter gated on the two general options and on running in a development
  environment (**read**);
- block and block-entity base behaviour, including a dirty-marking protocol;
- advancement criteria helpers;
- data structures and one algorithm helper;
- a game-test harness;
- a proxy pair for side-specific work.

## 9. Two behaviours a counterpart must **not** reproduce

Both are **read** directly and both are stated here as findings, not as
requirements.

### 9.1 The startup network fetch

At startup the framework fetches a plain-text file from a fixed public URL on a
background thread, parses it into a set of player identifiers, and uses that set
to decide who gets a purely cosmetic effect. It is guarded so it runs at most
once per launch, and failure is swallowed (**read**).

This is an outbound network request the player did not ask for, made at launch,
to a third-party host, keyed to nothing the player controls. **A Ravel counterpart
must not implement it.** The cosmetic effect it gates may be dropped entirely; if
some equivalent is wanted, its data must be local. This should be recorded as a
deliberate divergence rather than an omission, because a parity test written from
the original's behaviour would otherwise flag its absence.

### 9.2 The analytics identifier

The source carries a constant holding a third-party web analytics property
identifier. The reader found no live use of it in the pinned source (**read** —
searched; **inferred** that it is vestigial). A counterpart must not carry it.
Whether the original still reports anywhere is **unknown** and was not
determined; the reader looked at the constant and its absence of local callers,
not at every code path.

## 10. What this specification does not specify

This is the incomplete one, and the incompleteness is structural rather than
accidental: the entry is a framework of roughly seven hundred and fifty source
files across four modules, and a specification complete enough to reimplement all
of it would be a book.

- **Everything in §8.** Named, not specified. Any counterpart work in those areas
  needs a further reading pass with its own rights record, and this document
  should not be treated as covering them.
- **Host-platform plumbing.** Deliberately not read beyond confirming that the
  pinned implementation supplies the same contract on several hosts. Those host
  bindings are not Ravel behaviour and no source-side arrangement is specified.
- **The source config-file category names** per kind (§2.2). Read, but several
  coincide with upstream identifiers, so they are not transcribed. Stable
  Ravel-native setting keys are the deliberate names-surface divergence.
- **The persisted-field key scheme** (§7), marked unknown.
- **The entry's own shipped content and options** — the debug item family, the
  particle, and the two developer-environment options. Their existence is recorded;
  their behavior is not specified and must not be invented.
- **The settings screen.** A configurable property declares whether it appears
  there; what "there" looks like is not specified.
- **Whether the analytics identifier is live** (§9.2), marked unknown.
- **Coverage.** Read in full: the general configuration, the configurable-setting
  declaration and its four locations, the published kind list, the command
  surface, the message framework and its field-encoding layer, and the
  field-persistence declaration. Read partially: lifecycle ordering, from a
  dependant and the pinned entry point. Everything else: not read.
