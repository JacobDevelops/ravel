# RVL-120 — behaviour specification

Wave: `waves/m6-foundation.json`. Method: source-informed reimplementation.
Rights: the upstream project's repository root carries an MIT licence file, read
at the source; see `provenance/m6-foundation/RVL-120.json` for the pinned
revision, the licence identity and the digest.

Confidence markers: **read**, **inferred**, **unknown**.

This entry is a foundation seed admitted on its own rubric, and it depends on
nothing else in the inventory (**read**, from the wave manifest). One other wave
entry, RVL-025, names it as its only dependency, and four entries outside this
wave — RVL-018, RVL-051, RVL-200 and RVL-293 — name it too. It is therefore the
deepest thing in its family: nothing in the wave has to exist before it, and five
admitted counterparts cannot start until it does. What those dependents actually
consume is §16, and §5 to §12 are what §16 rests on.

---

## 1. What this entry is

A shared library with no gameplay of its own. It ships no block, no item, no
recipe document, no tag document and no loot document, and it contributes exactly
two things a player could ever see: one key binding and one interface texture
(**read**). Everything else it does is machinery for the mods that declare it as
a dependency: registered data carriers, a network channel with a general-purpose
command transport, a per-player display preference record, a value-binding and
screen-synchronisation mechanism, a grouping service for connected blocks, and a
handful of exactly-specified arithmetic primitives.

It is large for what it publishes — 313 source files, of which 300 are the
library proper, 5 are copied third-party interface declarations kept for optional
integrations, 1 is a test and 7 are a demonstration mod that is not part of the
shipped artifact (**read**). Roughly a third of the library proper is client
presentation: a hand-rolled widget toolkit, its layout system, its event system
and its font handling account for 95 of the 300 files (**read**). That
proportion matters to scoping a counterpart, because Ravel draws its own screens
and none of those 95 files describe behaviour a dependent can observe from the
outside.

The entry declares itself mandatory and present on both sides, with no dependency
other than the loader itself (**read**).

### 1.1 Ravel disposition

Ravel already owns most of what this entry's structure exists to provide. Content
declaration and registration, configuration declaration and resolution, saved-data
ownership, side isolation and screen drawing are public Ravel facilities, and a
counterpart expresses the behaviour below through them rather than rebuilding
them. What survives as counterpart work is the decision rules, the wire contracts
and the portable arithmetic: §5 to §12.

Two areas are host-blocked at the reviewed public revision rather than declined.
They are named where they occur, and a counterpart records them rather than
stubbing them.

## 2. Side, lifecycle and what happens when

The entry runs on both sides and its behaviour splits cleanly (**read**):

- At construction it claims three registration groups (§4), and asks to be told
  about common setup, network payload registration, configuration load and
  configuration reload. On a client only, it asks for three further points: client
  setup, key-mapping registration, and client tooltip-component registration.
- At construction it also declares its two configuration documents (§3), one
  client-scoped and one server-scoped. Neither is common-scoped and neither is
  synchronised to a joining client (**read**).
- At common setup, on every side, it latches whether an optional in-game manual
  mod is present (§15) and begins observing the per-player pre-tick signal, which
  is what pushes §5 to clients.
- At client setup it begins observing screen input, tooltip rendering and the
  client tick.

In Ravel terms: the registration groups belong in `ContentDeclaration` and are
claimed through `Registrar`; the two configuration documents are one
`ConfigurationDeclaration` resolved before `configure`; the observed signals are
`Mod::observe`; and the client-only observers are declared client-only rather than
guarded by a runtime side test.

**Ordering that is load-bearing.** The client tooltip-component registration and
the key-mapping registration both happen before client setup, and the per-player
tick observer is added at common setup and not before, so the first preference
push to a client cannot precede common setup (**inferred**; the source states the
listener set but not the guarantee).

## 3. Configuration

Two documents, each with a single grouping, 73 settings between them (**read**).

**Server document — three settings.** A debug-logging boolean defaulting off; a
boolean defaulting on that decides whether blocks built on this library record who
placed them, which is what makes the security channel of §6 meaningful; and an
integer upper bound on how much of a single upgrade material one machine may
absorb, defaulting to 256 and constrained to the range 1 to the signed 32-bit
maximum inclusive.

**Client document — seventy settings.** Every one is a colour for one role in the
widget toolkit: border, background, gradient and text colours for buttons,
toggles, sliders, slider knobs, text fields, lists and energy bars, in their
normal, disabled, selected, hovering and dragging states. A counterpart that draws
its screens through Ravel does not need these as tuning knobs, but it needs them
as a *document shape* if it wants a pack author's existing file to load.

Three rules about these settings are behaviour rather than presentation
(**read**):

- Each colour is stored as a hexadecimal **string**, not a number, and the stored
  form is the low 24 bits of the default only. The alpha byte is dropped on the
  way out.
- On the way back in, the parsed value is forced fully opaque by adding an alpha
  of 255. A pack author therefore cannot make any of these seventy colours
  translucent, whatever they write.
- Parsing is unguarded. A value that is not valid hexadecimal raises rather than
  falling back to the default, and the raise happens during configuration
  resolution.

**A quirk a counterpart has to decide about.** Colours are re-resolved on two
occasions: when the *client* document loads, and whenever *any* document of this
entry reloads. The load path tests which document arrived; the reload path does
not (**read**). On a dedicated server, where the client document is declared but
never populated, reloading the server document therefore re-reads seventy client
values that have no loaded backing (**inferred** — the source neither guards this
case nor documents it, and this read did not run a server to see what happens).
A counterpart should scope the re-resolve to the client document on both paths and
record the divergence, rather than reproducing an unguarded read.

## 5. Per-player display preferences

A record attached to each player, persisted to that player's stored data and
pushed to that player's client. The attachment declares a serialisation codec and
nothing more, so the record is **not** carried across a death or a dimension
change (**read**); a parity test written against "persisted with the player"
will disagree with the original on the far side of a respawn. Four fields
(**read**): which corner of the screen status icons
anchor to, an x offset and a y offset both defaulting to -20, and which of the
widget toolkit's visual styles that player prefers, defaulting to a flat-gradient
style.

Observable contract (**read**):

- The record carries a dirty flag that is **set at construction**. The first
  server-side player tick after the record exists therefore always pushes it to
  the client, whether or not anything changed. This is how a joining client learns
  the settings at all: nothing else sends them.
- Every mutation sets the flag again; sending clears it. There is no batching and
  no rate limit beyond one send per player tick.
- The reset path does **not** restore the constructed defaults. It sets the anchor
  corner to top-left, where a freshly constructed record anchors bottom-right
  (**read**). The offsets and the style do return to their defaults. A counterpart
  that treats reset as "construct a new one" produces a different anchor, and a
  parity test comparing a reset record against a fresh one will see it.
- The style setter accepts a name and refuses an unknown one by returning false
  without changing anything; the enumeration setter refuses only a missing value.

Only the server writes this record. The client receives it and applies it; there
is no client-to-server path for the offsets, and a single client-to-server payload
for the style alone (§9).

## 6. Common data every dependent's block carries

One record shape, registered twice: as an attachment on a block entity and as a
data component on an item. The record itself declares both a persistence codec
and a network codec, and each registration takes only what it needs: the
attachment declares the persistence codec alone, the item component declares
both (**read**). The four fields are the same either way:

- the name of the player who placed it, as a string, empty when unowned;
- that player's stable identity, optional and absent when unowned;
- a security channel as a signed integer, **-1 when unassigned**;
- a redstone mode, one of three: ignore redstone, require it low, require it high.

Two things here are wire contracts rather than incidental (**read**):

- **In saved data** the redstone mode serialises as a short display word rather
  than as the name of its enumeration constant, and the three words are not the
  three constant names (**read**). The exact spellings are **unknown to this
  document**: they are upstream serialised names, the names surface is
  unresolved, and this document maps none of them (§17). A counterpart that
  needs to read the original's saves must obtain the three spellings from the
  pinned artifact at build time rather than from here; one that writes the
  constant names instead writes saves the original cannot read, and a saved-data
  round trip will catch it.
- **On the wire** the same field takes a different form. The record's network
  codec routes it through an ordinal-based enumeration stream codec, so a packet
  carries a small integer and neither spelling is written (**read**). The display
  word is a persistence contract only. The ordinal layout is a wire contract in
  its own right: a counterpart that reorders the three constants breaks packets
  even where its saved spellings match.
- The optional identity field is encoded as an optional, so an unowned block
  round-trips through both save and network without inventing a placeholder
  identity. The name field is *not* optional and carries the empty string
  instead, which means the two fields can disagree: a record with a name and no
  identity is representable and is what the default constructs.

The default value is empty name, no identity, channel -1, redstone ignored.
Whether ownership is recorded at all is the server setting of §3.

## 7. The command transport dependents build on

The single most-used facility. A dependent declares named commands on its block
entity and calls them from its screen; this entry carries the call, runs it on the
authoritative side and carries any answer back.

Three command shapes (**read**):

1. **Fire and forget.** Runs on the server against the block entity, the calling
   player and a bag of typed arguments. Returns nothing.
2. **With a result.** Runs on the server, returns a bag of typed values, and a
   second handler runs on the client when it arrives.
3. **With a list.** Runs on the server, returns a list of one element type, and a
   client handler receives the list.

Registration and dispatch (**read**):

- A command is declared as a static field on the block entity type and marked, so
  the mechanism finds it by inspecting the type rather than by an explicit
  registration call. The marking may name the element type of a list command and
  a helper that knows how to encode it.
- Five element types need no helper because encoders are built in: signed
  integer, string, block position, item stack and fluid stack, which is the
  exempt set the marking's own documentation lists. For anything else the scan
  instantiates the encoder class the marking declares, and that field's default
  value is the encoder interface itself. Instantiating an interface fails, and
  the surrounding catch raises an explicit illegal-state error naming the
  failure (**read**). It raises when the block-entity type is first inspected,
  not at the first call of the command, so a missing helper is a startup fault
  and not a runtime one.
- Alongside the per-block-entity commands there are global commands, and the two
  keys they are filed under are not the same shape. A global command is keyed by
  the pair of owning namespace and command id, and the side it runs on is part of
  where it is filed, so a server command and a client command may share one key
  without colliding. A list command's element type and codec are keyed by the
  bare command name, with no namespace. This is the original's storage layout;
  the observable consequence is the key difference itself: a name that two
  namespaces both declare for a list command is one key in the element lookup
  and two distinct keys among the commands, so the two namespaces disagree about
  the element codec while both of their commands survive.
- **A duplicate key replaces silently** wherever a declaration is filed, and an
  unknown key returns false rather than raising (**read**). A counterpart should
  keep the return-false contract, because dependents branch on it, and should
  make the silent replacement diagnosable in both places one declaration touches,
  which is a deliberate divergence.

**Host-blocked**: nothing Ravel publishes lets a mod discover another mod's
statically declared commands by inspection. A counterpart declares its commands
explicitly at its own declaration point, and the guarantee that a command exists
before it is called is met by declaration order rather than by discovery.

## 8. Values, and how a screen stays current

A block entity exposes an ordered map of named values, each with a reader and a
writer. A screen opened on that block entity subscribes to the map, and the
subscription behaves as follows (**read**):

- On each check it walks the map and compares every current value against a
  remembered copy. **The first difference makes the whole set dirty**; it then
  re-copies every value and reports dirty once. There is no per-value dirty
  tracking.
- When dirty, it writes every value in map order, each with the encoder its
  declared type provides. The reader walks the map in the same order and assigns
  positionally.

That positional encoding is the load-bearing part, and it is required rather than
incidental: the wire carries no names and no count, so the two sides agree only
because they walk the same map in the same order. A counterpart must give the map
a deterministic order that does not depend on hash iteration, and must treat a
change to the declared value set as a wire-format change (**inferred** — the
source relies on the order without stating the requirement).

Alongside the map there are single-value subscriptions for a string and for a
float, and small adapters that present an integer, a short, an enumeration or a
boolean through the host's own narrow screen-data channel. The enumeration adapter
indexes its value array by the received number with no bounds check, so a
malformed or mismatched value from the other side raises (**read**). A
counterpart validates and clamps, and records the divergence.

## 9. The network surface

One channel, declared under this entry's own namespace, version string `1.0`, and
**optional**, meaning a client without the mod may still connect (**read**).

Thirteen payload kinds (**read**):

| direction | count | what they carry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| both ways | 1 | opaque data addressed to the open screen |
| client to server | 6 | style choice; open-the-manual request; list request; typed command; global command; data request |
| server to client | 6 | preferences; screen data; command result; global client command; requested data; login-finalised |

Three of those need their contract stated:

- **The opaque screen payload** carries a target identifier and a raw block of
  bytes, length-prefixed with a fixed 32-bit count rather than a variable-length
  one. On arrival it is routed to the player's currently open screen and is
  **dropped silently** if that screen is not one this library manages (**read**).
  The bytes are re-wrapped for decoding with a connection type that says "not one
  of ours", which disables the loader's own protocol-aware decoding for that
  buffer (**inferred**; the source picks the value without explaining it).
- **The login-finalised payload** exists so that sends deferred until after login
  can proceed. At this revision **its handler does nothing** (**read**). It is
  registered, sent and inert. A counterpart should not ship it, and should say so
  rather than reproducing a no-op packet.
- **The list request** and the **typed command** are the transport for §7. Their
  wire form is the command name plus the argument bag; the element codec is
  looked up by command name at decode time, so a client that has not registered
  the element type cannot decode a list it asked for (**inferred**).

Nothing here is authenticated beyond the host's own connection: any client may
send any global command id, and the handler that dispatches it decides. A
counterpart must treat the command name and the argument bag as untrusted input
and validate before acting on the calling player's behalf. That is a security
requirement this specification adds rather than one the original states.

## 10. Global saved data

One helper: data that belongs to a save as a whole rather than to a dimension. It
is always stored against the overworld's storage regardless of which dimension the
caller is in, keyed by a caller-supplied name, and created from a supplied factory
when absent (**read**).

It **raises on any client-side access** rather than returning empty (**read**).
That is deliberate and a counterpart should keep the refusal, because the data has
no client-side meaning and a silent empty would look like a wiped save. In Ravel
this is `SavedData` ownership with a server-only accessor; the overworld pinning
is expressed as one saved-data scope rather than a per-dimension one.

## 11. Grouping connected blocks

A service dependents use to treat a run of touching blocks as one thing. Each
participating block stores a group id; the service owns the groups.

Identity and persistence (**read**):

- Ids come from a counter that only increases and never reuses. The counter is
  persisted alongside the groups, so ids stay unique across a restart.
- A save holds the list of groups, each tagged with its id, plus the last id
  issued.

Placing a block (**read**): a fresh group is created for it, the block is stamped
with that id, and then the merge below runs.

Merging, on the six face neighbours (**read**):

- **No neighbour in a group**: nothing happens.
- **Exactly one neighbouring group**: if the dependent's compatibility test
  accepts the pair, this block joins that group. If it refuses, nothing happens
  and the block keeps its own group. The test is called here with the
  **neighbouring group first and this block's group second** (**read**).
- **More than one neighbouring group**: every compatible neighbour is merged
  into this block's group, and then a flood fill re-stamps everything reachable
  through the union of those neighbouring ids with this block's id. The test is
  called here with the **arguments in the opposite order**, this block's group
  first and the neighbour second (**read**).

The two orders are the second finding of this section. A test that is not
symmetric answers differently in the one-neighbour and the many-neighbour case,
and nothing in the source requires the dependent's test to be symmetric. A
counterpart should either call the test in one fixed order or require symmetry
of it explicitly, and state which it chose.

That third case does not match the second, and the mismatch is the finding of this
section: **the flood fill runs over the union of all adjacent ids whether or not
the compatibility test accepted them**, so a neighbour the test refused is
absorbed anyway as soon as a third group is touching (**inferred** — the code
adds every adjacent id to the set it re-stamps, and the merge result is not
consulted; the source does not say this is intended). A counterpart should
re-stamp only the ids that merged, and record the divergence, because reproducing
the original here means reproducing a rule that contradicts its own two-block
case.

Removing a block (**read**): the service recomputes membership around the gap
rather than dropping the block from its group. It walks out from each of the six
neighbours and stamps everything reachable through that neighbour's own id with
-1, clearing the whole region. It then walks the neighbours a second time and,
for each one still unstamped, starts a fresh group over the set reachable from
it with the removed block's own position held out of the walk. The **first** such
group reuses the removed block's own id; each later one takes a newly issued id.
The resulting list of pairs, each an id and the positions that now carry it, is
handed to the dependent's fixer, which owns how the original group's contents are
divided among them. This is the only place a group id is ever reused.

Three consequences a counterpart owes (**inferred** — the code does this and the
source does not say it is intended): a reused id outlives the block that carried
it and afterwards names a different set of blocks, so an id is not a stable
handle across a removal; between the two passes every touched block reads as -1,
so anything that inspects membership during a removal sees no group at all; and
the clearing walk carries no visited set, unlike the reassigning one, so it
revisits positions it has already stamped.

Two further limits (**inferred**): the flood fill and the group-discovery walk are
both recursive over neighbours with no depth bound, so a sufficiently large group
exhausts the stack; and the merge reads the world through a supplied lookup with
no loaded-chunk test, so a group spanning a chunk boundary behaves differently
depending on what is loaded. A counterpart should make both iterative and
explicit.

## 12. Arithmetic and data primitives that must match exactly

These are the parts where the specific algorithm *is* the behaviour, because
values computed by one side are compared against values computed by another.

### 12.1 The 32-bit change check

Dependents summarise a block of data into one 32-bit value and send it, so the
other side can tell whether its cached copy is stale. The value must match
bit for bit.

The construction (**read**): a reflected cyclic-redundancy accumulator over the
polynomial `0xEDB88320`, seeded to all ones and bit-inverted when read. Each step
folds an entire 32-bit word into the accumulator with exclusive-or, then runs
eight rounds of the standard shift-and-conditional-xor.

**It is not the textbook 32-bit cyclic redundancy check, and the difference is
not a detail.** The accumulator is a *signed* 32-bit integer and the shift is
arithmetic, so the sign bit propagates instead of a zero entering at the top. A
counterpart written from the textbook — or written in a language whose right shift
on an unsigned type is logical — produces different values for the same input, and
every staleness comparison against a dependent's expectation fails. In Rust this
means a signed 32-bit accumulator with the ordinary right shift, not an unsigned
one.

The convenience path folds the element **count first** and then each element, so
two sequences that differ only in length cannot collide trivially (**read**).

### 12.2 The 64-bit change check

A second, unrelated checksum for the same purpose at wider width (**read**): a
256-entry table-driven 64-bit cyclic redundancy, most-significant-byte-first,
seeded to zero and **not** inverted on read. It consumes one byte at a time and
carries two integer entry points beside that: a four-byte one that feeds the word
least significant byte first, and a single-argument one that silently keeps only
the low byte and discards the other three (**read**). A counterpart that ports
the pair by name folds different data. Its convenience path also folds the count
first, then each element.

The two checks share no code, no seed convention and no bit order, and a
counterpart must not unify them (**read**). The tables and constants are the
behaviour; a counterpart derives its table from the stated polynomial and pins it
with vectors rather than transcribing anything.

Both checks should be pinned in a counterpart against ground-truth vectors
produced by running the original on a fixed input set, not against a
reimplementation of the same rule.

### 12.3 The energy clamp

Energy is stored as a 64-bit value but several interfaces the entry has to talk to
accept only 32 bits. The clamp saturates at the signed 32-bit maximum above the
range and otherwise **truncates to the low 32 bits** (**read**). Only the upper
bound is guarded: a negative input passes through unchanged rather than clamping
to zero, and a value below the signed 32-bit minimum wraps.

The reporting helper takes both a stored amount and a capacity and **ignores the
capacity**, returning the plain clamp (**read**). A fuller scheme that would report
a usable approximation for very large capacities is present in the source but
disabled, and its own comment calls it too risky. A counterpart implements the
plain clamp, states that the capacity argument is unused, and does not implement
the disabled scheme.

### 12.4 Run-length coding

A compact encoding for long runs of small values, used where a large uniform
region is sent or saved (**read**):

- The output is a flat sequence of byte pairs: a repeat count, then the value.
- A run is capped at 255 and continues in a new pair beyond that. Values are
  written as single bytes, so the coder is defined only for values that fit in a
  byte, and the source neither checks nor documents that.
- **Reading past the end returns zero forever** rather than signalling
  exhaustion. Callers therefore have to know how many values they expect. A
  counterpart should expose an explicit end and keep a zero-filling reader only
  where a dependent relies on it.
- On the wire the whole buffer is one optional length-prefixed byte block.

### 12.5 Smaller primitives

- **A string interner** handing out ids from 1 upward, process-wide, never
  released and never persisted. Ids are therefore stable only within one run and
  must not be saved (**inferred**; the source neither says so nor prevents it).
- **Floor and ceiling helpers** that round toward negative and positive infinity
  respectively, differing from truncation for negative inputs. The floor helper
  has both a single- and a double-precision form; the ceiling helper is single
  precision only (**read**).
- **A 24-value orientation enumeration** for blocks that have both a mounting
  face and a facing within it: each value names its input side and a rotation
  step, the mounting face is the ordinal divided by four, and rotating cycles the
  low two bits within the group of four. A counterpart must keep the ordinal
  layout, because rotation is defined arithmetically over it and the serialised
  form is the value's own name.

## 13. The recipe kind

One crafting recipe kind: a shaped recipe that carries chosen item data from an
ingredient onto its result (**read**).

Observable contract (**read**):

- Declaratively it is an ordinary shaped recipe nested whole under a single
  field, so a pack author writes the familiar shape and changes only the kind.
- At assembly, every ingredient in the grid is examined. An ingredient whose item
  — or, for a placeable item, whose block — declares a set of data kinds to
  preserve contributes each of those it actually carries onto the result.
- **All matching ingredients contribute, in grid order, and later ones overwrite
  earlier ones for the same data kind** (**inferred** — the loop does not stop at
  the first match and does not merge). A recipe with two such ingredients is
  therefore order-dependent, and the source neither prevents nor documents it.
- Absent data is skipped rather than cleared, so the result keeps whatever the
  base recipe gave it.
- The recipe is filed in the miscellaneous crafting-book category, which the
  source marks as an unresolved guess (**read**).

## 14. Client input and the manual

One key binding, in this entry's own category, defaulting to F1 and active only
while a screen is open (**read**). Pressing it asks the host to open the in-game
manual at the page the current screen suggests, through the optional integration
of §15.

The binding is **polled on every client tick with a direct device query**, not
consumed as a press event, and the poll has no edge detection (**read**). Holding
the key therefore re-triggers the open on every tick. A counterpart should treat
it as a press and record the divergence.

Separately, the entry intercepts screen mouse and keyboard input at the highest
priority so that a modal window inside one of its screens receives events before
the underlying screen does, cancelling the original event when the modal or a
focused text field consumes it (**read**). This is presentation, and a counterpart
drawing through Ravel's own screens does not reproduce it.

## 15. Optional integrations

Four, all optional, none required for the entry to load (**read**): an in-game
manual mod, a hover-information probe, a hover-information tooltip mod, and a
recipe viewer. The 5 third-party interface declarations counted in §1 exist to
compile against these.

Only the manual integration's presence is latched, and it is latched **once** at
common setup and never re-checked (**read**). A counterpart may re-check freely,
since nothing depends on the latch being stable, but should keep the rule that
absence is not an error.

**Host-blocked**: Ravel publishes no facility for optional cross-mod integration
at this revision, so a counterpart records these four as pending rather than
stubbing them.

## 16. What dependents need

Five admitted entries name this one. RVL-025 is in this wave, is itself a
foundation seed, and names this entry as its **only** dependency, so it is the
first consumer a counterpart will be measured against. RVL-018, RVL-051 and
RVL-293 each name this entry together with RVL-025; RVL-200 names this entry
alone (**read**, from the plan rows).

Reading their plan rows against the sections above, the surface they consume is:

- **The command transport (§7) and the network surface (§9).** All five dependents
  carry `networking-probe` in their test sets, and four of the five are
  client-presentation entries whose screens exist to call commands on block
  entities. This is the single largest thing a counterpart owes them.
- **The value map and its screen synchronisation (§8).** Every dependent screen
  reads block-entity state through it, and the positional wire form means the
  counterpart's ordering rule has to be settled before any dependent is written,
  not after.
