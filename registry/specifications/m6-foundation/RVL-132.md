# RVL-132 — behaviour specification

Wave: `waves/m6-foundation.json`. Method: source-informed reimplementation.
Rights: the upstream project's repository root carries an MIT licence file, read
at the source; see `provenance/m6-foundation/RVL-132.json` for the pinned
revision, the licence identity and the digest.

Confidence markers: **read**, **inferred**, **unknown**.

This entry is a foundation seed. It depends on exactly one other inventory entry,
RVL-210, and on the game and the loader (**read**, from the pinned dependency
graph and the entry's own descriptor). Four mods in the pinned pack declare it as
a required dependency on both sides (**read**); §16 is what they observe.

RVL-210 ships compiled inside its own artifact the interface vocabulary that this
entry's project publishes separately and that both artifacts compile against. The
wave has decided that vocabulary is owned by RVL-210's counterpart. This document
therefore states what RVL-132 consumes of it and does not respecify it; RVL-210
§10 is where the vocabulary itself is described, and RVL-210 §17 is the census
this document was asked to recount against the pinned build.

---

## 1. What this entry is

A bridging library with no gameplay of its own. It ships no block, no item, no
recipe document, no tag document, no loot document, no advancement document, no
texture, no model, no sound and no screen (**read**). A player can encounter it
only indirectly: it makes vanilla containers, stations and dropped items answer
questions that only modded machines ask.

It does two separable things (**read**):

1. It declares **three ingredient kinds** — a stackable item, a fluid portion and
   a scalar charge — into the shared registry RVL-210 §10's vocabulary defines,
   giving each one a matcher, a serialiser and a decomposition into comparable
   parts (§4 to §7).
2. It **attaches capabilities to vanilla game objects** that vanilla does not
   attach itself: a work indicator, a heat reading, a recipe view, and item,
   fluid and charge stores on objects that carry an item but publish no store of
   their own (§9 to §12).

**Size.** The pinned artifact is built from 52 portable source files, all on both
sides (**read**). Six of those 52 are the in-game test harness's own classes:
they ship inside the artifact and are counted here, and §17 excludes only the six
separate one-file companion mods the harness drives, which are not shipped.
Beside the 52 the repository carries 35 unit-test files and those six companion
mods. Its shipped resources are 30 translation documents, 1 empty structure
template used by the harness, 2 logo images that are branding, the pack
descriptor, the mod descriptor and one access-widening document. Counted twice
from the tree.

The entry declares itself present on both sides, with the game, the loader and
RVL-210 as its only requirements (**read**).

### 1.1 Ravel disposition

Most of what this entry does is what Ravel exists to make unnecessary. Its whole
purpose is that vanilla objects do not publish the interfaces modded machines
want, so it reaches into them and supplies the answers. Two thirds of the source
is the reaching: delegating to a store held inside a dropped item, widening access
to fields the game keeps private, reflecting a heat value out of a burn timer.

A counterpart expresses the same observable answers through Ravel's own object
capability facility, and does not rebuild the vocabulary of §5, which RVL-210's
counterpart owns.

What survives as counterpart work is what a dependent can see and would break
without: the three ingredient kinds and their matching, ordering and hashing
(§6), the serialised forms (§7), the adapter table between store shapes (§8),
the attachment table (§9), and the recipe views (§11).

Three things are host-blocked rather than declined, and a counterpart records
them rather than stubbing them: the access widening of §13.1, the shipped class
declared in the host's own namespace of §13.2, and the fact that a capability
attached to an object the host owns is a Ravel facility rather than a portable
one.

## 2. Side, lifecycle and what happens when

The entry runs on both sides, and nothing in it is client-only (**read**).

- **At construction** it asks the loader for three lifecycle points, each at a
  deliberately late priority: registry construction, registry population and the
  cross-mod message point (**read**). Common setup, settings registration and
  compatibility loading reach it through the declaration framework of §5
  instead, not through a listener it adds here.
- **At settings registration** it declares its one settings document (§3)
  (**read**).
- **At registry construction**, at a deliberately late priority within that
  event, it installs its two alternative-set serialisers into the shared
  serialiser table RVL-210 §10's vocabulary publishes. This is the only thing it
  contributes to that table (**read**).
- **At registry population**, again at a late priority, it registers its three
  ingredient kinds into the shared ingredient-kind registry under three names
  (**read**). The late priority matters: a dependent that registers a kind of its
  own is free to run earlier, and nothing here depends on ordering against one.
- **At common setup**, on both sides, it asserts that all three kinds resolved
  and fails loudly if any did not, then subscribes every registered kind — its
  own three and any a dependent added — to the loader's capability-gathering
  event. A dependent's kind therefore gets its capability gathering wired by this
  entry rather than by the dependent (**read**). That is a contract, not an
  implementation detail: a counterpart that only wires its own three would
  silently break a dependent's kind.
- **At the cross-mod message point** it populates the storage-adapter table of
  §8. This is late on purpose: an adapter names a capability, and capabilities are
  not all present earlier (**inferred**; the source states the load point and
  states no reason).
- **At its compatibility-loading point** it registers two compatibility bundles:
  one for vanilla, always enabled, and one for a third-party crafting-station mod,
  enabled when that mod is present (§9).
- **When a server stops** it clears both recipe caches of §14.

**Ordering that is load-bearing.** The adapters of §8 are installed after common
setup, and the serialisers of §7 before registry population. A dependent that
asks for a storage adapter during its own setup has no entry to read
(**inferred**).

## 3. Configuration

The entry declares one settings document holding six properties, counted twice
(**read**). Three are server-scoped and three are not.

Server-scoped:

- whether an invalid recipe encountered while enumerating should abort loading
  rather than be skipped;
- whether a failure inside a compatibility bundle should abort loading rather
  than be logged;
- a list of data-component kind names to ignore when deciding whether two items
  carry equal data. This is the only setting a pack author is likely to touch,
  and it changes matching results, so it is behaviour (§6.4).

Not server-scoped:

- whether an anonymous startup ping may be sent;
- whether an update check may be made;
- whether every unequal-data comparison should be logged, marked in its own
  description as a performance hazard.

The third-party list is re-read on every settings load and reload, and the
comparator of §6.4 is rebuilt from it each time, so the setting takes effect
without a restart (**read**).

**Two of these six describe outbound network behaviour.** The startup ping and
the update check are both on by default, and the update check fetches a document
whose address is assembled from the running game version (**read**). A
counterpart does not reproduce either. That is a design decision, not a rights
one: Ravel does not ship code that contacts a third-party service by default, and
RVL-210 §9 records the same decision for a heavier case in its own entry. The
divergence belongs in the counterpart's notes so it is not mistaken for an
omission.

## 4. What it registers

**Three ingredient kinds and nothing else** (**read**). No block, item, entity,
menu, particle, effect, trigger, argument kind, data component kind or loot
modifier is registered anywhere in the tree, and no deferred register exists.

The three are:

- **a stackable item**, whose quantity is a count;
- **a fluid portion**, whose quantity is a volume;
- **a scalar charge**, whose quantity is the charge itself.

Each is registered under a name in the game's own namespace rather than the
entry's (**read**). Those three names are storable by a dependent and by a saved
world, which is exactly what the names surface in the provenance record does not
clear, so this document does not write them. A counterpart that must be
save-compatible reads them from the pinned artifact; a counterpart that need not
be picks its own.

Each kind also carries a translation key and a decomposition into **category
types**: three for the item kind (its identity, its count, its data), three for
the fluid kind (its fluid, its volume, its data) and one for the charge kind (the
charge). Seven in total, counted twice. A category type says whether it is the
kind's primary axis, how to project an instance onto it, which match flag selects
it, and whether it is the quantitative one. Dependents index by these, so the
count and the roles are behaviour (**read**).

## 6. How the three kinds match, hash and order

This is the part dependents depend on most closely, because a machine's filter,
its search index and its sorted display all run through it.

### 6.1 Match conditions

The item and fluid kinds carry an **integer flag set**; the charge kind carries a
**single boolean**. Both are treated through one interface, so a dependent writes
one filter for all three (**read**).

For the flag-set kinds: combining is bitwise or, removing is bitwise and-not, and
testing is a non-empty bitwise and. Testing therefore answers yes when *any*
requested flag is present, not when all are (**read**). That is the semantics a
counterpart must keep; it is what makes a partial filter widen rather than narrow.

For the boolean kind: combining is or, testing is equality, and removing yields
false when the two agree and the original otherwise (**read**). The removal rule
is asymmetric with the other two and is very likely unintended (§15).

Each kind names three standard conditions: match anything, match exactly, and
match exactly but ignore quantity. For the item kind the last is identity plus
data; for the fluid kind, fluid plus data; for the charge kind it is the same as
match-anything, because a charge has nothing but its quantity (**read**).

### 6.2 Quantity

Every kind reports a quantity as a 64-bit value and can be rebuilt with a
different one. The item kind's maximum and the fluid kind's are both the largest
32-bit signed value; the charge kind's is the largest 64-bit signed value
(**read**).

Rebuilding is defined to return the same instance when the quantity is unchanged,
and a copy otherwise (**read**). A dependent that mutates a returned instance
therefore mutates the original in the unchanged case. That aliasing is behaviour a
counterpart must either keep or eliminate deliberately; Ravel's value types make
eliminating it the natural choice, and the divergence is safe because no caller
can distinguish a copy from an alias except by mutating one.

Rebuilding the fluid kind at zero yields the empty portion, and rebuilding an
*empty* portion at a non-zero quantity yields a portion of the game's default
fluid rather than an error (**read**). That fallback is arbitrary and a
counterpart should refuse instead (§15).

### 6.3 Hashing and ordering

Each kind supplies a hash and a total order, and dependents key maps and sort
displays with them (**read**).

- **The item kind** orders empty before non-empty; then by identity when the
  identities differ, using the game's numeric item index rather than a name; then
  by count; then by the data comparison of §6.4.
- **The fluid kind** orders empty before non-empty; then by fluid name when the
  fluids differ, comparing the registered names as text; then by volume; then by
  the data comparison.
- **The charge kind** orders by difference.

The two orders are not built the same way: one falls back to a numeric registry
index and the other to a name. The numeric index is not stable across differing
mod sets, so an order built on it is stable within one game session and not
between two (**read**, the mechanism; **inferred**, the consequence). A
counterpart orders both by name and records the divergence, because a dependent
that persists a sorted order would otherwise see it change under an unrelated mod
being added.

The fluid kind's hash folds fluid, volume and data, skipping data when empty. The
item kind's hash comes from RVL-210's helper. Two instances that compare equal
under the exact condition hash equally in both cases (**inferred**; the source
asserts nothing and the unit tests cover only examples).

### 6.4 Comparing item and fluid data

Both stackable items and fluid portions carry a map of typed data components, and
comparing two of them is one routine shared by both (**read**). It is worth
stating in full because a pack-visible setting changes its answer.

1. Identical or equal maps compare equal immediately.
2. The ignore list from §3 is resolved once, on first use, from names to
   component kinds and cached. Either side's key set is filtered only if it
   actually holds an ignored kind.
3. If the surviving key sets differ, the two sets are rendered as sorted lists of
   component-kind names and compared as text, shorter set first on a common
   prefix.
4. Otherwise each shared key's values are compared: by their natural order when
   the value type has one, and otherwise by identity hash.

Step 4's fallback is the important one. Two structurally equal values of a type
with no natural order compare unequal whenever they are distinct objects, so
equality of such items is not stable across a copy (**read**). This is the defect
the debug-logging setting of §3 exists to diagnose, which is evidence upstream
knows of it (**inferred**). A counterpart compares such values structurally.

The resolved ignore list is cached on first use and never invalidated, while the
setting that feeds it is reloadable, so a reload rebuilds the comparator but a
comparator that has already resolved its list keeps it. The rebuild in §3 is what
makes this harmless, and only because the whole comparator is replaced
(**inferred**).

## 7. Serialised forms

Each kind serialises an instance and a match condition to the game's tag format,
and reads both back. These forms are storable by a dependent and by a saved
world, so they are a compatibility surface and are specified exactly (**read**).

| kind | instance | condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| stackable item | the game's own optional item encoding, plus an escape field (below) | a signed 32-bit integer |
| fluid portion | the game's own optional fluid encoding, or an empty compound when the portion is empty | a signed 32-bit integer |
| scalar charge | a signed 64-bit integer | a byte, 1 for true and 0 for false |

**The item kind's count escape.** The game's own item encoding caps a count at 99.
When the count exceeds 99, the entry writes the item with its count clamped to 99
and adds one extra integer field carrying the true count; on reading, that field
replaces the count when present (**read**). The field name is a compatibility
requirement and is obtainable from the pinned artifact rather than written here.
A counterpart must implement both halves: a reader that ignores the field sees 99
silently, with no error, which is a data-loss path rather than a decode failure.

**Tolerances read from the decoders**: the charge decoder accepts a 32-bit
integer as well as a 64-bit one, and widens it, for backwards compatibility with
an older form. Every other decoder rejects a tag of the wrong shape with an
error. The item decoder additionally requires a compound and rejects anything
else, even though the value it parsed came back fine (**read**).

**Round-trip guarantee.** Instance and condition round-trip for all three kinds,
with two exceptions a counterpart must preserve or fix deliberately: a fluid
portion's empty case is written as an empty compound and read back through the
general path, and an item count above 99 round-trips only through the escape
field.

## 8. The store-adapter table

A dependent asks this entry for a store of a given ingredient kind on a given
game object, and gets one whether or not the object publishes that kind natively.
Twelve adapters are installed, counted twice (**read**): the charge kind, the
item kind in a slot-addressed flavour, the item kind in a slotless flavour and
the fluid kind, each on three object classes — a block, an item and an entity.

Each adapter is bidirectional (**read**):

- **From the host's store to the kind's store.** Iteration yields the host's
  contents; a filtered iteration wraps it with the matcher of §6; the capacity is
  the sum of the per-slot or per-tank capacities; insertion and extraction
  delegate.
- **From the kind's store to the host's store.** A slot-addressed source is
  wrapped as a slot-addressed host store, one-to-one. A slotless source is
  wrapped as a host store that reports **one more slot than it holds**, so the
  last index always reads
  empty and can be inserted into. That is deliberate, not a defect: a host store
  interface has no insert-anywhere operation, so the phantom slot is the only way
  to expose one (**inferred**; the source states no reason). A counterpart with a
  slotless insert does not need the phantom slot and should not have one.

Three behaviours in the table are load-bearing and easy to lose (**read**):

- **The combined item store.** When an object publishes both a slot-addressed
  item store and a slotless one, the adapter uses the slotless one for iteration,
  filtering, capacity and unslotted access, and keeps the slot-addressed one for
  everything indexed. A dependent gets the cheaper answer without asking.
- **Extraction by prototype from a slot-addressed store** walks slots, matches
  each against the prototype under the given flags, and accumulates across slots
  until the requested quantity is met, extracting for real only once the whole
  quantity is available. A partial match is not committed. That all-or-nothing
  rule is what a machine's ingredient reservation depends on.
- **A missing capability yields an empty store, not an error** (**read**).

The charge adapter narrows a 64-bit quantity to 32 bits on every insert and
extract, because the host's charge interface is 32-bit (§15).

## 9. What it attaches, to what, on which side

Two compatibility bundles, both enabled by default and both individually
disableable through RVL-210's settings framework (**read**). Everything below is
on both sides.

**The vanilla bundle makes 46 attachments**, counted twice:

| capability attached | game objects | count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| work indicator | the three smelting stations, the brewing station, the campfire | 5 |
| heat reading | the three smelting stations, the campfire, and every bucket-like item by class | 5 |
| item store | 17 coloured and uncoloured portable container items, the carrying pouch item, the dropped-item entity, the two picture-frame entities, and the composting block | 22 |
| fluid store | the dropped-item entity and the two picture-frame entities | 3 |
| charge store | the dropped-item entity and the two picture-frame entities | 3 |
| recipe view | the brewing station, the three smelting stations, the campfire, every crafting-table block by class, the cutting block and the upgrade block | 8 |

**The third-party bundle makes one further attachment**, a recipe view over that
mod's crafting station block entity, present only when that mod is (**read**).

Three of these six capability kinds — the work indicator, the heat reading and
the recipe view — come from the vocabulary RVL-210 owns. The other three are the
host platform's own (**read**).

**The delegation rule for the three entity attachments.** A dropped item and a
picture frame hold an item, and each of the three stores is attached to the
entity only if the item it holds publishes the same store; every operation then
forwards to the item's store, re-reading the held item on each call, and writes
the item back after a mutating call (**read**). Two consequences a counterpart
must keep: the entity's store appears and disappears as the held item changes,
and a caller holding the entity's store across a change sees the new item rather
than a stale one.

## 10. Work and heat

Both are single-value readings a dependent polls; neither has any state of its
own (**read**).

**Work** answers two questions: is there work to do, and can work proceed.

- The smelting stations: work exists when the input slot is non-empty, a recipe
  matches it, and assembling that recipe yields a non-empty result. Work can
  proceed when the station is already burning or its fuel slot holds something
  that burns.
- The brewing station: work exists when its current contents are brewable. Work
  can proceed when its fuel count is above zero.
- The campfire: work exists when any of its slots is non-empty. Work can proceed
  when the block is lit.

**Heat** answers a current, a minimum, a maximum and a default, all as absolute
temperatures offset from the freezing point of water, which is a constant the
vocabulary publishes.

- The smelting stations report the freezing point plus the raw remaining burn
  ticks, with no maximum. That is a tick count read as a temperature, and it is
  not a physical quantity (**read**). It is nevertheless the number every
  dependent has been reading, so a counterpart reproduces it.
- The campfire reports the freezing point plus 1100 when lit and the freezing
  point otherwise, with the lit value as its maximum.
- A bucket-like item reports the temperature its contained fluid declares, and the
  freezing point when empty, with no maximum.

## 11. Recipe views

A recipe view answers two questions: enumerate the recipes this station can run,
and given a set of inputs, what would come out. The eight vanilla attachments of
§9 all answer both: seven answer them from the world's own recipe collection and
the brewing station answers them from a derived closure, because brewing has no
recipes as documents (**read**).

**The general station view** covers seven of the eight vanilla attachments. It is
built with the recipe kind, a predicate on the input count, a way to build the
recipe's input from a grid, whether empty slots are dropped before matching, and
whether the output must be checked as well as the input.

- Enumeration filters the world's whole recipe collection to that kind, drops
  recipes marked special, converts each through the vocabulary's own converter,
  drops conversion failures, and caches the result (§14).
- Simulation from inputs builds a 3×3 transient grid, fills it in order, refuses
  when the inputs name more than one ingredient kind or are empty after the
  optional empty-slot filter, then asks RVL-210's cached lookup and returns the
  matching recipe's result.
- Simulation from a recipe definition, when output checking is on, additionally
  requires the definition's output to name exactly one ingredient kind and
  exactly one instance, and then scans for a recipe matching both input and
  output. Only the cutting block turns this on, because it is the one station
  where one input has several outputs (**inferred**; the source states the flag
  and not the reason).

Building a 3×3 grid for every station is a shortcut: a station with one input slot
gets an eight-slot grid it never reads. It costs an allocation per simulation and
is invisible from outside.

**The brewing view** does not enumerate the world's recipes, because brewing has
none as documents. It computes the closure instead (§11.1). Its simulation mixes
the given inputs in order, each against the running result, then blanks the first
position, and returns the three remaining positions.

Its input-count predicate is a defect: it admits every size (§15).

### 11.1 Enumerating brewing

Brewing recipes are not documents, so the entry derives them (**read**):

1. Start from a single water-filled bottle.
2. Collect every ingredient named by the two brewing tables the game holds, one
   for container changes and one for potion changes, expanding each to its
   concrete alternatives.
3. Breadth-first, mix every collected ingredient against every item at the
   current frontier. Keep an output when it is non-empty and either changes the
   item kind or yields a different, non-water potion. Each kept result becomes a
   four-position recipe: the ingredient in the first position and the input in
   the other three, producing nothing in the first and the output in the other
   three.
4. A result already present is not added again, and only genuinely new results
   extend the frontier.
5. If the accumulated set passes 7500 recipes, abort with an error naming a
   probable cycle.

The 7500 cap is a disclosed ceiling: it is a guess at where a legitimate pack
ends and a cycle begins, and a pack with many potion-adding mods could reach it
legitimately. A counterpart keeps a cap, because the closure is genuinely
unbounded under a badly-behaved mod, and makes it a setting rather than a
constant.

The result is computed once per process and never invalidated, so a data-pack
reload that changes brewing does not change this list (**read**). That is a
defect (§15).

## 12. Item stores inside items

Two attachments give an item its own item store: a portable container item and a
carrying pouch. Neither has a fixed slot count. Both report a slot count taken
from the list they currently hold, so an empty container item publishes no slots
at all (**read**). Both read the whole list out of the item, mutate it, and write
it back on every single-slot operation (**read**).

Insertion and extraction both run against that list-valued item, and their
semantics are ordinary except in two places a counterpart must match:

- **Insertion refuses a partial merge into an occupied slot when the items are
  not the same item with the same data**, returning the whole input rather than a
  remainder.
- **Extraction splits the existing entry in place and writes the remainder back
  only when not simulating**, which means a simulated extraction mutates the
  in-memory copy it read and discards it. That is safe only because the copy is
  re-read each call (**read**).

The pouch differs in three ways (**read**): its per-slot limit is 64 regardless of
the item's own maximum; an item is valid for it only when the pouch's remaining
weight admits at least one; and its insertion is capped by that weight rather than
by a stack size. The container item's per-slot limit is also 64 and it accepts
anything.

Both report a slot count taken from the current contents, so a store's slot count
changes as the store is used. A dependent that caches the count sees it go stale
(**read**).

## 13. How it reaches into the host

The entry intrudes on the host in two separate ways, and a counterpart is
blocked on both.

### 13.1 Access widening

The entry ships one access-widening document holding 15 entries, counted twice
(**read**): three on the brewing tables and their entry type, one on a tag
class's backing array, six across the smelting stations for their recipe kind,
burn timer, lit test and burn-duration lookups, two on the brewing station for
its fuel count and its brewable test, and two on the carrying pouch plus one on
its contents for weight arithmetic. Three plus one plus six plus two plus three
is the fifteen (**read**).

Every one of them is read-only use: the entry widens to read a value the game
keeps private, never to write one (**read**).

### 13.2 A shipped class in the host's own namespace

One of the 52 shipped source files is declared in a package under the game's own
root namespace rather than the project's (**read**). That class is the one that
builds the brewing recipe list of §11, and it is the only shipped file that does
this.

The placement obtains nothing. The package it names is not the package of the
brewing types it reads, so it gets no package-private access from sitting there,
and it reaches the brewing state through the widened fields of §13.1 like any
other caller (**read**). It is namespace occupation and nothing more
(**inferred**; the source states no reason and the placement looks like a
leftover from an earlier game version).

It is still a host intrusion a counterpart cannot reproduce, because a Ravel mod
declares its own names and never the host's. The behaviour is unaffected: the
recipe list of §11 is specified from what the class computes, not from where it
sits.

### 13.3 What a counterpart cannot obtain

Ravel does not widen host access and does not let a mod occupy a host namespace,
and §10 to §12 are written against the values §13.1 reaches. A counterpart
obtains each value through a Ravel hook that exposes it deliberately, and where
no such hook exists the behaviour is blocked rather than approximated. The
blocked set is:

- the raw burn timer behind the smelting heat reading;
- the brewing station's fuel count;
- the brewing station's brewable test;
- the pouch's weight arithmetic;
- the host-namespace placement of §13.2, which is blocked outright and needs no
  replacement because it carries no behaviour.

Four blocked values and one blocked placement (**read**, counted from the lists
above). The widening is the larger of the two intrusions and the one most likely
to need a new Ravel hook, because it is what the blocked values sit behind; the
placement grants no access and blocking it costs nothing.

## 14. Caching and when it is wrong

Three caches exist, and all three are keyed and invalidated differently
(**read**).

- **The general station view's cache** is keyed by recipe kind and dimension
  name, and holds the converted recipe collection. It is cleared when a server
  stops.
- **A third cache**, keyed by a caller-supplied string, holds a converted
  collection for a station whose recipes are not the world's own. Nothing in the
  pinned build files anything in it; it hangs off a base class the original
  carries for dependents. This is the original's structure and the observable
  consequence is none; do not port the shape. It is cleared when a server stops.
- **The brewing closure** is a process-lifetime value with no key and no clearing
  at all (§11.1).

A dimension is part of the station key. Recipes are per-server rather than
per-dimension in the pinned build, so the dimension in the key makes the cache
larger and never wrong (**inferred**).

**What is wrong**: none of the three is invalidated on a data-pack reload, and the
two that are cleared at all are cleared only when the server stops. On an
integrated server a reload that changes a recipe leaves every station view
answering from the old collection until the world is left (**read**, the clearing
points; **inferred**, the consequence). A counterpart invalidates every cache it
keeps on reload as well as on shutdown, and that is the single most useful
divergence in this document.

## 15. Defects

Every row was read directly from the pinned source. None is to be reproduced.

| # | what | consequence | disposition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The brewing view's input-count predicate is a disjunction of "at least two" and "at most four", which is true for every value. | Every input count is admitted, including zero and one. A caller relying on the check to reject a malformed input is not protected; the later size test catches the empty case only. | Implement the intended range check. |
| 2 | A list-of-lists helper subtracts a sub-list's size only when the index is strictly greater than it. | Reading the element at exactly a sub-list's size throws instead of moving to the next sub-list. The class is unreferenced in the pinned build, so nothing hits it today. | Do not port it. It is dead code with a boundary error. |
| 3 | The charge kind's equality narrows both 64-bit values to 32 bits before comparing. | Two charges differing only above the low 32 bits compare equal, and the kind's own maximum quantity is the full 64-bit range, so the values are reachable. | Compare at full width. |
| 4 | The charge kind's ordering returns the 32-bit difference of two 64-bit values. | The order is not a total order over the kind's own range: it overflows and can report a larger value as smaller. Any sorted display or ordered map of charges is affected. | Compare, do not subtract. |
| 5 | The charge kind's condition removal returns false when the two conditions are equal and the original otherwise. | Removing a condition from itself clears it, but removing a condition it does not have leaves it set, so removal is not the inverse of addition. The flag-set kinds have no such asymmetry. | Define removal as the boolean and-not. |
| 6 | Rebuilding an empty fluid portion at a non-zero quantity yields a portion of the game's default fluid. | A caller that meant "this much of nothing" silently receives water. | Refuse, or return empty. |
| 7 | The shared data comparison falls back to identity hash for value types with no natural order. | Structurally equal data compares unequal after a copy, so items that should stack or match do not, non-deterministically. This is what the debug-logging setting exists to diagnose. | Compare structurally. |
| 8 | No cache in §14 is invalidated on a data-pack reload; the brewing closure is never invalidated at all. | A reload that changes recipes leaves every station view and the whole brewing list stale for the life of the process. | Invalidate all three on reload. |
| 9 | The item kind's order falls back to a numeric registry index while the fluid kind's falls back to a name. | An item order is stable within a session and not across two with different mod sets, and the two kinds are inconsistent with each other. | Order both by name. |
| 10 | The charge adapters narrow every quantity to 32 bits at the host boundary through a checked cast helper. | The kind admits 64-bit charges that no adapter can carry; the helper's behaviour above the 32-bit range is **unknown**, because its declaration is in RVL-210's vocabulary and was not read. | Saturate explicitly at the boundary and record the ceiling. |
| 11 | The item count escape of §7 writes a clamped count that a reader unaware of the extra field accepts without error. | Silent data loss rather than a decode failure, for any count above 99. | Keep the escape for compatibility; make the counterpart's own reader require it. |

## 16. What the pinned pack's dependents observe

Four mods in the pinned pack declare this entry as a required dependency, all on

dependency graph). No mod declares it optionally, and no mod outside those four
names it. Counted twice.

The four are a machine-network mod, a crafting-automation mod, a terminal mod and
a transfer mod, all by the same author as this entry and as RVL-210, and all
outside this wave. This entry is admitted as a foundation seed rather than for
them.

RVL-210 §17 lists the subsystems its dependents declare against, and marks that
list inferred because it was counted against an unpinned checkout of this
project. §17 asked for a recount against the pinned build. This is it, taken from

Of §17's quantity collections, the ingredient-keyed map and the filtering

## 17. What this specification does not specify

- **The interface vocabulary this entry compiles against.** It is owned by
  RVL-210's counterpart per the wave decision, described in RVL-210 §10, and its
  declarations are in a separate repository that a source archive of this
  revision carries as an empty directory. §5 is written from the use sites and is
  marked read for what this entry does with the vocabulary and unknown for the
  exact signatures. This is the single largest limit on this document.
- **The three registered names, the count-escape field name, and the seven
  category-type names.** All are compatibility-required spellings and all are
  obtainable from the pinned artifact. The names surface is unresolved, so no
  save-compatible identifier mapping is specified and a counterpart's alias
  declaration is empty.
- **The host's item, fluid and charge interfaces, its recipe manager, its brewing
  tables and its container items.** Host concerns.
- **The four dependents' own behaviour.** Each has, or will have, its own entry.
- **The six companion test mods and the 35 unit tests.** Not shipped, and read
  only to confirm which subsystems have coverage. They are the reason §6 and §8
  can be stated with confidence and §10 to §12 cannot: the tests cover matching,
  serialising and the adapter table, and cover no attachment.
- **The two logo images.** Branding, not opened.

**Coverage.** All 52 shipped source files were read, and the mod descriptor, the
settings declarations, the access-widening document and the resource inventory
were read in full. Not read: the vocabulary declarations (absent from the
archive), the unit tests and companion mods beyond their file names, the 30
translation documents, the two logos and the empty structure template. Nothing in
this document is written from an unpinned tree.
