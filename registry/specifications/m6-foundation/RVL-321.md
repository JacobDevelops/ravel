Wave: `waves/m6-foundation.json`. Method: source-informed reimplementation.
Rights: the upstream project's repository root carries an MIT licence file, read
at the source; see `provenance/m6-foundation/RVL-321.json` for the pinned
revision, the licence identity and the digest.

Confidence markers: **read**, **inferred**, **unknown**.

This entry is the cluster scenario's subject. It depends on RVL-042, and RVL-002
and RVL-086 sit downstream of it (**read**, from the plan rows). Where this
document needs something RVL-042 already states, it cites that document's section

## 1. What this entry is

A progression mod built on one loop: a player plants a *crop*, the crop matures
on tiered ground, and harvesting it yields a material that would otherwise have
to be mined, killed for, or traded for. Around that loop sit five tiers of
material, five tiers of ground, a set of powered machines, two multiblock
crafting rituals, a set of tools and armour that accept slotted modifiers, and a
catalogue of about a hundred and forty crops, most of which exist to produce
another mod's material and are switched off when that mod is absent.

It is a large entry — roughly two hundred and sixty source files and twenty-two
thousand lines, plus about seven hundred and seventy data-declared recipes — but
most of that bulk is catalogue rather than mechanism (**read**). The mechanism
is small enough to state exactly, and this document states it.

The entry's metadata declares each of its dependencies mandatory and both-sided,
the shared library of RVL-042 among them with a floor version; it states no side
of its own (**read**). It ships
**no mixin**, and its only intrusion into the host is an access widening of
exactly **two host members**: a static helper that answers whether tilled ground
is near water, used by §9's moisture rule, and a sapling's tree generator, used
by §7's strong fertiliser (**read**, from the packaging metadata; the count
agrees with the inventory's recorded two access transformers and zero mixin
configurations). Everything else goes through published registration, event and
capability surfaces. That is what this entry's `mixin-integration` dimension has
to assert against: not a mixin, but two widened members, both of which a
counterpart must replace with its own logic — the water test is reimplementable
from the block positions around a point, and the tree generator is not reachable
at all, so §7's sapling branch is **host-blocked**.

### 1.1 Ravel disposition

Ravel already owns content declaration and registration, configuration
declaration, tag identity, saved-data ownership, network payload declaration and
side isolation, and a counterpart expresses the behaviour below through those
rather than rebuilding them. What survives as counterpart work is the decision
rules and the portable arithmetic: §5, §7, §8, §9, §10, §11, §12.1 and §14.

Several areas are **host-blocked** at the reviewed public revision — they need a
surface Ravel does not publish. Each is named where it occurs, and a counterpart
records them rather than stubbing them.

## 2. Side, lifecycle and what happens when

Everything below is **read**. The entry runs on both sides and its ordering is
observable, because a dependent registering its own crops has to land inside one
specific window.

At construction, in this order:

1. Block and item registration observers are attached, and eleven separately
   scoped registries (armour materials, creative grouping, block entities,
   item-level data kinds, menu kinds, ingredient kinds, data-pack condition
   kinds, recipe kinds, recipe serialisers, world features, biome modifiers) are
   attached to the loading bus.
2. Network payloads and capability registration are attached (§13).
3. On a client only, eight presentation observers are attached. Side is decided
   by a static environment query, not by a runtime level check.
4. One configuration document is declared, common-scoped and resolved at startup
   (§3).
5. The three extension registries are made reachable by extensions (§4).
6. Extensions are discovered and their charge types registered (§4).

At common setup, eight world-scoped observers are attached and the three
extension registries log their sizes. At client setup, three more presentation
observers are attached.

Registration itself happens later, in the host's registration window, and its
internal order is §6. The one guarantee a dependent can rely on is that its own
crop declarations are asked for inside block registration and are refused
anywhere else (§4).

In Ravel terms: the eleven registries are one `ContentDeclaration` claimed
through `Registrar`; the configuration document is one
`ConfigurationDeclaration` resolved before `configure`; the world-scoped
observers are `Mod::observe`; and the client-only observers are declared
client-only rather than guarded by a runtime side test.

One document, common-scoped, resolved at startup, in two groupings (**read**).
Twenty settings. It is **not** synchronised to a joining client; §13 records
what is synchronised instead, and why the unsynchronised settings are safe.

**General grouping**, sixteen settings:

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| chance a defeated pathfinding creature drops the tier-1 material | fraction | 0.2 | 0.0–1.0 |
| uses on the basic ritual catalyst | integer | 1000 | 10 upward |
| seconds between growth pulses | integer | 10 | 1 upward |
| chance a harvested crop yields the weak fertiliser | fraction | 0.1 | 0.0–1.0 |
| second seed may drop | boolean | on | |
| crops require their tier's own ground to grow | boolean | **off** | |

| the two top tool tiers are enchantable at a table | boolean | on | |
| the two top armour tiers are unbreakable | boolean | **off** | |
| automated fake players may use the wetting tool | boolean | on | |
| a full top-tier armour set grants its growth aura | boolean | on | |

**World grouping**, four settings: three booleans enabling each of the three

Seven of these settings are also published to extensions as live suppliers, read

**Host-blocked**: the pulse block's interval and the catalyst's use count
are read live on every use rather than at resolution, which Ravel's configuration
resolution allows; nothing here needs a host surface Ravel lacks.

## 4. Extensions, the three registries, and when registration is allowed

This entry is extensible: other mods add crops, tiers, crop kinds, slotted
modifiers and creature charge types to it. RVL-002 is exactly such an extension,
so this section is the contract RVL-002 is written against.

**Three registries** (**read**): crops (holding crops, tiers and crop kinds),
slotted modifiers, and creature charge types. Each is a single process-wide
insertion-ordered table keyed by identifier.

**Duplicate handling is per registry and is not uniform** (**read**):

- A crop is refused if its *path* collides with an already-registered crop's
  path, regardless of namespace. Two mods cannot both contribute a crop called
  the same thing even in different namespaces.
- A tier, a crop kind, a modifier and a charge type are each refused on full
  identifier collision only.
- A charge type is additionally refused if any of the creature identifiers it
  claims is already claimed by another type. Claiming is global and first-wins.
- Every refusal is logged and skipped. Nothing throws, and nothing tells the
  caller it was refused.

**The registration window is enforced for crops and only for crops** (**read**).
A latch is opened immediately before extensions are asked for their crops and
closed immediately after; a crop registered outside that window is refused with
an error log. Modifiers and charge types have no such latch.

**Ordering of the extension callbacks** (**read**):

1. At construction: one pass over the extensions configures each extension and
   takes its charge types immediately after, so configure and register-charge
   run interleaved per extension rather than as two passes; a **second** pass
   over all extensions then adjusts charge types.
2. During block registration: every extension registers crops, then default crop
   blocks are created for any crop that did not supply one, then the crop table
   is re-sorted (§6).
3. During item registration: crop material and seed items are created, then
   every extension adjusts crops.
4. Also during item registration: every extension registers modifiers, the
   modifier items are created, then every extension adjusts modifiers.

The two-pass shape is the contract: an extension may safely reach for another
extension's contribution in its *adjust* callback and must not in its *register*
callback. RVL-002 raises the tier ceiling, which means it registers a tier and
crops in the register pass and points them at blocks it owns in the adjust pass.

Each extension carries a small configuration record naming its own namespace and
three booleans that opt out of the three dynamically contributed recipe kinds of
§10. Those booleans are combined with the per-crop equivalents by conjunction at
the moment the crop is registered, so an extension that opts out cannot be
overridden by a crop that opts in (**read**).

**Host-blocked**: extensions are discovered by scanning every loaded mod's
annotation index and instantiating what it finds, and the three registries are
made reachable by a write that bypasses the ordinary published surface. Neither
is dependent-visible behaviour and a counterpart must
reproduce neither: Ravel has no annotation index to scan and no reason to install
a registry by reflection. What a counterpart reproduces is the ordering above and
the refusal rules, with the entry's own contribution always ordered first
(**read** — the entry inserts its own extension before the scan runs).

## 5. The crop model

Three declared things (**read**). This is data a dependent supplies, so the
defaults are part of the contract.

### 5.1 Tier

A tier carries an identifier, an integer *value*, a colour, a text colour, and —
filled in later, during the adjust pass — the ground block and the material item
that belong to it. Its defaults are: fertilisable **true**, second-drop enabled
**true**, base second-drop chance **0.1**.

Six tiers ship. Five are numbered one to five with values one to five. The sixth
is a side tier for the four elemental crops and carries **value one**, so it
shares tier one's ordinal, its colour and its text colour, and — after the adjust
pass — tier one's ground and material. The consequence is that the elemental
crops sort with tier one and are drop-eligible on tier one's ground (**read**).

`is this block my effective ground` is exact block identity against the tier's
own ground block, with a null ground answering false. There is no "counts as any
ground" escape at this revision (**read**).

### 5.2 Crop kind

A crop kind carries an identifier, a stem model reference, and — filled in during
the adjust pass — the base seed item that crafting a seed of that kind consumes.
Two ship: a material kind and a creature kind, differing only in that base seed
(**read**).

### 5.3 Crop

A crop carries an identifier, a tier, a kind, a crafting material (a lazily
resolved ingredient, either an item with optional item-level data or a tag), and
optional overrides. Its defaults (**read**):

- base second-drop chance **-1**, meaning *defer to the tier*;
- respects effective ground **true**;
- enabled **true**;
- register the crop block, the material item and the seed item: **all true**;
- glint **false**;
- all three dynamic recipe kinds **enabled**;
- required biomes: **empty**.

Setting a supplied block or item flips the corresponding register flag to false
unless the caller asks otherwise, so a crop that brings its own block is not
registered twice (**read**).

A crop's colour setter writes the same value into three separate colours (flower,
material, seed), and each is packed with **full opacity** into a four-channel
value; a zero colour means "do not tint", so a crop cannot ask for pure black
(**read**). Charge types pack their colour at **opacity 204** instead
(**read**) — two different opacity constants in one entry, and a counterpart must
keep them distinct rather than unify them.

## 6. Registration, its order, and the identifiers it produces

Everything here is **read**, and the whole section is what `registry-parity`
observes.

**Block registration** runs in one pass: the static block table in declaration
order, then the crop latch opens, extensions contribute crops, a default crop
block is created for every crop that wants one and has none, each crop block is
registered, the latch closes.

**Item registration** runs in one pass: block items in the order their blocks
were declared, then the static item table, then every crop's material item, then
every crop's seed item, then the gear table, then the modifier items.

Two ordering facts a dependent can observe:

- **Crop block ids come before crop item ids, and material items come before
  seed items across the whole catalogue** — not crop by crop. All materials are
  registered, then all seeds.
- **After block registration the crop table is re-sorted by tier value**, with a
  stable sort, so within a tier the declaration order survives and across tiers
  the table is ascending. Everything that iterates crops afterwards — item
  registration, the creative listing, the dynamic recipes of §11 — sees the
  sorted order, and item registration therefore does **not** follow declaration
  order. A counterpart that iterates in declaration order produces a different
  registration sequence and a different creative listing.

**Identifier shape**: a crop's block, material and seed identifiers are all built
from the crop's own path plus a fixed per-role suffix, **in this entry's
namespace**, never in the declaring extension's namespace (**read**). A crop
contributed by RVL-002 therefore registers content under this entry's namespace.
That, plus the path-collision rule of §4, is why crop paths are effectively a
single global space.

**Registered content**, by kind (**read**): sixty-six blocks with items plus one
crop block registered without an item; six armour materials; one creative
grouping; twelve block entity kinds; five item-level data kinds (§13); seven menu
kinds; three ingredient kinds (§10.4); four data-pack condition kinds (§10.5); six
recipe kinds with six serialisers, plus two further serialisers for crafting-grid
recipe kinds (§10); one world feature; three biome modifier kinds; and, per crop,
up to three registrations.

**Host-blocked**: none of this. Registration is a Ravel facility and the ordering
above is expressible as declaration order plus one sort.

## 7. Whether a crop may grow

Four gates, all **read**, all evaluated together on every growth attempt. A crop
grows only if all four hold; otherwise the attempt is silently dropped and the
crop stays at its age.

1. **Foundation.** If the crop declares a required foundation block, the block
   *two* below the crop must be exactly that block. No crop in this entry's own
   catalogue declares one; the gate exists for extensions.
2. **Ground.** If the "require effective ground" setting is on — it defaults
   **off** — the block directly below must be the crop's tier's own ground block.
   **The tier-1 crop is exempt by identity**, tested against the specific crop
   rather than against a property, so an extension cannot make its own crop
   exempt.
3. **Biome.** If the crop declares a non-empty biome set, the biome at the crop's
   position must be in it by identifier.
4. Only then does the ordinary host growth step run.

The gate is applied to growth from a random tick and to growth from fertilising
alike, and to the question asked before a fertilising attempt as well as to the
attempt itself, so it cannot be bypassed by fertilising (**read**).

**Ordinary bone meal never advances a resource crop.** The crop block answers
"this fertilising attempt did not succeed" unconditionally, so the host's own
fertiliser consumes nothing and does nothing (**read**). The entry's own two
fertiliser items get past that by testing the tier's *fertilisable* flag instead
of asking the block whether the attempt succeeded:

- The **weak fertiliser** (the one crops themselves drop) performs one ordinary
  fertilising step on a crop whose tier is fertilisable.
- The **strong fertiliser** sets a crop straight to its maximum age, and
  additionally handles saplings by triggering tree growth. On anything else
  bonemealable it performs the ordinary step.
- Both consume one item, both fire the host's fertilising event first and honour
  a cancellation, and both are also registered as dispenser behaviours
  (**read**).

**The pulse block** is the third growth path (**read**). On placement it
schedules itself. On each scheduled tick it scans the vertical column starting
**two blocks above itself** up to *range* + 2 above, takes the **first**
bonemealable block found, gives that one block a single random tick, and
reschedules. Six pulse blocks ship with ranges 9, 18, 27, 36, 45 and 54.

Two exact rules there are worth keeping:

- Only the first bonemealable block in the column is ticked, so a pulse block
  under a stack of crops only ever serves the lowest one.
- The reschedule delay is computed as `(int)(configured seconds × uniform(0.9,
  1.1)) × 20` — the truncation to an integer happens **before** the multiply, so
  the delay is a whole number of seconds and a configured value of 1 yields
  either 0 or 20 ticks. A counterpart that multiplies first and truncates later
  produces a visibly different growth rate.

**The armour set aura** is the fourth path (**read**). While a player wears a
full set of the top armour tier and the setting is on, once every twenty ticks,
every block within a radius-5 cube of the player's standing position that carries
the host's crop tag gets a single random tick with probability one half, drawn
per block. The draw is from a global source.

**Host-blocked**: block state, the random tick, the fertilising event and the
biome lookup are host concerns. A counterpart implements the four-gate predicate,
the pulse block's column rule and delay arithmetic, and the aura's radius and
per-block probability as portable logic, and leaves the ticking to Ravel.

## 8. What a harvest yields

This is the arithmetic the cluster scenario measures and `loot-parity` checks. It
is **read** in full.

Crops carry no loot table. The crop block computes its drops directly, which
means the result is not data-overridable and not affected by fortune, silk touch
or the harvesting tool (**read**).

### 8.1 The second-drop chance

Given the block directly below the crop, the chance is built up and clamped:

- Start at zero. If the crop's **tier** has second drops disabled, stop at zero.
- If the block below is **any tier's ground**, add the crop's own base chance, or
  the tier's base chance when the crop's own is at or below the -1 sentinel.
- If the crop **respects effective ground** and the block below is **this tier's
  own ground**, add a further **0.1**.
- Clamp to 1.0.

The two additions are not exclusive: a crop on its own tier's ground gets both,
so with shipped defaults it sits at 0.2 while the same crop on a different tier's
ground sits at 0.1 and on plain ground at 0.0.

### 8.2 The general crop

At maximum age only. The produce count is set to one before the origin position
is consulted, so a drop request that carries no origin still yields one produce
and one seed at maximum age; only the three draws below are skipped (**read**):

- produce starts at 1; a second is added if a draw is under the second-drop
  chance;
- seeds start at 1; a second is added if the "second seed" setting is on **and** a
  draw is under the same chance;
- one weak fertiliser is dropped if a draw is under the fertiliser chance
  (default 0.1).

**Three independent draws**, all from a global random source, all compared with
`<`. The produce and seed draws use the same chance but are separate draws, so
the two second-drops are independent rather than correlated. A counterpart takes
the draws from a caller-supplied source so the rule can be tested, and must keep
them independent.

Below maximum age the crop yields one seed and nothing else.

### 8.3 The tier-1 crop

The tier-1 crop replaces the produce rule with an arithmetic one over the tier
*value* of the ground below, and drops **no fertiliser at all** (**read**):

produce = `(int)(0.5 × value + 0.5)`, then, if the value is greater than one and
even, one more with probability one half.

| ground tier value | produce | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1 | |
| 2 | 1 | plus one at 50% |
| 3 | 2 | |
| 4 | 2 | plus one at 50% |
| 5 | 3 | |

Ground that is not tiered at all leaves produce at 1. A drop request that
carries no origin likewise leaves produce at 1 and skips both the tiered-ground
arithmetic and the second-seed draw, yielding one produce and one seed at
maximum age (**read**). The seed rule is otherwise the general one. The
half-chance bump on even tiers is what makes the progression smooth rather than
stepped, and it is required rather than incidental.

### 8.4 The harvesting machine's harvest

The machine (§12.5) does not use the above directly. It asks the host for the
block's drops with itself as the breaking entity, then removes **exactly one**
item matching the crop's own seed from the result and keeps the rest, then resets
the crop to age zero rather than breaking it (**read**). The single withheld seed
is the replant, and it is what makes the machine sustainable rather than
consuming. This is the same withheld-seed rule as RVL-042 §11's sweeping harvest,
and a counterpart should share one implementation.

**Host-blocked**: the drop request, the loot context and the crop's own seed
lookup are host concerns; RVL-042 §15 already records that the seed lookup is
reached reflectively in the shared library. A counterpart implements §8.1 to §8.3
as a pure function of (age, maximum age, tier, ground tier, settings, draws) and
leaves the drop request to Ravel.

## 9. The tiered ground

Five ground blocks, one per numbered tier, each extending the host's tilled
ground and each carrying its tier (**read**). This is the block the cluster
scenario's crops sit on, so its four behaviours are stated exactly.

### 9.1 Moisture

Bounded 0 to 7, in the host's own block state. Fertile means moisture greater
than zero — strictly greater, so a value of zero is not fertile (**read**).

On each random tick (**read**):

- if the ground is **neither** near water **nor** under rain, and moisture is
  above zero, moisture drops by exactly one;
- otherwise, if moisture is below 7, it is set **straight to 7** — not
  incremented.

The asymmetry is the rule: drying is gradual and wetting is instantaneous. A
counterpart that increments on the wet branch produces ground that takes seven
ticks to become fertile after rain instead of one. The near-water test is the
first of the two widened host members named in §1; a counterpart reimplements it
rather than reaching for it.

### 9.2 Falling on it

Any entity landing on this ground takes **full fall damage** with a multiplier of
1.0 (**read**). The host's own tilled ground converts back to dirt when jumped on
and this ground does not; it takes the damage path instead and survives. That is
the behavioural difference a player notices, and it is deliberate.

### 9.3 What it drops

Computed directly by the block rather than by a loot table, so it is not
data-overridable at this revision (**read**):

- broken with a silk-touch tool: **itself**;
- otherwise: one dirt, **plus** one of the tier's own material with probability
  25%, drawn as `random integer in [0,100) < 25` from the level's own source.

The tool is read from the drop request's optional tool parameter, so a break with
no recorded tool takes the second branch.

### 9.4 Converting ground

Right-clicking ground with a tier material, gated on the conversion setting of
§3 (**read**):

- **plain tilled ground** becomes that tier's ground, **carrying its moisture
  across unchanged**, with a sand-breaking sound, consuming one material;
- **tiered ground of a different tier** becomes the new tier the same way,
  consuming one material, and additionally pops **one of the outgoing tier's
  material** on top with probability 25%, drawn from a global source;
- **tiered ground of the same tier**, and anything else, is left alone and the
  interaction passes through.

Moisture carrying across is the load-bearing part: a converted plot does not
reset to dry, so a player upgrading ground under a mature crop loses no growth
progress. The refund on a tier change is what makes upgrading and downgrading
roughly symmetric in cost.

Six tilling recipes in the shipped data (§10.4) produce the plain tilled ground
from ordinary materials, which is what makes the whole chain reachable without a
tool that tills.

**Host-blocked**: block state, the random tick, the rain and water queries, the
drop request and the interaction result are all host concerns. A counterpart
implements the moisture transition as a pure function of (moisture, near water,
raining), the drop rule as a function of (silk touch, tier, draw), and the
conversion as a function of (current ground, held tier, setting, draw), and leaves
the block to Ravel.

## 10. Recipes

Six machine and ritual recipe kinds, two crafting-grid recipe kinds, three
ingredient kinds and four data-pack conditions (**read**). This section is what
`recipe-parity` observes.

### 10.1 The two ritual recipes

Both are altar-and-ring shapes with an item on the altar and items on eight
surrounding stations, and both match by unordered assignment of the ring items to
the declared ingredients (**read**).

**The four-station ritual.** One altar ingredient, up to eight ring ingredients,
one result, and a boolean (default false) that copies the altar item's
item-level data onto the result. Matching requires the ring item count to equal
the declared ingredient count exactly.

**The eight-station ritual** splits its ring into four ingredient stations and
four reservoir stations. It declares up to four ingredients **and** up to four
material stacks with counts. Ingredient matching is as above; separately, the
reservoir contents must cover every declared material stack by count, and the
recipe reports which are missing and by how much so the interface can show it.
A document carries four of each, and the ring reads materials and ingredients in
alternation with a material first.

**A serialisation defect in the eight-station ritual** (**read**, from both
directions): the network encoding writes exactly the four real ingredients (the
odd positions of the interleaved list) and the four materials, and the network
decoding reads four and four, so **the network path is symmetric and correct**.
The document encoding is not: the field getter for the ingredient list hands back
the *interleaved eight*, while the document decoder refuses more than four with
an explicit error. A recipe decoded from a data pack therefore cannot be
re-encoded to a document. Whether anything in a running game re-encodes a recipe
to a document is **unknown**; the visible consequence is that any tool that
round-trips this recipe kind through its document form fails on it. A counterpart
defines one symmetric document encoding — four ingredients in, four out — and
records the divergence.

### 10.2 The enchanting recipe

Two counted ingredients and a target item, with the enchantment named by the
recipe (**read**). The resulting level is `min` over the two inputs of
`floor(available count / required count)`, capped at the enchantment's own
maximum. Consumption is `required count × level` from each input and one from the
target. A plain book becomes an enchanted book instead.

**A defect in the compatibility test** (**read** as to behaviour, **inferred** as
to intent). Before enchanting, the recipe walks the target's existing
enchantments and refuses the craft when either:

- the target already carries **this** enchantment at a level greater than or
  equal to the one being applied — correct, this is what stops a downgrade; or
- the target carries a **different** enchantment and the host reports the two as
  **compatible**.

The second test is inverted. It refuses precisely the pairs that may coexist and
permits precisely the pairs that may not. The plain reading of the surrounding
code is that it meant to refuse on incompatibility. A counterpart refuses on
incompatibility and records the divergence; a parity test against the original
will disagree on every target that already carries any compatible enchantment,
and that is expected.

### 10.3 The four single-input recipes

- **Reprocessing**: one ingredient, one result. Matches one non-empty input.
- **Charge extraction**: one ingredient plus a container in the third slot; the
  container must be empty or already hold the named charge type and must not be
  full. The result is the container with a declared amount of charge added,
  capped at that type's requirement.
- **Creature summoning**: one counted ingredient and a weighted list of creature
  kinds. Produces no item at all; its effect is §12.6.
- **Smelting** is the host's own kind, used unchanged by the powered smelting
  machine.

### 10.4 The two crafting-grid recipe kinds and three ingredient kinds

**A tilling recipe**: a shapeless recipe that additionally returns any tool in
the grid that satisfies the tool ingredient as a *remainder* with one more point
of damage, dropping it entirely if that would destroy it (**read**). Six ship,
converting ground materials.

**An emptying recipe**: a shapeless recipe matching exactly one charged container
and nothing else, yielding an empty one (**read**). Its match rule is
order-sensitive in a way worth stating: it walks the grid and refuses as soon as
it sees a non-empty slot **after** it has already seen a charged container, so a
container in the last slot matches and one in the first slot with anything after
it does not.

**Three ingredient kinds** (**read**): every tool of the tilling family (declares
itself *simple*, and resolves by scanning the item registry); a charged container
holding more than zero charge (declares itself **non-simple**); and a
crop-role selector naming a crop and one of three roles — its tier's
material, its kind's base seed, or its crafting material (declares itself
**non-simple**). The crop-role selector is the one that matters to
dependents: it lets a data pack name a crop's material without knowing the item
it resolved to, which is what makes an extension's crops usable from this entry's
own recipe data.

### 10.5 The four data-pack conditions

A crop is enabled; a modifier is enabled; a crop has a non-empty crafting
material; the plain-grid seed recipes are enabled (**read**). The first is by far
the most used — four hundred and sixteen appearances across three hundred and
forty-one shipped documents, some carrying it more than once — and
its meaning is that a crop belonging to an absent mod contributes no recipes even
though its block and items are still registered (§4, §15).

### 10.6 What the shipped data actually uses

Counted across the shipped recipe files (**read**): three hundred and
ninety-five plain shaped, fifty-nine plain shapeless, fifteen stonecutting, seven
smelting; fifty-seven four-station rituals, eighteen eight-station rituals,
forty-two enchanting, thirty-nine charge extractions, twenty-two summonings, six
tillings, one emptying; and from RVL-042 §6, **seventy-six damage-transferring
shaped recipes, twenty-eight tag-result shaped recipes and five
component-transferring shaped recipes**.

The twenty-eight tag-result recipes are the direct dependency on RVL-042 §5, and
RVL-042 §5.3 records that the shipped selection is arbitrary — the tag's first
member wins and the configured preference is inert. Twenty-eight of this entry's
results are therefore decided by host member order in an installation with no
unification mod, and frozen on first ask into the file of RVL-042 §5.2. A
counterpart adopting RVL-042 §5.3's proposed rule changes those twenty-eight
results in a fresh installation and leaves them unchanged in an existing one.
This entry's `recipe-parity` dimension must be written to expect that.

**Host-blocked**: recipe kinds, serialisers, ingredient kinds and data-pack
conditions all need surfaces Ravel does not publish, and RVL-042 §6 already
records the same for the shared library's kinds. A counterpart implements the
matching and assembly rules as portable logic over declared inputs and records
the registration as blocked.

## 11. Recipes contributed at load

Three recipes per crop, contributed while the host assembles its recipe
collection, through the event RVL-042 §8 publishes (**read**). This is the
strongest thing this entry takes from RVL-042 and the ordering guarantee there is
what makes it correct.

For each registered crop, in the sorted order of §6, and only if the crop is
enabled and the corresponding per-crop and per-extension booleans of §4 both
hold:

1. **A ritual seed recipe.** A four-station ritual whose altar item is the crop
   kind's base seed and whose eight ring ingredients alternate the crop's
   crafting material and the crop's tier material, starting with the crafting
   material, producing one seed. Skipped if the tier has no material, the kind
   has no base seed, or the crop's crafting material resolves empty.
2. **A plain-grid seed recipe**, gated additionally on the setting of §3 which
   defaults **off**: a 3×3 shaped recipe with the base seed centred, the tier
   material on the four edges and the crafting material on the four corners.
3. **A seed-conversion recipe** turning one seed into **two** of the crop's
   produce.

Each is given an identifier built from the crop's path and a fixed per-recipe
suffix (**read**).

Two consequences worth keeping. First, a crop whose crafting material is a tag
that resolves to nothing at load contributes no seed recipe but still contributes
a seed-conversion recipe, so a seed obtained another way is still recoverable.
Second, because the contribution happens during assembly rather than after,
everything downstream — the ingredient cache of §13, the reservoir's accept
predicate of §12.7, the recipe book — sees these recipes as though they had come
from the data pack.

**Host-blocked**: RVL-042 §8 records that Ravel publishes no recipe-assembly
interception point, so a counterpart cannot make this live at the reviewed public
revision. What a counterpart implements is the derivation — the function from a
crop to its three recipe declarations — and records the contribution point as
blocked.

## 12. The machines, the rituals, and what survives a reload

This is the section the cluster scenario's save-and-reload step exercises. All
**read** unless marked.

Every block entity here owns an inventory saved and loaded by **RVL-042 §7's
envelope**, including
that section's above-maximum stack counts, its partial-load tolerance and its
slot-limit and output-slot rules. What each block entity adds on top is listed
below, and those additions are its own.

### 12.1 The upgrade tier arithmetic

Six upgrade tiers, applied by placing an upgrade item into a machine's
single-slot upgrade inventory (**read**):

| upgrade | tier value | operation-time multiplier | fuel-usage multiplier | fuel-capacity multiplier | added range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1 | 0.833 | 2 | 2 | 1 |
| 2 | 2 | 0.625 | 4 | 3 | 2 |
| 3 | 3 | 0.4 | 6 | 4 | 3 |
| 4 | 4 | 0.125 | 16 | 5 | 4 |
| 5 | 5 | 0.025 | 48 | 6 | 5 |
| 6 | **5** | 0.005 | 96 | 7 | 6 |

Each derived value is `(int)(base × multiplier)`, truncating toward zero
(**read**). A counterpart reproduces the truncation exactly; it is visible at
every tier because none of the bases divides evenly by every multiplier.

**A defect in the acceptance rule** (**read**). An upgrade may be applied only
when the machine currently holds no upgrade, or when the held upgrade's tier
*value* is strictly less than the new one's. The sixth upgrade takes its value
from the fifth tier, so **its value is 5, the same as the fifth upgrade's**, and
a machine already holding the fifth upgrade refuses the sixth. The only way to
reach the sixth is to apply it to a machine that has never held the fifth. Every
other multiplier in the table says the sixth is a genuine step up, so this is
almost certainly unintended. A counterpart orders upgrades by their own position
rather than by a borrowed tier value, and records the divergence.

The tooltip colour is not derived from the tier value: it is a separate text
colour borrowed from the same tier the value comes from, which is why the two top
upgrades display in the same colour (**read**).

### 12.2 The fuel and energy model, shared by five machines

Every powered machine holds a store with a base capacity of **80000** and burns
solid fuel into it (**read**):

- When the store is not full and the store's own fuel slot holds a burnable item,
  and no burn is in progress, the item's burn time is multiplied by **20** and
  both the remaining and the total are set to that; one item is consumed.
- Each tick, `min(remaining, usage × 2, headroom)` is moved from the burn into
  the store, and the remaining is reduced by however much the store actually
  accepted. When the remaining reaches zero the recorded total is cleared.
- Capacity tracks the upgrade: on any change of upgrade the capacity is set to
  `(int)(80000 × capacity multiplier)`, or reset to the base when the upgrade is
  removed. **Energy already stored above a reduced capacity is not clamped**
  (**inferred** — the store's capacity setter writes the field and nothing
  re-checks the stored amount).

Per-machine bases (**read**):

| machine | operation ticks | usage per tick | notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| powered smelting | 200 | 20 | operation time comes from the recipe when it declares one |
| seed conversion | 200 | 20 | |
| charge extraction | 100 | 40 | |
| harvesting | 100 | 40 | plus a scan cost of 10 |
| creature summoning | 200 | 20 | |

The running loop is **not** uniform, and the harvesting machine is the odd one
out (**read**):

- The four recipe machines — powered smelting, seed conversion, charge extraction
  and creature summoning — run only while the store holds at least one tick's
  usage, charge that usage on every tick they advance, and complete when progress
  reaches *or exceeds* the derived operation time.
- The harvesting machine charges nothing per tick. It advances on the same
  energy floor, completes only when progress is **strictly greater** than the
  operation time, and charges energy at that point alone: the full usage for a
  completed harvest, the scan cost for a step that finds nothing to harvest.

Two further rules cut across the five. A neighbouring redstone signal disables
the harvesting machine and the creature-summoning machine outright; the other
three ignore redstone entirely. And losing power holds progress rather than
resetting it, while losing the input resets progress to zero — except in the
creature-summoning machine, which resets only when its input slot is *empty*, so
an input present but short of the recipe's declared count, or an input matching
no recipe at all, holds progress instead of clearing it.

### 12.3 What each block entity persists

Beyond RVL-042 §7's inventory envelope (**read**):

- **Five powered machines**: progress, remaining burn, recorded burn total, the
  energy amount, and the upgrade inventory. The harvesting machine additionally
  persists its **scan cursor**, which is what makes its sweep resume where it left
  off across a reload rather than restarting — directly observable in the cluster
  scenario.
- **Two altars**: progress and an *active* flag.
- **Pedestals, reservoirs, the fitting bench and the enchanting bench**: nothing
  beyond their inventories.

**One spelling inconsistency** (**read**): three machines write the energy amount
in the store's own form and two write it as a plain integer under the
same key, while all five read it back through the store's deserialiser. Both
spellings produce an integer tag, so the saved shape is the same and a world
written by one is read correctly by the other (**inferred** — the store's
serialiser is the host's, and the host's is an integer tag). A counterpart writes
one shape.

**Host-blocked**: Ravel hands a mod a payload for its saved data rather than a
key-addressed compound, exactly as RVL-042 §7 records. A counterpart defines one
payload per block entity carrying the fields above, implements the fuel and
progress arithmetic as portable logic, and leaves the write to Ravel's saved-data
lifecycle. **No save-compatible identifier mapping is specified by this
document**, so a counterpart's alias declaration is empty.

### 12.4 The two rituals

Both altars hold two slots — input and output, output-only for extraction, and
insertion refused while the output is occupied — and both read a ring of eight
fixed offsets around themselves every tick (**read**).

- The altar is inert until *activated*: either a neighbouring redstone signal, or
  a right-click with the activation tool. Activation latches until the craft
  completes or the input is removed.
- While active and a recipe matches, progress rises by one per tick and the craft
  completes at **100**.
- On completion the recipe's remainder for each ring slot is written back into
  that station, the result goes to the output slot, the altar's own remainder
  goes back to the input slot, and progress and the active latch are cleared.
- Removing the input at any point resets progress and the latch.
- The eight-station ritual additionally requires its reservoirs to cover the
  declared material stacks before it will advance at all.

The ring offsets are two fixed sets of eight positions at the altar's own height,
one set at distance 3 on the axes and 2 on the diagonals, the other the same set
in a different declaration order (**read**). The order is observable, because the
ring stations are matched to recipe slots by position in that list.

**Host-blocked**: the ring scan reads neighbouring block entities, which Ravel
does not publish. A counterpart implements the progress rule, the latch, the
remainder write-back and the coverage test over a supplied ring.

### 12.5 The harvesting machine

Sixteen slots, the first being fuel and the rest output, insertion refused
everywhere but the fuel slot and extraction refused from it while it holds
burnable fuel (**read**).

Its sweep is a cursor over a square in front of it:

- Range is `1 + added range`, so 1 with no upgrade and 7 with the sixth; the
  square is `(2 × range + 1)` on a side and its centre is `range + 1` blocks in
  the facing direction, at the machine's own height.
- The cursor is a single integer that increments each time progress passes the
  operation time and wraps at the square's area. It decomposes as
  `x = index mod side − range`, `z = index div side − range`, then is mapped
  into world axes by the facing.
- Landing on a mature crop whose seed resolves: harvest per §8.4, charge the full
  usage. Landing on an immature crop, or on one whose seed cannot be resolved:
  charge the **scan** cost. Landing on anything that is not a crop at all:
  charge **nothing**, and the step is free.
- A redstone signal disables the machine entirely: it neither advances nor scans.
  This is the shared gate of §12.2, not a rule of this machine's own.
- Harvested items fill output slots from the second slot onward, and the order is
  the reverse of the obvious one: at each slot the **empty** test is applied
  first, so the first empty slot wins over a matching stack that sits later.
  Anything that still does not fit is dropped on the ground at the harvested
  position.
- **A shipped duplication follows from that order** (**read**): a stack that
  partially merges into one or more matching slots and then reaches an empty slot
  is written into that slot at its **original full count**, not at what is left,
  so the merged portion is created twice. A counterpart tracks the remainder into
  the empty slot and records the divergence, since reproducing it would make the
  machine an item duplicator.

The cursor being persisted (§12.3) plus the sweep being a wrapping scan rather
than a spatial search is what makes throughput proportional to crop density and
independent of where the crops are — a counterpart must reproduce the wrapping
cursor, not "find the nearest mature crop".

### 12.6 The creature-summoning machine

Two slots, an input capped at 512 and a fuel slot (**read**). Its recipe is
re-resolved on load, on receiving a block update and on any inventory change, so
its display is correct without a tick.

On completion: draw a creature kind from the recipe's weighted list; refuse if
**32 or more** living entities of that class are already within a cube of side
`2 × 3` centred on the machine; pick a position by a uniform draw over a
`(2 × 3 + 1)` square at the machine's own height; try up to **20** positions
until one is unobstructed; on success add the creature and consume the recipe's
declared input count; on failure consume nothing and reset progress.

Two exact facts: the position draw is over `[0, side²]` and appears to be
**inclusive** of the upper bound, so the last index would be drawn out of range
and fold to a position one row beyond the square (**inferred** — the draw calls a
helper in the shared library, outside this entry's source, and this document does
not read it); and the attempt counter is decremented before
the loop test, so an entity that is unobstructed on the twentieth try is still
rejected (**read**). Both are almost certainly unintended and both are shipped
behaviour. A counterpart draws over a half-open range and accepts a success on
the final attempt, and records the divergence.

### 12.7 The reservoir

One slot with a limit of **40** (**read**), and an insertion predicate that
accepts only items appearing as a declared material stack in some eight-station
ritual recipe — read from the cache of §13, which means the predicate is
**empty until the first data load** and, on a client, empty until the sync
payload arrives. Right-clicking with an item inserts what fits; right-clicking
with nothing, or with an item that does not fit, ejects the contents at the
player's own position with no pickup delay.

The block reports a comparator signal derived from its inventory, and drops its
contents when broken.

### 12.8 The fitting bench and slotted modifiers

An item may carry slotted modifiers, stored as a list of (identifier, slot)
pairs in item-level data (§13). The bench is a menu with a target slot, two
modifier slots and four material slots, and it applies modifiers **as a side
effect of the menu's contents changing**, not as a craft: whenever the contents
change, for each of the two modifier slots, the modifier in the slot is written
onto the target if it differs from what is there, and removed if the slot is
empty (**read**).

Acceptance rules (**read**): a modifier may be applied only if it shares at least
one declared kind with the target and its own tier is at most the target's; a
slot index at or beyond the target's slot count is ignored; removing a modifier
also clears the target's area-offset value (§13).

**Host-blocked**: menus and slots are host surfaces; the four material slots'
accept predicates name four specific crops' materials by identity. A counterpart
implements the acceptance rules and the add/remove semantics as portable logic
over a declared modifier set.

## 13. Item-level data, the cache, and networking

**Five item-level data kinds**, all persisted with the item and all synchronised
to clients (**read**):

- a boolean: the wetting tool is running;
- a list of (identifier, slot) pairs: the slotted modifiers on a tool or armour
  piece;
- an integer: stored experience, capped at **1200**;
- a record of (charge-type identifier, amount as a floating-point number): the
  container's contents;
- a record of two integers: an area offset, horizontal and vertical.

Four of these are also published to extensions a second time under the same
identifiers, so an extension can name them without depending on this entry's
internals (**read**).

**Four network payloads**, on a channel declared at version `"1"` (**read**):

- server to client: a unit payload that plays the experience-pickup sound;
- server to client: the ingredient cache (below);
- server to client: a map from item identifier to a colour, loaded from data;
- client to server: a nudge to the held tool's area offset, applied as a clamp of
  the current offset plus the nudge to plus or minus the tool's largest
  area-modifier range.

**The ingredient cache** is the one with real consequences (**read**). On the
server, when tags are updated *and only for a server data load*, the entry
rebuilds a table from item to the list of ingredients that accept it, for four
recipe kinds, plus the set of items that appear as a declared material in any
eight-station ritual. Both are then pushed to a joining player, or to every
player on a reload.

Everything that answers "may this item go into this machine from this side"
consults that cache (§12.7, and the sided insertion predicates of three
machines). The consequences are exact and worth stating:

- On a client the cache is **only** what the payload delivered; a client that
  never received one accepts nothing.
- The rebuild is skipped for a client-side tag update, so a single-player world
  relies on the same payload path.
- Because the cache is keyed by concrete item, an ingredient that matches by a
  predicate rather than by item set is reachable only through the items it
  enumerates.

**The colour map** is loaded from every data pack under one fixed path, parsed as
identifier-to-hexadecimal pairs, cleared and rebuilt on each reload, and consulted
with a fallback to white (**read**). Four entries ship.

**Host-blocked**: Ravel publishes payload declaration, so the four payloads
themselves are expressible; the datapack-sync and tags-updated signals that drive
them are not. A counterpart declares the payloads, implements the cache as a
portable index over declared recipes, and records the trigger points as blocked.

## 14. World generation

Three deposits, each a configured feature, a placement, and a biome modifier
gated on one of the three settings of §3 (**read**). This is what
`worldgen-parity` observes, and the parameters are the whole contract.

| deposit | feature | size | targets | count per chunk | height | biomes | generation step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| first ore | host ore feature | 8 | stone-replaceable and deepslate-replaceable, two block states | 16 | uniform, absolute −32 to 64 | overworld | underground ores |
| second ore | host ore feature | 8 | stone-replaceable and deepslate-replaceable, two block states | 12 | uniform, absolute −60 to 24 | overworld | underground ores |
| nether stone | **this entry's own feature** | 64 | one block-match target | 4 | uniform, absolute 0 to 128 | nether | **raw generation** |

All three use the host's square-spread and biome-filter placements, and all three
declare an air-exposure discard chance of zero (**read**).

Two things about the third deposit are load-bearing:

- **It runs at the raw-generation step, not the ore step.** That is the earliest
  decoration step, before ores, so the stone it places is present as a target for
  anything generating later. Every other deposit here runs at the ore step. A
  counterpart that files all three under one step changes what generates on top
  of what.
- **It substitutes a rarer ore inside the cluster.** The feature reproduces the
  host's blob-placement algorithm — the same ellipsoid sweep, the same visited
  set, the same height gate against the ocean-floor heightmap — with one change:
  at each position that would receive the target state, the substitution happens
  only if the rarer ore's registry entry is bound **and** a draw against the
  configured chance (default **0.05**) succeeds; otherwise the target state is
  placed.
  The draw is per block, not per cluster, and it is taken from the feature's own
  supplied random source, so it is reproducible from the world seed.

The biome modifiers each read their configuration boolean at the moment biomes
are modified, so turning a deposit off affects chunks generated afterwards and
leaves existing chunks alone (**inferred** — the modifier is consulted during
biome construction and nothing rewrites generated chunks).

**Host-blocked**: Ravel publishes no world-feature or biome-modifier declaration.
A counterpart implements the substitution rule and the placement parameters as
declared data plus one portable predicate, and records the registration as
blocked.

## 15. Absent-mod crops, and the two shapes of "disabled"

Worth its own section because it is the single largest source of behavioural
difference between two installations of the same pack (**read**).

About ninety of the catalogue's crops name a material belonging to another mod.
Each is registered unconditionally but is marked **disabled** when none of its
declared companion mods is loaded. In a development environment every crop is
enabled regardless.

A disabled crop:

- **still registers** its block, its material item and its seed item, so its
  identifiers exist in the registry and a world that contains them loads;
- **contributes no dynamic recipes** (§11 tests the enabled flag first);
- **is excluded from the shipped data recipes** by the condition of §10.5;
- **is hidden from the creative listing**.

So "disabled" means *unobtainable*, not *absent*. That distinction is what makes
`registry-parity` and `recipe-parity` measure different things for this entry: the
registry is the same in every installation, and the recipe set is not.

Separately, the crafting material itself may resolve empty — a tag with no
members, or an item that is not registered — and that is a **third** state: the
crop is enabled, its identifiers exist, its seed-conversion recipe exists, and
only its seed recipes are missing. A counterpart keeps the three states apart.

## 17. What this specification does not specify

- **The catalogue.** About a hundred and forty crops, sixty-six blocks, the full
  item list, six armour materials, seven tool materials, twenty-five slotted
  modifiers and about thirty creature charge types were **read as existing** and
  are not transcribed. They are data, every one of their names is an upstream
  identifier, and §4 plus §5 is the machinery a counterpart needs to carry
  equivalent data of its own choosing. The one thing carried across from the
  catalogue is structural: the tier values, the multiplier tables of §12.1 and the
  placement parameters of §14, because dependents observe those as arithmetic.
- **The twenty-five slotted modifiers individually.** Their common shape is §12.8
  and §13; each one's effect — an attribute change, a periodic effect, an
  area-of-effect behaviour on use — is read as existing and not specified. The one
  piece of shared arithmetic that is specified is the area-offset clamp of §13.
- **The client presentation set.** Seven menu screens, eight block-entity
  renderers, the model and colour handlers, the overlay and the tooltip handler
  were read for their gating only. Layout, key choices and translation text are
  presentation. The one substantive client rule stated is that the ingredient
  cache and colour map exist on a client only through the payloads of §13.
- **The three third-party compatibility layers** (a recipe-viewer integration, a
  block-inspection integration and a scripting integration), read as existing.
  They add no behaviour to the entry; they expose existing behaviour to another
  mod, and each names that mod, so nothing about them is transcribed.
- **The data generators and the guide book**, read as existing only.
- **The translation strings and the shipped translations**, read as existing;
  their text is upstream material and is not transcribed. The entry's textures,
  models and sounds were fetched with the source, were not read as behaviour, and
  nothing is shipped from them.
- **The experience-container arithmetic**, read in part. Its cap of 1200 and its
  add and remove rules are in §13; the level-and-progress conversion it performs
  on the player is a reimplementation of a host routine and is not specified.
- **Whether the eight-station ritual's document-encoding defect (§10.1) is
  reachable in a running game**, marked **unknown**. It matters only for tools
  that round-trip recipes through their document form.
- **Whether the energy amount can exceed a reduced capacity in practice**
  (§12.2), marked **unknown**: the capacity setter does not clamp, but no shipped
  path reduces capacity while the store is above the new value except removing an
  upgrade, and that case was not traced further.
- **Whether the charge-container ordering used when distributing charge across a
  player's inventory can throw**, marked **unknown**. The comparator used there is
  not a consistent ordering — it answers on a nullness test of whichever operand
  it is handed — and a host sort may reject such a comparator on a long enough
  list. It was not reproduced. A counterpart uses a total order (already-typed
  containers first, then by slot index) and records the divergence.
- **Coverage.** Read in full: the configuration document; the three registries
  and every ordering and refusal rule in §4; the crop, tier and crop-kind model
  and all its defaults; the four growth gates and all four growth paths; both drop
  rules and the second-drop arithmetic; every ground rule in §9; all eight recipe kinds and both
  serialisation directions of the six that have one; all three ingredient kinds
  and all four conditions; the dynamic recipe derivation; the upgrade table and
  its acceptance rule; the fuel and energy model and every machine's persisted
  fields; both rituals; the harvesting sweep; the summoning attempt; the reservoir
  predicate; the fitting rules; all five item-level data kinds and all four
  payloads; the ingredient cache and the colour map; and all three world deposits
  including the custom feature. Read in part: the tool and armour classes, for
  their modifier dispatch and their tier and durability rules but not their
  per-tier statistics; the client handlers, for gating only. Read as existing
  only: the catalogue, the modifiers' individual effects, the screens and
  renderers, the compatibility layers, the data generators, the translations and
  the binary assets. Not read: the build configuration beyond the version
  declaration, and the shipped recipe files beyond a type census and a sample of
  each kind.
